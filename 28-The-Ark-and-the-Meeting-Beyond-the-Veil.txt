Chapter 1
The Page That Opened East
The next leaf in Daniel's family book had been folded the wrong way for so long that it would not lie flat.
He put a clean cup on one corner and a photograph on the other. It was still October 2026. The ceiling had been repaired, his daughter was supposed to be finishing her homework, and the kitchen smelled of toast.
Mia leaned across his shoulder.
“That's another boat.”
“I think it's the same one.”
The drawing showed the wooden hull on one side of the page. Beside it, without any attempt to make them the same size, stood a small chest and a long rectangular court. The chest had carrying poles. A colored gate opened at the eastern end of the court.
Underneath, the copyist had written three headings: what the text says, what we measured, what happened afterward.
Daniel smiled. His grandfather had used almost those words whenever someone at the dinner table became too certain about a memory.
The hand emblem appeared in the margin. He rested his forearm beside it while he read the first note.
Do not stretch the little Ark until it becomes the ship. You will lose the place where the people are waiting.
Mia pointed at the court.
“Is that a garden?”
“A tabernacle. A place of worship.”
“Why is it in our book?”
He read further. Someone had copied references from Genesis and Exodus. The large vessel measured three hundred by fifty by thirty cubits. The Covenant Ark measured two and a half by one and a half by one and a half. A note beside the first used an eighteen-inch cubit for the story's familiar hull: four hundred and fifty by seventy-five by forty-five feet.
The writer had underlined the word assumption beside that conversion. The cubit itself was an old body measure, not a promise that every ancient builder used Daniel's ruler.
“They're comparing them,” he said.
Mia turned her exercise book sideways and drew two rectangles of very different shapes.
“They don't match.”
“No.”
“Then why compare them?”
Daniel looked at the three headings again.
The next line answered her more directly than he could.
Because a resemblance can tell you where to look. A difference can stop you opening the wrong door.
He checked the clock. There was time for a few pages before supper. He had thought that before.
In the story, Lazara was standing in the Ark's harbor hall with one hand over her mouth, listening to a person everyone had entered in the records as dead.
Daniel sat down.
Mia pulled out the chair beside him. Her homework lay between them, two unequal rectangles waiting beneath a question she had not yet written.
Chapter 2
A Voice Without a Grave
The voice came through Harbor South after the basin had been emptied for inspection.
Lazara stood inside the thirty-six-by-sixty-foot receiving hall. Beyond the low windows lay the separate harbor, eighteen hundred feet long and nine hundred across, its stone bottom shining with a shallow skin of water. The twelve-by-ten-foot door behind her opened onto S20-C of the carrier.
She knew those dimensions now. They were how she knew that the voice was coming from a place she could not see, rather than from a person hiding behind the nearest pier.
“If somebody hears this, please do not close the eastern way.”
Sella dropped the bundle in her arms.
She had come north with the returning Ark to help settle the Atlantis records. Iven had stayed at the new bay settlement with Noma and the other garden families. Sella had spoken cheerfully about being useful. Now her face had emptied.
“Dera?”
The basin gave back the name in a thin echo.
“Dera!”
Lazara caught her before she reached the wet stair.
“Wait. Tell me.”
“My sister.”
Elias came through the carrier door carrying a wooden sounding rod. He stopped when he saw them.
“She was on a survey party,” Sella said. “Four years before the gardens failed. They went into the displaced district beyond the old map station.”
Lazara knew the rest without wanting to. People had watched the way close. A report had been written. A name had moved from the list of the missing to the easier list of the dead.
The voice returned.
“There are children here. The shouting is us.”
Elias set down the rod very carefully.
Across the basin, something pale moved beneath the water film. For an instant it resembled a face. Then it became a street drawn at an impossible angle, and vanished.
Aven ordered the harbor door held open but barred against crossing. A voice did not give them a safe landing. Nor did a woman's certainty give them permission to lose her.
Sella pressed both hands against the timber rail.
“They called that place the wicked quarter. They said the cries were what remained of people who shouldn't be answered.”
Lazara felt anger rise, hot enough to make her careless if she let it choose what came next.
“Who said?”
“People who were afraid to go back.”
From the basin came another sound: a small cough, followed by someone asking for water.
This time everyone in the hall heard it.
Ves arrived with a blank register. She wrote down the exact words before interpretation could take them away.
On the first line she entered: An unidentified group is asking for help.
Below it she left space for their names.
Chapter 3
The Ship They Actually Had

The six deck carrier and its separately linked garden

Before Aven allowed a search, she made them walk the Ark again.
Not because Lazara had forgotten it. Because fear had made the destination seem close enough to run toward, and their ship still existed at measured distances.
They began on the northern quay beneath the pyramid. Snow had found its way through a damaged upper vent and melted beside a repaired stone. Far below the broken cap, the old parent building continued through miles of understructure. It could shelter an outpost. It could not promise that every old route remained sound.
At H05, seventy-five feet from the wooden hull's stern, they passed through the side door. Twelve feet wide, ten high. The receiving vestibule beyond it measured twenty-four by eighteen. A red-brown mat ended at the honey-resin reveal of the inner seam.
Lazara stopped Sella there.
“If we return frightened, this is the place we name. Not just the Ark. This vestibule.”
Sella nodded, though her eyes stayed on the pale landing beyond.
They climbed the separate hundred-and-twenty-foot folded approach, rising seven feet six inches to the carrier's stern vestibule. The outer ship remained four hundred and fifty by seventy-five by forty-five feet. Its three physical stories did not grow when the passage opened.
Inside, the linked carrier occupied seven hundred and eighty feet by one hundred and forty-four by one hundred and forty-four. Thirty-foot vestibules stood at either end of seven hundred and twenty feet of numbered stations.
Twenty-four stations. Thirty feet from frame to frame. The parent tower's twenty-four floors preserved as an order laid lengthwise.
At S01-C, Aven made Elias read the deck directory aloud.
Keel, zero. Foundry, twelve feet. Commons, twenty-four. Homes, thirty-six. Learning, forty-eight. Gardens, sixty. Above the garden floor lay the great volume of light, framing, and service bridges, not another seven floors somebody could offer to refugees.
Sella listened impatiently until they reached the assisted lift at S08. There she pictured her sister trying to climb with an injured person.
“We'll need this working.”
“Yes,” Aven said.
At S21-L they entered the route registry, a room twenty-four feet by eighteen. Its twelve panels named source, destination, local time, and return. Two operators checked every identity against paper.
The eastern harbor signal had no verified destination.
“We will find one,” Sella said.
“We will look,” Aven answered. “Until then, these people need us to know the difference.”
Lazara put the ship plan beside Ves's blank register. She drew the entire return route in charcoal, including the ordinary hull's twelve-foot aisle and four-foot stair doors.
The drawing did not bring Dera closer.
It made a place for her to arrive.
Chapter 4
Two Arks on One Table
The Bible lay open in the S12-C common table hall, between a load book and a plate of bread.
Ves had brought several old translations. The family copy used the familiar King James wording, and she kept that open while checking the measurements against the older-language notes.
Elias drew Noah's Ark on one sheet. On another he drew the Ark of the Covenant.
“Same name,” Sella said.
“In this translation,” Ves replied. “Not the same Hebrew noun. Tebah for Noah's vessel and the basket that carried the infant Moses. Aron for the Covenant chest.”
Sella looked toward the quiet harbor passage.
“A child in a little ark.”
“That is a closer word connection. We shouldn't lose it by making every ark into one machine.”
Elias wrote the dimensions beneath his drawings. Three hundred, fifty, thirty cubits for Noah. Two and a half, one and a half, one and a half for the Covenant Ark.
He put his finger beside the two lengths.
“Six to one for the ship's length against its width. Five to three for the chest. Scaling won't turn one into the other.”
Lazara had expected the failure to disappoint them. Instead it cleared the table of an attractive mistake.
They read the coverings next. Noah's vessel was pitched inside and outside. The Covenant Ark was made of shittim wood and overlaid with gold inside and outside. Its poles were also wood covered with gold. There were rings, a mercy seat, two cherubim, and testimony placed within.
“Gold,” Aven said from the doorway.
She had been waiting for someone to notice the problem.
“Not aboard our hull.”
Elias nodded. The wooden ship's rule remained absolute. Whatever awaited them, they would not quietly replace its isolation with a theory that sacred metal could not answer a magnetic storm.
“So the covering matters differently,” Lazara said. “Pitch protects a floating refuge from water. Gold belongs to a sacred chest. We can't use one reason for both.”
Ves turned to the sanctuary instructions.
Measured construction. A pattern given rather than improvised. Work that had to be finished as commanded. Covenant in the Flood account. Covenant testimony in the chest. Presence promised among a people.
“These are the connections,” she said. “They are large enough without pretending the dimensions match.”
Sella broke a piece of bread and did not eat it.
“How do they find my sister?”
Elias moved the tabernacle drawing between the two arks.
“Perhaps we've been looking for the shape of a container,” he said, “when the record is describing the order of an approach.”
Outside the table hall, a cart passed along the twelve-foot Common Spine. Its driver slowed for two children, waited, and went on.
Lazara watched the doorway after it was empty.
“Then we start with where people enter.”
Chapter 5
A Court with an Eastern Gate
They laid the court out with string on the Commons training floor.
There was no room to reproduce its full length there without blocking the route to the lifts. Elias used a scale drawing for the whole plan and a full-size section for the entrance. He wrote SCALE on the smaller board in letters large enough for an impatient councilor.
Ves read from Exodus. The court extended one hundred cubits by fifty. The gate stood on the east, twenty cubits wide. The hangings were five cubits high.
The inner sanctuary required a different drawing. Twenty boards along each long side, each a cubit and a half across, gave thirty cubits in length. The familiar reconstruction was thirty by ten by ten, but Ves marked the width and inner divisions as reconstruction from the instructions, not a single verse listing a finished building's dimensions.
“If a corner is uncertain,” she said, “we don't solve it by speaking more loudly.”
Lazara held the entrance string while Sella moved a wooden marker through it.
Gate. Altar. Basin. Tent entrance. Holy Place. Veil. Most Holy Place.
The Ark belonged beyond the veil. The table stood north in the Holy Place, the lampstand south. The incense altar belonged before the veil. The washing basin stood outside, between the altar of burnt offering and the tent.
Sella moved the marker again, slower.
At the basin she stopped.
“That's the shape in Dera's survey book.”
She ran to the archive cupboard and returned with a notebook held together by a broad cloth tie. A sketch showed a route through an eastern opening, a square obstacle, a round mark, and two rooms divided by a line.
The old survey office had labeled it a pump station.
Ves placed it beside the court plan.
Nobody spoke for several moments.
“Not proof,” she said eventually. “But enough to search the next page.”
Behind the sketch lay a copied phrase from an old route register: meeting approach, traveler side.
The letters ran diagonally where the page had been damaged. One word had been expanded into a frightening warning by a later clerk. Ves checked the older characters and crossed out the expansion.
“It doesn't say condemned.”
Sella stared at her.
“What does it say?”
“Displaced.”
The string in Lazara's hand had begun to cut into her skin. She loosened it.
On the floor, the little marker waited at a round point between a square and a doorway.
Their first useful clue was a place where someone had mistaken geography for judgment.
Chapter 6
The Garden with Its Own Address
Noma would have approved of the argument about the garden annex.
The carrier's upper garden deck and the separate annex were not the same place. A supply clerk had written both as Gardens on a transfer sheet. Aven sent the sheet back before one crate moved.
Lazara accompanied the corrected delivery through the annex threshold. A small door opened onto twelve hundred feet by six hundred of growing space, registered separately from the carrier. Rows of climbing beans disappeared beneath a soft artificial dawn. Water moved in channels that knew which floor they belonged to.
The annex had not drowned when its door closed during the Atlantis voyage. Its bounded local state had held. Reopening access at the northern hub had required a destination check, reservoir inspection, and an argument with a pump valve that remembered better days.
Now it could feed a rescue expedition. It could not feed an unknown number forever without a harvest schedule.
Sella knelt beside a bed of salt roots brought from Atlantis.
“Dera used to steal these raw.”
“Iven does that,” Lazara said.
“I know. I kept telling him it was his father's side.”
For the first time since the voice, she smiled.
Beyond the annex's observation wall, the map chamber displayed the garden plates. Intact countries moved through a depth full of stars, each with an Eden whose water and seasons remained in agreement. Their own plate showed a damaged rim and a wandering dawn.
A fracture crossed its map. The survey party's last route lay along it, drawn sideways to the direction in which a person should have been able to walk.
“They didn't fall down,” Elias said. “They went across an address that turned.”
He moved a transparent overlay. A road lined up with one of the old harbor signals. Turn it again, and the same road appeared to pass through a wall.
The cries could enter one plate while the people stayed on another. A door could open onto a remembered room without its floor agreeing to hold a returning foot.
The explanation did not make the voices less frightening. It made them belong to people.
Sella watched a small dark place between two perfect gardens.
“If those work, why don't they hear?”
“Their maps may not include this broken edge,” Ves said.
“Then put it on ours.”
They did.
Lazara wrote Displaced survey party beside the fracture. She did not write ghosts. She did not write the dead.
Under the heading for assistance needed, she left room for what they had yet to learn.
Chapter 7
The Complete Mixture
The formula was on the back of a water-stained maintenance card.
It had survived because someone had used it as a bookmark in a history of the tabernacle's coverings. Sella found it while searching for her sister's copied route.
One gallon of water. Two cups of milk of magnesia. Two cups of salt.
There was nothing underneath except a reference to placement and the words keeper's suspension.
Elias read both sides three times.
“No missing ingredient.”
“You sound disappointed,” Lazara said.
“I'm disappointed in whoever hid the placement sheet.”
The card called its old housing an EMP strikebox. Ves put the phrase in a column headed old equipment terminology. It was not a name used in Exodus. Nor was the mixture among the tabernacle's biblical furnishings, oils, or incense.
“Then why is it in this book?” Sella asked.
“Because somebody later used the pattern to position a traveler-side device,” Ves said. “We have to find their work without turning it into Moses's instruction.”
At the northern service workshop, outside the wooden Ark, they found a surviving ceramic vessel inside a battered case. Its cloudy contents had separated. A reflective foil lining remained around the case's inner wall. No ingredient had vanished. No secret mineral waited to be added.
The vessel had room above the liquid. Lazara checked that because the card began with a gallon of water and then added two more measures. A gallon-sized container would not have been large enough for the whole contents.
On the bench beside it lay a broken placement bracket and a map grid whose eastern edge had been torn away.
Elias returned the bracket to the indicated position in the workshop's test receiver.
Nothing happened.
He tried the aligned orientation.
Still nothing.
Sella looked from the silent vessel to the return panel.
“The mixture is complete.”
“Yes.”
“Then stop looking inside it.”
He took his hand away.
Lazara laid the tabernacle plan across the grid. Not because the basin held this liquid in Scripture; it held washing water. Because the old travelers had copied a relationship between an entrance, an outer court, and a protected inner boundary.
The position they needed was not aboard the wooden ship. It stood on the landward side of a meeting approach.
Sella traced the round mark in Dera's notebook.
“She found the place.”
Then she looked toward the dark receiver.
“And we keep asking the bottle to be the map.”
Chapter 8
What the Gold Could Not Do
The northern administrator wanted the Covenant Ark brought home.
His name was Selden. He had not seen the sanctuary, did not know whether it existed, and had already cleared a locked exhibit room.
“Protection,” he said when Aven asked why.
They met in a cold office above the dock. The keeper's suspension remained at the shore workshop. The expedition's wooden tools waited aboard the hull. Selden's polished metal watch rested very far from both.
“If the old chest controls the passage, it belongs somewhere secure.”
Ves closed her Bible.
“The text says testimony is kept within it and a meeting is promised above the mercy seat. It doesn't say the person who possesses it commands the presence.”
“You understood what I meant.”
“I did. That's why I'm answering.”
He turned to Elias.
“Gold inside and out. Surely you see the engineering resemblance.”
Elias looked at the repaired stair visible through the office window. He had once accepted work from people who described human beings as difficulties in a mechanism.
“A resemblance isn't a circuit diagram.”
Selden's mouth tightened.
“Are you refusing to examine it?”
“I'm refusing to tell you what it does before we've found it.”
Lazara put the expedition register on the table. Sella's sister remained its first unresolved name.
“This is the object of the search,” she said. “People.”
Selden studied the page. For a moment she thought he might recognize the simplicity of it.
Instead he asked whether the rescued people could be useful witnesses to the chest's location.
Aven stood.
“The northern hub authorizes the search within its existing rescue agreement. No claim of possession is included.”
He warned that he could withdraw storage and staff.
“Then put that in writing,” she said.
On the quay, Sella was waiting. She had heard enough from their faces.
“Is he going to stop us?”
“Not today,” Lazara said.
Elias looked back toward the office.
“He may try to make us choose between the rescue and bringing something back for him.”
Sella drew the old hand emblem on the corner of her register, exactly as it appeared in her family's records.
“Then he should have looked at the book before asking.”
She carried it through H05, past the receiving bench and the closed seam, and waited for the destination check before taking another step.
Her fear had not become smaller.
It had learned where to stand.
Chapter 9
The Road Turned on Its Side

The displaced wilderness between intact garden plates
BRIAN CHAPMAN SR MSP A+ CIW
They reached the fractured approach through a verified shore gateway three days later.
The wooden Ark traveled inside its carried harbor. Its separate carrier, garden, and basin retained their registered boundaries. At the destination, Aven reopened only the routes whose return identities held. The garden remained provisionally closed until its local service check was complete.
Lazara crossed from the ordinary hull to the carrier with the same physical distances under her feet as before. She could smell bread at S12-C. She could hear Renn arguing with a cart wheel at S04.
Then Harbor South opened onto a cliff whose horizon stood upright.
The sea ran along the left side of the sky. Above the cliff, a road passed through the base of a luminous garden plate and disappeared behind the image of a mountain.
Below that mountain lay the half-covered face of a stone giant. Gravel filled the hollow beneath its cheek. Trees had grown along a shoulder that had once belonged to an ordinary person caught in the rupture.
Lazara remembered Ilyra's request for boots. Recognition made the enormous body more difficult to look at, not easier.
Aven tested the shore landing. It held within a small marked area. Beyond it, pebbles sometimes fell sideways.
They fixed no metal anchor to the wooden ship. The shore station's old equipment remained on stone, separated from the hull by the checked threshold. The foil-lined housing traveled as shore cargo through an independent service transfer, never aboard the Ark.
Elias inspected its ceramic vessel after arrival. The contents were unchanged.
Ves aligned the local map with Dera's survey sketch. The eastern road lay beyond a fold they could see but not yet enter.
At sunset, a voice came out of the empty cliff.
It was singing badly.
Sella took one step forward and stopped herself.
“That's the wrong verse,” she said.
The singing ceased.
Lazara waited beside her.
From the stone came a very faint reply.
“You always said that.”
Sella made a sound Lazara had never heard from her before, grief caught halfway into relief.
“Dera?”
“If it's you, don't follow the white road. It puts you back where you started.”
The voice broke into static, then returned.
“There is a tent. We can see it. We can't reach the entrance.”
Ves looked toward the upright horizon.
The sanctuary had become more than a sketch.
Chapter 10
A Promise Has an Address
The first message clear enough to question lasted forty-three seconds.
Ves timed it with a sandglass while Mera wrote. Lazara kept Sella beside the rail. Elias watched the landward receiver and did not touch it when the voice strengthened.
“How many with you?” Lazara asked.
“Twenty-seven. Two need carrying. There are three children.”
“Are you all from the survey?”
“No. Eleven of us were. The rest came from roads that opened and closed.”
The time difference entered more painfully than the count. Four years had passed for Sella. Dera believed she had been trapped for thirty-six days.
Neither woman could speak for a moment.
Mera wrote both measures. She did not correct one into the other.
“We are here,” Sella said at last. “Iven is safe. He thinks the surface sky is too large.”
“How old is he?”
Before Sella could answer, the channel failed.
She stood holding the unfinished sentence.
Elias turned away from the receiver. His first instinct had been to adjust it, to pull one more answer out of a system that was already losing agreement. He forced his hand to stay at his side.
“We need a return identity they can recognize.”
Lazara opened Dera's map.
“The tent entrance faces east. She's standing on a road that has turned. We keep saying east as if we share a sunrise.”
Ves found the older phrase in the tabernacle notes: before the door. It named a relationship, not a compass bearing on every broken piece of land.
They marked the sequence again. Court gate, outer altar, basin, tent, veil, inner chest. Some parts of that ordered approach could be seen through the fold. Others were hidden behind displaced ground.
The missing placement sheet belonged to the traveler-side service box, outside the sacred court. Its grid took its direction from the sanctuary, but its purpose was to hold a map connection between people on different sides.
“A meeting approach,” Ves said.
Sella looked at the closed return panel.
“So we tell her where we're standing in relation to the door.”
At the next signal they did.
Dera was quiet while she checked it.
Then she answered, “I know that place.”
The light above the receiver held for six seconds longer than before.
Nobody cheered. Mera read the location back. Dera corrected a stair count. Elias entered the corrected destination without pretending it had become safe merely because it had become clearer.
They had not opened a passage.
They had agreed on where a promise would have to arrive.
Chapter 11
The Vessel in the Wrong Place
They tested the suspension at the shore receiver without sending anyone through.
The cloudy liquid occupied its sealed ceramic vessel. The foil lining stayed inside the landward housing, well away from the wooden hull. Every ingredient matched the complete formula on the card.
Elias set the vessel on the bracket whose position had survived on the grid.
For an instant the cliff became a room.
A child stood beside a chair, holding a cup in both hands. Behind him a woman raised her face toward the sudden light.
Then the room turned. The floor climbed the wall. The child vanished before Lazara could move.
Aven closed the test.
Sella was already shaking her head.
“Do it again.”
“We have to know why it turned.”
“She was there.”
“And the floor wasn't where her feet were.”
Sella stepped away from Elias as if he had struck her.
He let her.
The next signal brought Dera's voice, angry with fright. Someone on her side had fallen when the doorway appeared. He was conscious. His wrist was hurt. They would not approach another opening unless the people operating it explained what they were doing.
Lazara pressed her palms against the rail.
“We agree.”
She told Dera the test had been closed. No traveler would be sent. The next change would be announced before they tried it.
When the signal ended, she turned to Sella.
“She gets to say no.”
Sella looked toward the blank cliff. Her anger had nowhere easy to go.
“I've waited four years.”
“She's been waiting too.”
They sat on the cold service step until Sella could stop trembling.
Inside the workshop, Elias laid the failed image over the court plan. The bracket had preserved its distance from a boundary but lost which side of that boundary it belonged to.
“Within and without,” Ves said, looking at the two covering passages.
In Genesis, pitch belonged on both surfaces of Noah's vessel. In Exodus, gold belonged inside and outside the Covenant Ark. The tabernacle itself arranged coverings, screens, and rooms in a particular order. A copied margin mark had been mistaken for permission to reverse the traveler station.
They had placed the housing on the coordinate side corresponding to the sanctuary interior. Its actual location belonged outside the court, aligned with the washing approach.
The liquid was complete. The map was reversed.
Elias wrote the error in the test book, including his own name.
Then he went to Sella.
“You should hear this from me before we try anything else.”
She listened with her arms folded.
When he finished, she asked him to explain it again, slowly enough that she could tell Dera herself.
Chapter 12
What the Coverings Preserved
Ves asked for cloth instead of another mechanism.
At S22-L, the learning workshop had a long table, excellent north light, and a cupboard of materials nobody had yet decided were too ordinary to matter. Lazara carried down linen, coarse woven wool, and two empty wooden frames.
They were making a teaching model, not a consecrated sanctuary. Ves wrote that on its card and told the apprentices why.
“If we leave this for someone else to find, they should know what they are looking at.”
She showed the inner linen arrangement first: ten curtains, each twenty-eight cubits by four, coupled in two groups. Then eleven goats' hair curtains, each thirty by four. Over those, the KJV account named red-dyed rams' skins and the outer covering it called badgers' skins.
The exact animal represented by that old translated skin name remained disputed in the reference notes. Ves kept the KJV wording on the source column without pretending the zoology was settled.
Renn touched the coarse cloth.
“The beautiful work goes inside.”
“Much of it,” Ves said.
They laid the layers over the little frame. Outside, the model became plain. Inside, the blue, purple, and scarlet remained visible above a person's imagined approach.
Elias held the reversed service grid beneath it.
He had been treating every covering as if it performed the same electrical function. Here were layers that joined, protected, enclosed, marked a boundary, and shaped an interior. The word covering had concealed several different jobs.
Lazara watched him move the grid to the outside edge.
“So the foil belongs to its own case,” she said. “It doesn't replace a veil.”
“And neither case nor liquid belongs on the mercy seat.”
He looked toward Ves.
“I was trying to make the comparison prove too much.”
She nodded, pleased enough not to soften it.
At the edge of the table, Sella had been folding a strip of blue cloth. She tucked it into the record book as a marker.
“Dera and I shared a room with a curtain between our beds. When she was angry, she used to pull it so hard the whole rail fell.”
She smiled briefly.
“I keep remembering a dead person. She's probably furious that I've imagined her being patient all these years.”
Lazara put the cloth cupboard back in order while they talked. The ordinary act helped.
Before leaving, Sella took one small piece of blue thread. She did not call it sacred or lucky. She wanted something her sister might use to mend a torn sleeve.
Chapter 13
The Little Ark Among the Reeds
At supper, an apprentice asked why Moses's basket mattered to a rescue involving the Covenant Ark.
Ves opened Exodus at the beginning.
The child had been placed in a little ark of bulrushes, coated with slime and pitch, and set among the flags by the river. His sister watched to see what would happen. Pharaoh's daughter found him, recognized him as one of the Hebrew children, and had compassion on him.
The story was familiar enough that people almost stopped hearing it.
Sella did not.
“His sister stayed where she could see.”
“Yes,” Ves said.
“And when someone needed to speak, she spoke.”
The common table hall became quiet around them.
Lazara looked at the large ship drawing, the little basket sketch, and the Covenant chest. The same English word invited a single explanation. The actual stories asked for several kinds of care.
Noah gathered provisions and living creatures into a measured refuge. The infant Moses crossed danger in a small protected container while people outside it watched and acted. The Covenant Ark belonged within a sanctuary and among a people moving through the wilderness.
None could be reduced to a box that did everything by itself.
Elias asked the apprentice to fetch their return plan.
“Who is watching the people on the far side while we work?”
They had operators watching panels, a lookout watching the cliff, and two people checking the vessel's housing. Nobody had been assigned the sole task of listening to what Dera's group said they needed.
Sella took that place.
She would not make technical adjustments. She would keep the speaking record, repeat warnings, and make sure an urgent human sentence did not disappear beneath a more interesting measurement.
At the next contact, Dera said they were nearly out of clean cloth. A child had developed a fever. The injured man's wrist had swollen.
Sella wrote every word.
Then Dera asked how old Iven was.
“Seven.”
The silence on the other side lasted so long Sella thought the connection had gone.
“He was three,” Dera said.
“I know.”
“Did he forget me?”
Sella pressed her knuckles to her mouth.
“He knows the stories I tell. He stole salt roots last week.”
A breath of laughter came through the receiver.
Then Dera began crying, and Sella stayed with her while the sandglass emptied.
No one interrupted to ask whether the sorrow was useful to the search.
Chapter 14
A Stranger's Weather
The first thing they sent through the corrected test was a leaf.
Lazara had wanted cloth. Aven chose something light enough that they would see which direction it fell.
The leaf crossed the opening, turned once, and settled on a patch of pale ground. Its shadow stayed beneath it. The distant road remained a road.
They waited through three agreed intervals.
Dera approached from the other side, outside the marked boundary. She looked older than Sella remembered in some ways and younger in others. There was a streak of white at her temple that had not been there thirty-six days ago, or four years ago, whichever side of the meeting they used.
She did not step into the opening.

Chapter 1
The Stone That Had a Kitchen
In September 2026, Tessa Ward lost a spoon inside a mountain.
  She had stopped to eat beside the exposed rock face because the rain had finally lifted and her sandwich had begun to taste of its wrapper. A strip of hillside had slipped during the night. Her job was to measure the movement before the research crew brought their heavier equipment up the track. Nothing in the work order mentioned masonry, and nothing in Tessa's training suggested that a spoon should vanish when dropped against a crack.
  She heard it land somewhere below her feet. Then she heard a second sound: a chair being pushed across a floor.
  Tessa remained perfectly still. Water dripped from the spruce branches behind her. The valley's abandoned weather station ticked in the wind. She was forty miles from the nearest kitchen she knew about. She called her colleague on the radio and asked whether anyone had entered the survey trench from its lower end.
  Nobody had.
  She photographed the crack with a scale beside it, marked its position, and moved back from the unstable ground. Her brother would later say this was the moment she ought to have run. Tessa would point out that running on a fresh landslide was a good way to make the mystery somebody else's search operation.
  Amos Reid arrived with a rope bag and the authority to stop the day's work. He managed the privately held research parcel for a local cooperative whose members had spent years fighting people who treated the valley as unoccupied because they had not bothered to learn who used it. He looked at the new face, then at the spoon-shaped gap in Tessa's lunch kit.
  “You want me to authorize a rescue for cutlery.”
  “I want you to hear something.”
  They waited. Far below, a metal object struck a basin. A woman said something neither could make out. Amos crouched, keeping his weight away from the broken edge. The crack opened between two blocks too regular to explain as the mountain's own jointing. He did not touch them.
  “Someone's been building here,” he said.
  Their first assumption was an unrecorded installation. Their second was a contractor's practical joke. Amos called the parcel office, checked the old leases, and asked for a structural specialist. Tessa placed a recorder on the rock and went back to the radio. By late afternoon, the hillside had become an inconvenient discovery with enough witnesses to prevent an easy denial.
  The specialist reached them after dark. Iona Marsh, the archaeologist who joined the call remotely, instructed everyone to leave objects where they were, document the setting, and establish who had authority over any investigation. Tessa was relieved. She had expected someone to turn her dropped spoon into an invitation to crawl beneath a mountain. Instead, they fenced the approach, checked for movement, and went to the field cabin to make tea.
  On the cabin television, a replay of the year's Winter Games showed athletes crossing a brightly lit stadium. One of the technicians claimed the ceremony's geometry resembled a diagram he had seen online. Amos asked him to find the complete diagram rather than the clip. The technician could not. The conversation moved to a missing fuel receipt, which was the sort of mystery they could solve before bedtime.
  At two in the morning, the rock recorder transmitted a child's voice.
  “Don't let the bread burn, Nell.”
  Tessa woke in her clothes. She replayed the sentence through headphones. The voice was ordinary, impatient, close to the microphone. A door closed. Somebody laughed. Then a low vibration filled the recording, and the device's clock jumped eleven seconds while the cabin clock did not.
  She carried it to Amos. They listened without speaking. In the background, beneath the laughter, a bell rang twice and a woman asked where the clean cups had gone. Tessa had expected a buried machine or a trapped worker. A household was worse. A machine could be switched off. A household meant people believed they had a safe place to put down a cup.
  By morning, the survey had revealed three sloping surfaces descending beneath the debris. They met at angles that made the technicians stop using the word irregularity. The structure was not an entire mountain cut into a pyramid. It was a built stone body, its upper portion concealed by old slope material, its lower courses bedded into the rock. They could establish form before they could establish age.
  A representative from the leaseholding research company arrived in a clean helicopter and asked for the recordings. He called the discovery a refrigeration experiment. Tessa asked why a refrigeration experiment contained a child named Nell. He said she had misheard.
  She sent the files to Iona before answering him.
  The company representative threatened to terminate the survey contract. Amos told him the land agreement did not give him authority to remove evidence or personnel. Their argument took place beside the fenced trench while rain returned to the valley. Tessa kept the radio open so the office could hear both men.
  That afternoon, the specialist inserted a camera through the existing crack without enlarging it. The image showed a stone shaft, a dark ledge, and something pale at the bottom. Tessa's spoon lay beside a glazed tile painted with a blue line. Beyond the ledge was a wall bearing three simple marks: a square, a plumb line, and the outline of a boat.
  Iona asked for measurements. The company representative asked for silence. Amos asked Tessa whether she could confirm that the camera's cable had not been cut.
  She checked it with her own hands.
  Then the kitchen appeared.
  It lasted only a breath. A wooden table, flour on a woman's sleeve, a child reaching toward a cooling loaf. Across the room, daylight entered through a curved window framed by ribs that looked like the inside of a ship. There was no water beyond it. There were trees.
  The picture vanished before anyone could call out.
  Tessa wrote everything down while her memory was still inconveniently clear. At the bottom of the report, she added the question nobody wanted in a formal document: If someone is living in there, why does the wall have no door?
  Seven years later, Elian Voss would read that question beside a photograph of Laura Kest's prison courtyard. By then the spoon had been entered into an evidence inventory, the company had changed its name twice, and a girl called Nell was old enough to help her mother design a house.
  Tessa had never met her.
Chapter 2
A Room for the Person Not Home
Mara Bell put six plates on the table and removed one before anybody noticed.
  She had been doing it since the river camp rescue. Five people were expected for supper: herself, Daniel, June, Elian, and Miriam. The sixth plate belonged to the habit of waiting. Some nights it represented Laura Kest. Some nights it represented everyone whose name remained on Elian's list. Tonight Mara put it in the cupboard because June had asked for one meal during which the missing were allowed to be somewhere other than between them.
  Daniel struggled with the lid of the pickle jar. June reached across, stopped herself, and waited until he asked. His injured arm was stronger than it had been before the compulsory provisions of the recovery act were suspended. Strength did not return politely. It arrived as one more task he could almost manage, followed by pain and a bad temper he disliked more than the pain.
  “You may witness my triumph,” he said when the lid moved.
  June applauded without making it a ceremony. Mara laughed. Elian, who had lost another man named Daniel years earlier in the gateways, felt the familiar double movement of affection and grief. Her brother-in-law's survival did not diminish her husband's death. She had learned that two men could share a name without one becoming an answer to the other's absence.
  Miriam arrived with bread from the independent kitchen. Her locket caught the light as she took off her coat. She had spent the afternoon speaking to Elian's father, her older brother, about ordinary things they had lost decades of practice discussing. He had told her about a leaking shed. She had cried afterward because he had finally believed she might still care about a leaking shed.
  They ate chicken and potatoes. June described a school assignment about local history and the argument that began when one child said everyone displaced through time ought to be removed from the class photograph until their ages were verified. The teacher had corrected him. The photograph was postponed anyway.
  “Do you want me to call?” Mara asked.
  “I want the photograph taken.”
  Daniel put down his fork. “Then that's what we'll ask for.”
  The conversation might have ended there if Elian's phone had not lit beneath her hand. She turned it facedown. Hale sent a second message. Then a third, containing only the words Please open the photograph.
  June saw her hesitate.
  “It isn't a meal without the missing if you sit there pretending not to read it.”
  Elian apologized and opened the file.
  It showed stone beneath melting snow, a scale marker, and a sloping seam. The attached report came from an Alaska survey conducted in 2026. Beneath the modern catalog number was a drawing of a boat with six interior bands. One band held the same arrangement of trees that the unidentified girl had drawn in Laura's shelter.
  Elian enlarged the lower edge. Three tiny marks appeared beside the hull: a square, a hanging weight, and a spoon.
  Miriam leaned forward.
  “The child drew that too.”
  The supper changed. Mara saw it happen in their shoulders before anybody spoke. She wanted to hate the photograph for entering her house. Instead she asked June to bring the spare lamp so they could all see without leaning over Elian's phone.
  Hale joined them by call. He had found the survey in a contractor's archive released during the Orison proceedings. It had been filed under an equipment dispute, not a monument discovery. Its oldest witness, Tessa Ward, was alive. She had refused five offers to sell her story and one offer to sign a revised account saying the kitchen sounds were radio contamination.
  “Have you spoken to her?” Elian asked.
  “She wants us to come. Not a broadcast. People who can tell her whether the child is safe.”
  “We don't know that.”
  “I told her.”
  Daniel asked whether the stone could be another receiver. Hale said it could contain one. That was not the same as the entire structure being a machine. He had learned to begin with differences while other people rushed toward the shape that made the best headline.
  Mara looked at the six bands inside the boat. A staircase ran along one side, then vanished where the drawing had been damaged. She had spent years sorting passenger records and could recognize a missing field in any form.
  “There's a way out that isn't shown.”
  Hale requested a closer picture. The drawing contained a narrow blank line from the lowest band to the stern. It might be damage. It might be a service route. Elian thought of the ark's hidden annex and the immense harm done by rooms somebody had called necessary without explaining them to the household above.
  She asked Mara to mark the line on a copy, never the original.
  June brought paper. Then she brought the sixth plate from the cupboard and set it beside the lamp. No one commented. The meal was cold by then, but they served themselves again while Hale read Tessa's first report aloud.
  Before the meal ended, Miriam asked June what she planned to put in the history assignment. The girl described the town before disappearance: a shop sign painted badly, a broken drinking fountain, a teacher who remembered every child's older siblings.
  “Not the famous part?” Miriam asked.
  “Someone else will do that.”
  Miriam nodded. She had spent years hoping to be found and discovered afterward how quickly a recovered person became a story other people already knew. June's small details would leave room for inhabitants rather than only the event.
  She offered to record an interview about ordinary life before her own disappearance. June accepted on condition that Miriam could stop whenever she wanted. They arranged a time instead of beginning over supper.
  Elian watched them make the appointment. The next rescue would demand her attention. This one needed an hour already agreed between two people.
  Afterward, Elian found June washing dishes with unnecessary force.
  “You can still have ordinary evenings,” Elian said.
  June handed her a towel.
  “I know. I wanted this one.”
  Elian dried a bowl. There was no defense that would make the girl wrong. She would have to go to Alaska, and it would still be true that somebody deserved an evening without a doorway.
  They finished the dishes. Then June asked what coat her aunt would take. The weather report predicted rain, and grief had not made Elian good at packing.
  Before leaving, Elian wrote her flight details where June could find them. She promised to call after landing, not to return by a date she could not guarantee. Daniel offered a jar of preserved fruit for the journey. Mara put it in a padded bag without telling either of them that she had kept the spare plate out.
Chapter 3
The Surveyor's Daughter
Nadiya Chen met Elian in Anchorage with a cardboard model wrapped in a blanket.
  “My daughter thinks it looks cold without a roof,” she said.
  The model was a long curved building divided by transverse walls. Its bow ended in a glazed commons. Its stern held stairs and a service court. Beneath it, small blocks represented foundations placed on rock rather than on a continuously heated slab. Someone had drawn beds in several rooms and a dog in the garden.
  Elian knew the shape before Nadiya unfolded the final flap.
  “Where did you get this?”
  “My father drew it before he died.”
  They stood among travelers collecting skis, equipment cases, and ordinary luggage. A family argued over a misplaced mitten. The airport made the model's impossible familiarity harder to dismiss. Nadiya was not presenting an oracle. She needed to find her rental vehicle before its office closed.
  Her father, Elias Chen, had been a stonemason who restored old public buildings. He loved concealed workmanship: a date on the back of a lintel, initials beneath a stair tread, a small carved animal nobody could see after the roof was finished. He said buildings deserved memories that were not advertisements.
  His lodge friends had helped set several ceremonial cornerstones. His wife, Lin, had arranged homes according to a spatial tradition she had learned from her mother, paying attention to entrances, water, movement, and the places where a person could sit without feeling exposed. Their daughter had grown up between a plumb bob and arguments about where light belonged. Neither parent had taught her that a symbol could replace a load calculation.
  Elias died in 2032. The model had been in his workshop for years.
  “Did he work in Alaska?” Elian asked.
  “Once. The company said it was a cold-storage facility.”
  They drove to a small guesthouse where Nadiya's husband, Owen Calder, was trying to assemble a travel cot for their son. Kit was six and refused to sleep in anything described as travel equipment. Nell, thirteen, had appropriated the desk and covered it with drawings of the model's interior.
  Owen introduced himself and waited for Elian to recognize the surname.
  “Rafe's brother,” he said when she did.
  Rafe had not mentioned a brother. Owen heard the silence correctly and returned to the cot. Families divided for reasons too ordinary to appear in the archive: a loan, a funeral, a sentence repeated in anger until nobody remembered the first version.
  Nell pointed to the model's lowest band.
  “Granddad called this the remembering room.”
  Nadiya gently corrected her. “He called it a memory store. Paper, tools, spare parts.”
  “Then why does it have a window looking into a wall?”
  Elian crouched beside the desk. The girl had found a small aperture on the model's hidden side. It aligned with a blank space in the corresponding plan.
  “Sometimes models contain mistakes,” Nadiya said.
  Nell lifted a loose cardboard panel. Beneath it was a folded tracing, yellow at the edges. Her mother stared. She had moved the model through three houses and never removed that section because it appeared to be part of the base.
  The tracing showed a pyramid in section. At its center lay a chamber shaped like the negative space beneath the model's keel. Around the drawing ran a series of domestic measurements: table height, stair rise, a child's reach, the turning circle of a chair.
  “These aren't dimensions for a tomb,” Nadiya said.
  “They're for people living there,” Elian answered.
  Owen stopped fighting the cot and came to look. His face changed when he saw the contractor's mark at the bottom. His freight company had moved stone under that mark years earlier. The invoices described protective cladding. Several shipments had been paid through Orison's older accounts.
  Nadiya looked from her husband to the tracing.
  “You knew?”
  “I knew a customer. I didn't know a pyramid.”
  The distinction was true and insufficient. Elian watched them reach the edge of an argument they would need privacy to have. She took Kit downstairs to ask the guesthouse owner about hot chocolate.
  He wanted to know whether all houses had hidden rooms. She said most had spaces people forgot to look at. He considered the answer, then asked whether his father would leave if his mother remained angry.
  “I don't know what they'll say to each other,” Elian told him. “They know you're here.”
  He pressed both hands around the cup.
  Upstairs, Nell listened to her parents lower their voices. She had wanted finding the tracing to make them proud. Instead it had given them something else to fear. She placed the little cardboard roof back over the model, smoothing the blanket as if it could keep the house quiet.
  When Elian returned, Owen had laid his old invoices beside Elias's tracing. Nadiya had opened a fresh notebook and written three headings: what we know, what we assumed, what we still owe.
  They agreed to travel together. Owen would answer questions about the freight. Nadiya would examine the construction. Nell would stay with the guesthouse owner during the first site visit, which she considered an intolerable injustice until her mother entrusted her with the original model.
  Before bed, Nell brought her mother a separate drawing. It showed the model as a house rather than a section: curtains, a shoe rack, a place where Kit could leave a toy without tripping someone.
  “Those aren't technical details,” Nadiya said, then heard herself.
  “They tell you what people will do.”
  Her daughter was right. Architectural plans contained beds that never needed making and tables without crumbs. Life would supply the obstructions.
  Nadiya asked permission to copy the drawing into her working notebook.
  Nell demanded an author's credit.
  They wrote her name beneath the sheet. Kit, listening from the floor, insisted that the dog receive one too. They did not own a dog. Nell explained that future residents might, and his objection became another note: accommodation for animals must be planned before someone arrives refusing to abandon one.
  Owen read it and offered to measure appropriate carriers.
  Their family had not solved the freight records. They had found one useful thing they could do together without pretending the argument was over.
  That night, Elian called Rafe.
  His first response was a long breath. His second was an address for a repair shop where Owen could obtain a part for the survey vehicle. He said nothing about forgiveness. Elian passed on the address anyway.
  By morning, the family had built the cot correctly. Kit was sleeping on the floor beside it.
Chapter 4
A Mountain Is Not an Empty Place
Amos Reid stopped their vehicle before the track entered the research parcel.
  He had a clipboard, a thermos, and no interest in the names that had made Elian recognizable on television. The gate remained shut while he checked their equipment list against the agreement negotiated with Iona Marsh. No sampling without authorization. No published coordinates. No removal of objects because someone considered them personally significant.
  “It's stone,” Owen said, looking toward the slope. “Not a collection of jewelry.”
  Amos tapped the folded tracing in Nadiya's hand.
  “People have emptied whole places one interesting stone at a time.”
  Owen accepted the correction. The gate opened.
  Tessa waited at the field cabin, older than the woman in her archived photograph, with a repaired waterproof coat and the lunch kit that still lacked its spoon. She greeted Elian by asking about the girl called Nell. When Nadiya said her daughter was safe in town, Tessa looked away for a moment and then asked whether they wanted tea before the climb.
  The land had changed since 2026. The original slump had been stabilized around the access area, but other parts of the valley carried new scars. Tessa explained thaw-related movement, drainage changes, the difference between ground that looked solid and ground that a summer's warmth could undermine. Nadiya listened closely. A refuge built without understanding its ground could preserve every symbolic proportion and still tilt into its own service trench.
  They approached on a raised path that kept visitors away from the excavation edges. The pyramid emerged slowly from the slope: pale courses under protective sheeting, a steep face running down toward a cut made for scientific access. Its size was impressive without resembling the fantasies sold in online videos. They could see only part of the whole.
  Iona met them at the entrance. Her first question concerned Elias's tracing. Her second concerned its custody history. Nadiya wanted to discuss the resemblance. Iona wanted to establish whether the tracing had been altered after the discovery became known.
  “He was dead before the files were released,” Nadiya said.
  “That helps date possession. It doesn't date the drawing.”
  The bluntness hurt. It also made Nadiya trust her more than she wanted to.
  They photographed the paper, compared its fiber and annotations with Elias's dated notebooks, and set aside the parts they could not yet establish. His drawing might be evidence of involvement in a modern installation. It did not automatically prove a visitor from another century had given him instructions.
  Inside, the passage descended between stone faces fitted with unusual care. The seams were narrow. Tool marks remained where no ceremonial visitor would have looked. Nadiya ran her light across the work and recognized a mason's sequence: reference line, rough fit, final dressing. Several marks resembled her father's habits. She did not say so until she had photographed them.
  At the lower landing, a blue line crossed the floor.
  “That's the tile from the camera,” Elian said.
  Iona stopped them before the line. Instruments showed a small timing discrepancy across it. They were not to cross until the monitoring team understood whether the difference was stable.
  From beyond the line came the smell of cooling bread.
  Nadiya made a sound Elian had never heard from an adult trying to remain professional. Her mother had baked a particular dark loaf when Elias worked late. The smell was close enough to make her want the past before she had considered the danger.
  Tessa took her arm.
  “I spent seven years wanting my spoon back,” she said. “We can wait another ten minutes.”
  They waited nearly an hour. The discrepancy narrowed, then widened, then settled into a measurable rhythm. Ada, speaking through an isolated data link from Haven's clinic, advised against opening any active receiver. The site already contained a passive overlap. They could observe it without feeding it more power.
  Elian set a camera on their side and asked anyone beyond the line to answer.
  For several minutes, only water moved somewhere beneath the floor. Then a woman's voice said, “Which year are you asking from?”
  “2034.”
  Silence.
  The woman spoke again, more quietly.
  “We were told not to answer that year.”
  Iona asked her name. The voice withdrew. A chair scraped, exactly as it had in Tessa's first recording.
  Nadiya found herself staring at the wall beside the blue line. A shallow niche held a ceramic cup. Its handle had been repaired with a staple, an old method her father had admired. Beneath it was a tiny scratched boat and a date written in Elias's hand.
  Not his birth year. Not the year he had worked in Alaska.
  2038.
  Owen reached toward her, but she had already moved back from the boundary. She sat on the cold landing and took out her notebook. Her father was dead. A date in his handwriting did not make that untrue. It made the story of his last years incomplete in a way she could no longer bear to ignore.
  She drew the niche, its height, the cup's shadow, the path of the blue line. Then she noticed that the shadow pointed toward a seam higher than the doorway.
  “There's another room,” she said.
  Iona examined the wall and nodded once. They would investigate it with permission, supports, and witnesses. The day's discovery ended there, with nobody crossing a line they could not yet explain.
  At the cabin, Iona showed them a catalog from an unrelated archaeological survey. Its most informative object was an ordinary fragment found beside a hearth, significant because someone had recorded precisely where it lay.
  “A thing without its setting can tell a different story every time someone wants one,” she said.
  Nadiya thought about the cup. A dramatic owner might have seized it as proof of Elias's survival. An impatient contractor might have thrown it away. Its height, repairs, position, and manufacturing history now kept several possible explanations apart.
  She asked Iona to include her emotional identification in the field record.
  “Not as proof,” she said. “As something that could make me overlook other evidence.”
  Iona entered the statement without treating it as a confession.
  Outside, Amos checked the valley's weather station. People still needed ordinary forecasts while studying an extraordinary room. He sent the team a road update and told them when the next vehicle would leave.
  No one was allowed to become so fascinated by the monument that they forgot how to get home.
  On the walk back, Nadiya called Nell. She asked about the model's window, then listened to her daughter describe it in exacting detail. For the first time since arriving in Alaska, she understood that entrusting the girl with the model had been more than a consolation prize.
Chapter 5
The Square and the Hanging Weight
The hidden room opened because Owen knew how heavy a shelf ought to be.
  He had spent half his working life deciding whether a crate's declared weight matched the machine required to move it. The niche above the blue line looked shallow. Its lintel extended far deeper than the surrounding construction required. Someone had disguised a supported opening as an ordinary change in the masonry.
  They did not pull on it. Iona called the structural team. Amos checked the access agreement. Nadiya drew what they could see and marked the parts they were guessing. By afternoon, the team had installed support and located a release through an existing inspection hole. The panel moved outward on a track beneath its dust.
  Behind it lay a room no wider than a bedroom. A desk stood beneath a dead lamp. Ceiling grooves represented the six bands in Elias's boat drawing. On the desk rested a plumb bob, a square, and a packet sealed inside a food container.
  Nadiya recognized the container. Her father had complained about its lid for twenty years.
  She put her hands in her pockets while Iona photographed it. When the packet was opened under the camera, it contained copies of Elias's drawings and a letter addressed to his daughter. He had written it in 2031.
  My dearest girl, if this reaches you after the hill has opened, I owe you a better explanation than the company allowed me to give.
  Nadiya asked everyone except Elian to leave. Iona explained that the room remained an active investigation. They compromised on the doorway, where the others could wait without pretending the letter had no owner.
  Elias described a consultation in which contractors showed him masonry supposedly recovered from an old installation. They wanted a plan based on its proportions. He identified weak joints, inaccessible services, and a map repeated at several scales. The company became interested when he explained that the pattern could preserve maintenance instructions without electricity.
  Then they showed him a recording of himself, older, standing inside the completed chamber.
  He thought it a trick. Later he thought it a promise. By the time he understood that it was persuasion, he had signed enough papers to make silence look like his own decision.
  Nadiya stopped reading. Elian knew what waited before she saw the next paragraph. Every compromised system seemed to discover the same mechanism: give someone a glimpse of something they want, then charge obedience for the rest.
  Elias had designed joints and a mnemonic plan for a proposed refuge. He had never visited the pyramid. The recording used a body scan taken during a contract medical examination. His future appearance had been generated, not witnessed.
  The date 2038 belonged to a construction schedule.
  Nadiya cried with an anger sharpened by relief. For a day she had entertained the possibility that her father lived somewhere she could reach. Now she had his explanation and his death together.
  At the end, Elias wrote about the tools. His lodge used them as moral images. He had allowed a company to use those images to make its ambitions seem older and wiser than its accounts. The tools still measured what they measured. They did not authorize the people holding them.
  She examined the square. Calibration marks matched the ceiling's miniature bays. Suspended from the lamp hook, the plumb bob aligned with a particular groove.
  Iona permitted a documented test. Nadiya hung the weight and overlaid a tracing on the ceiling image.
  The point indicated a missing stair.
  “Not a cipher,” she said. “A section reference.”
  A future worker could recognize the construction problem. Someone who only admired the symbols might stare at the wrong wall for years. Elias had hidden his instructions where attention to the work itself could recover them.
  They returned to the landing. The matching seam lay below the upper stair flight, where the full-scale plan placed an inspection panel. Behind it was a cable chase and another document sleeve.
  This one contained no family letter.
  Its first page bore the Restoration Trust's emblem, amended by a newer hand. The second listed Laura Kest's residents. The youngest had no surname. Beside her entry somebody had written HOUSE CHILD — RETAIN.
  Elian called Laura through the overlap. The woman answered before the question ended.
  “Don't tell them you've found her.”
  They could not yet tell whether Laura meant herself or the child. Elian asked only who might be listening. Laura described a ceiling speaker that sometimes repeated conversations she had believed private. Its operator claimed the repetition was part of the shelter's protective monitoring.
  Hale asked her to identify an unpowered room.
  “The laundry. There is a sink. The lights fail when we use the heater.”
  “Then don't turn the heater on for us,” he said. “We'll meet you beside the sink if we can verify the opening.”
  Nadiya photographed the roster again, including its blank spaces. She had wanted the chamber to contain her father. Instead it held evidence of a child whom adults had agreed to store as if a category could replace a family.
  Back at the cabin, she opened the container once more to examine its underside. Elias had scratched a final sentence where the packet would protect it from abrasion.
  If you build it, let the people know where the rooms are.
  She copied that instruction onto the first page of her notebook. Then she telephoned her mother and read it aloud. Lin listened without speaking until Nadiya reached the end.
  “He said that after I left,” Lin answered.
  There was another story waiting there. Nadiya asked whether she could hear it. Her mother said yes, but not while her daughter was exhausted beside a discovered doorway.
  Nadiya spent the evening comparing the recovered tool marks with photographs from her father's workshop. One curved finish initially looked identical. Enlarged, it differed in the spacing of the strokes. A later mason had copied a method, not necessarily a hand.
  She felt both disappointed and steadied.
  The distinction gave the workers who came after Elias their own existence. They had learned, imitated, corrected, and sometimes misunderstood. Calling every good joint her father's would erase them as efficiently as a company logo.
  She added UNKNOWN WORKSHOP HAND to the record and left space for a name.
  When Owen saw the annotation, he asked whether she minded not knowing.
  “Yes.”
  She turned the page.
  “Minding isn't evidence.”
  That night, they talked about food instead. It was the first call in years in which neither had mistaken postponement for refusal.
Chapter 6
The Girl Who Remembered the Orchard
The first visitor crossed carrying no machine capable of commanding anyone.
  Salma insisted on that condition. Laura described a frightened settlement whose administrators examined objects for possible disobedience. A medical bag could be opened. A hidden transmitter could become an excuse to lock everyone down.
  The overlap reached a utility room. Its boundary remained stable for twelve minutes in repeated unoccupied tests. Salma would use a physical tether and an agreed withdrawal signal. No patient would move until the receiving team understood the next door.
  Laura met her beside the sink.
  She was thinner than the photograph Adrian Kest had displayed. Her hair was unevenly cut. She asked whether her husband still held office, then heard about the suspended powers, the inquiry, and his continuing search.
  “Searching doesn't cancel what he did.”
  “No.”
  Only then did she ask whether he was well.
  The infirmary occupied a converted laundry. Sheets had been mended from older sheets. The people maintaining it had too few supplies and answered to an office that valued order more than the reasons residents were distressed.
  A girl watched from behind a curtain. She held a slate and a chipped blue cup. Her solemn expression belonged to a child who knew which questions made adults exchange looks.
  “Iris,” Laura called. “You can come here if you want.”
  She stayed behind the curtain.
  Salma sat on the floor. Her knees complained. She let Iris hear the complaint and smiled when the girl glanced toward her.
  “Do you always sit there?”
  “Only when the chairs are too far away.”
  “The chairs belong to examinations.”
  Salma looked at the numbered seats along the wall and moved her bag away from them.
  “Then this is a conversation.”
  Iris showed her an orchard drawn in plan: a path around a central tree, a square at one edge. Elian recognized Sela's garden from the archive.
  “How do you know that place?” Salma asked.
  “I went there in my sleep.”
  Laura touched the curtain instead of the girl. Administrators claimed Iris possessed inherited temporal memory. They used her drawings to identify intervals, then said her visions justified keeping her apart. Her parents' names had vanished from the common roster.
  Salma asked what Iris remembered before the garden.
  “A room with a light in the wall. They put pictures on it. If I remembered, I could have the blue cup.”

1  God Makes the World
Genesis 1–2
Before there were houses, oceans, trees, birds, or people, there was God. God spoke, and light appeared. He made the sky, the seas, the dry land, and every kind of plant. He filled the sky with the sun, moon, and stars. He filled the water with fish and the air with birds. Then He made animals of every kind. At last, God made people in His image. Adam and Eve were given a beautiful garden to care for. God looked at everything He had made and called it very good. The story of creation reminds us that the world is not an accident. Life is a gift, and people are called to care for the earth and for one another.
2  Adam and Eve Choose Their Own Way
Genesis 3
Adam and Eve lived in a beautiful garden and enjoyed friendship with God. God gave them freedom and one clear boundary. A serpent questioned God's words and made disobedience sound exciting. Adam and Eve chose to distrust God and eat what He had told them not to eat. Right away they felt shame and fear. They hid, but God came looking for them. Their choice brought pain and death into the human story, yet God did not stop loving them. He promised that evil would not win forever. Even when people make wrong choices, the Bible shows God beginning a plan to rescue and restore.
3  Noah Builds an Ark
Genesis 6–9
The world had become full of violence, but Noah trusted God. God told Noah to build a huge ark because a flood was coming. Building it took faith, patience, and a lot of work. Noah followed God's instructions while his family helped him. Animals came into the ark, and then the rain began. Water covered the land, but the ark floated safely. After many days, the waters went down. Noah sent out birds to look for dry land, and finally the family stepped outside. Noah thanked God. God placed a rainbow in the sky as a sign of His promise. The story teaches faithfulness, obedience, and hope after a storm.
4  The Tower of Babel
Genesis 11:1–9
After the flood, people began building a city with a giant tower. They wanted to make themselves famous and keep everyone together under their own plan. God saw that their pride was growing. He confused their languages so they could no longer understand one another easily. The building project stopped, and the people spread into different lands. Babel is a story about pride and about people trying to build greatness without God. It reminds us that being impressive is not the same as being wise. God values humility, faithfulness, and people who listen to Him.
5  God Calls Abraham
Genesis 12:1–9
Abraham lived in a familiar place with his family, but God asked him to travel to a new land. God promised to bless Abraham and make his family a blessing to many nations. Abraham did not know every detail of the journey. He simply trusted God enough to begin. He packed his belongings, traveled with Sarah and their household, and followed where God led. Along the way Abraham built altars and remembered God's promises. The story teaches that faith sometimes means taking the next step before we can see the whole road. God can use an ordinary family to bring blessing far beyond what they imagine.
6  Abraham and Sarah Receive a Promise
Genesis 15; 18; 21
Abraham and Sarah grew old without the child God had promised them. At times the promise seemed impossible. One night God brought Abraham outside and told him to look at the stars. His family, God said, would become more numerous than he could count. Years passed, and Abraham and Sarah had to keep learning trust. At last their son Isaac was born. Sarah laughed with joy. The long wait taught them that God's timing can be different from ours. The story encourages children to remember that waiting is not the same as being forgotten. Hope can remain even when an answer takes a long time.
7  Jacob's Dream
Genesis 28:10–22
Jacob was traveling alone and stopped for the night. He used a stone for a pillow and fell asleep beneath the open sky. In a dream he saw a stairway stretching between earth and heaven, with angels going up and down. God spoke to Jacob and repeated the promises given to his family. When Jacob woke up, he realized that God had been near even though he had felt alone. He set up the stone as a reminder and promised to follow God. Jacob's dream teaches that a lonely place can become a place of hope. God can meet people when they feel uncertain, afraid, or far from home.
8  Joseph and His Colorful Coat
Genesis 37
Joseph was one of Jacob's sons. His father gave him a special robe, and Joseph also had dreams about his future. His brothers became jealous. Instead of dealing with their anger wisely, they sold Joseph to travelers going to Egypt and told their father a terrible lie. Joseph suddenly lost his home and family. The story begins with unfairness and sadness, but it does not end there. Later, God would use Joseph's difficult journey to save many lives. This story helps children understand that jealousy can hurt people badly, while faith and courage can keep growing even during hard changes.
9  Joseph Forgives His Brothers
Genesis 39–50
Joseph's life in Egypt was full of ups and downs. He worked faithfully, was treated unfairly, spent time in prison, and eventually helped Pharaoh understand important dreams. Joseph became a leader who prepared Egypt for a famine. Years later, his brothers came looking for food and stood before him without recognizing him. Joseph had power to get revenge, but he chose forgiveness. He wept, revealed who he was, and helped his whole family survive. Joseph did not pretend the wrong things had been good. Instead, he trusted that God could bring good even through painful events. Forgiveness opened the door to a restored family.
10  Baby Moses Is Protected
Exodus 1–2
A cruel Pharaoh ordered Hebrew baby boys to be killed. Moses' mother hid her baby as long as she could. Then she placed him in a carefully prepared basket and set it among the reeds of the Nile while his sister watched nearby. Pharaoh's daughter found the baby and decided to protect him. Moses' own mother was even able to care for him while he was small. What looked like a helpless situation became part of a much larger story. Moses would grow up and later lead God's people out of slavery. The story shows courage, loving protection, and the surprising ways help can appear.
11  The Burning Bush
Exodus 3–4
Moses was caring for sheep in the wilderness when he saw a bush that seemed to burn without being destroyed. As he came closer, God called his name. God told Moses that He had seen the suffering of His people in Egypt and wanted Moses to help lead them to freedom. Moses felt nervous and gave many reasons why he might not be the right person. God promised to be with him. Moses learned that courage does not mean never feeling afraid. Courage can mean obeying God while still feeling nervous. The burning bush became the beginning of Moses' great mission.
12  Crossing the Red Sea
Exodus 14
After Pharaoh finally let the Israelites leave Egypt, he changed his mind and sent an army after them. The people found themselves trapped between the soldiers and the sea. They were terrified. Moses told them to trust God. God made a way through the water, and the people crossed on dry ground. When the danger had passed, they celebrated their rescue. The Red Sea story became one of Israel's great memories of deliverance. It teaches that people can feel trapped and still discover that a path exists where they could not see one before. Fear does not have to be the final word.
13  Bread in the Wilderness
Exodus 16
The Israelites were traveling through the wilderness and began worrying about food. God promised to provide. In the morning, small flakes appeared on the ground. The people called the food manna. They gathered enough for each day and learned not to hoard it. Before the Sabbath they gathered extra so they could rest. The manna taught the people to depend on God one day at a time. It also taught them that rest mattered. The story reminds children that daily needs can be met in ordinary ways and that gratitude is healthier than constant complaining.
14  The Ten Commandments
Exodus 19–20
At Mount Sinai, God gave Israel commandments to shape their life as a community. The first commandments focused on loving and honoring God. The others taught people to honor parents, protect life, tell the truth, respect marriage, avoid stealing, and refuse greedy jealousy. The commandments were not given as a ladder for earning God's rescue; Israel had already been brought out of slavery. They were instructions for living as God's rescued people. Jesus later summarized God's law as loving God and loving our neighbors. The Ten Commandments remind us that love has practical shape.
15  Joshua and Jericho
Joshua 6
Jericho was a fortified city with strong walls. Joshua and the Israelites were told to follow unusual instructions. For six days they marched around the city. On the seventh day they marched around it seven times. Priests blew trumpets, the people shouted, and the walls fell. The story emphasizes trust in God's direction rather than confidence in human strength. Joshua's army still had to obey carefully and act courageously, but the victory was not presented as something they could brag about. It was a reminder that God's ways may surprise us.
16  Gideon's Small Army
Judges 6–7
Gideon did not feel like a great hero. When God called him, Gideon felt weak and unsure. Yet God promised to be with him. Gideon gathered an army, but God told him the group was too large. Eventually only three hundred men remained. They carried torches, jars, and trumpets. At the right moment they broke the jars, raised the lights, blew the trumpets, and surprised the enemy camp. Gideon's story teaches that success does not always depend on having the biggest team or the most impressive tools. Faithfulness and courage can matter more than appearances.
17  Ruth Chooses Loyalty
Ruth 1–4
Ruth lost her husband and faced an uncertain future. Her mother-in-law Naomi planned to return to Bethlehem and told Ruth she could stay behind. Ruth chose to go with Naomi. She promised to remain loyal and make Naomi's people her people. In Bethlehem, Ruth worked hard gathering leftover grain so they would have food. A kind man named Boaz noticed her faithfulness and treated her with respect. Eventually Ruth became part of a new family and an ancestor of King David. Her story celebrates loyalty, kindness, hard work, and the way outsiders can be welcomed into God's story.
18  Samuel Hears God's Voice
1 Samuel 3
Samuel was a boy serving at the tabernacle under Eli. One night he heard someone call his name. Samuel ran to Eli, but Eli had not called him. This happened again. Finally Eli realized that God was calling the boy. He told Samuel to answer, 'Speak, for your servant is listening.' Samuel listened carefully to God's message. The story reminds children that listening is an important part of faith. People often think important work belongs only to adults, but Samuel's story shows that young people can learn to hear, understand, and respond to God's truth.
19  David and Goliath
1 Samuel 17
A giant soldier named Goliath challenged Israel's army. Everyone was afraid. David was a young shepherd who had come to bring food to his brothers. He could not understand why everyone thought the giant was unbeatable. David refused heavy armor he was not used to wearing. He took his sling and stones, trusting God rather than Goliath's size. David's stone struck Goliath, and the giant fell. The story is not a promise that every problem disappears instantly. It is a lesson that courage can grow when we remember that frightening things are not bigger than God.
20  David Shows Mercy to Saul
1 Samuel 24
King Saul became jealous of David and chased him. One day Saul entered a cave without realizing David and his men were hiding deeper inside. David had the opportunity to hurt Saul, but he chose not to. He quietly cut a piece from Saul's robe instead. Later David showed Saul the cloth and explained that he could have attacked him but had refused. David trusted God to deal with the situation rather than taking revenge. The story teaches self-control and mercy. Having the power to hurt someone does not mean we should use it.
21  Solomon Asks for Wisdom
1 Kings 3
When Solomon became king, he knew the job was bigger than he was. God invited him to ask for what he wanted. Solomon did not ask first for riches, long life, or victory over enemies. He asked for wisdom so he could lead the people well. God was pleased with that request. Solomon became famous for wise decisions. His story reminds children that being smart is not just knowing facts. Wisdom means understanding what matters, listening carefully, and choosing what is right. Asking for wisdom is a good prayer at any age.
22  Elijah and the Quiet Voice
1 Kings 19
After a dramatic victory, Elijah became exhausted, afraid, and discouraged. He ran into the wilderness and felt completely alone. God first gave him rest, food, and time. Later Elijah stood on a mountain. A powerful wind came, then an earthquake, then fire, but the story says God's message came in a quiet voice. Elijah learned that God was still working even when he could not see it. The story is gentle with tired people. Sometimes what we need first is rest and care. God's presence is not always loud or dramatic.
23  Naaman Learns Humility
2 Kings 5
Naaman was an important army commander, but he had a serious skin disease. A young Israelite servant girl told his family about the prophet Elisha. Naaman traveled to see him, expecting something impressive. Instead Elisha sent a simple message: wash in the Jordan River seven times. Naaman felt insulted because the instruction seemed too ordinary. His servants encouraged him to try. When he obeyed, he was healed. Naaman learned that pride can make simple help hard to accept. Humility means being willing to listen even when the answer is not flashy.
24  Esther Speaks Up
Esther 2–8
Esther became queen of Persia, but she had not told the king that she was Jewish. When a powerful official made a plan to destroy the Jewish people, Esther's cousin Mordecai asked her to speak to the king. Approaching the king without being invited could be dangerous. Esther asked her people to fast, then bravely went in. She carefully explained the danger, and the king acted to protect her people. Esther's story teaches that courage can mean using your voice when others are in danger. A person may be in a particular place at a particular time for a good purpose.
25  Daniel Chooses Faithfulness
Daniel 1
Daniel and his friends were taken far from home to Babylon. They were taught a new language and trained to serve the king. Even though much around them changed, they wanted to remain faithful to God. Daniel respectfully asked for permission to follow a simple diet rather than eat the king's food. The official agreed to a test. Daniel and his friends did well, and they continued learning. Their story shows that faithfulness can be quiet and respectful. You do not have to be rude to stand for what you believe is right.
26  Daniel in the Lions' Den
Daniel 6
Daniel served the king so faithfully that jealous leaders tried to trap him. They convinced the king to make a law that would punish anyone who prayed to anyone except the king. Daniel continued praying to God as he always had. He was thrown into a lions' den. The king was upset and hoped Daniel's God would rescue him. In the morning Daniel was alive. The story teaches steady faith. Daniel did not suddenly begin trusting God when trouble came; he had been building a life of prayer for years.
27  Jonah and the Big Fish
Jonah 1–4
God told Jonah to go to Nineveh and warn the city to change its ways. Jonah ran in the opposite direction and boarded a ship. A terrible storm came, and Jonah eventually ended up in the sea, where a great fish swallowed him. Inside the fish Jonah prayed. Later he was brought safely to land and went to Nineveh. The people listened and turned from their wrong actions. Jonah was surprised that God showed them mercy. The story teaches that God's compassion can reach people we may not expect. It also teaches that running from responsibility usually makes the journey harder.
28  Three Friends in the Fiery Furnace
Daniel 3
Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego were commanded to bow to a giant golden image. They refused because they worshiped God. The king became furious and ordered them thrown into a blazing furnace. But when he looked inside, he saw the three men walking around unharmed—and another figure with them. The friends came out without even smelling like smoke. Their story is about courage under pressure. They did not know in advance exactly how God would help them. They simply decided that fear would not choose their worship for them.
29  The Angel Visits Mary
Luke 1:26–38
Mary was a young woman living in Nazareth when the angel Gabriel came with an astonishing message. She would have a son named Jesus, and He would be called the Son of the Most High. Mary was confused and asked how this could happen. The angel explained that God's power would be at work. Mary chose trust and said she was willing to serve God's purpose. Her life would not suddenly become easy, but she accepted the calling with faith. Mary's story teaches humble courage and willingness to say yes to God.
30  Jesus Is Born
Luke 2:1–20
Mary and Joseph traveled to Bethlehem because of a government census. The town was crowded, and they found no normal guest room available. Jesus was born in humble surroundings and laid in a manger. Nearby shepherds were watching their flocks at night when angels announced good news: a Savior had been born. The shepherds hurried to Bethlehem and found Mary, Joseph, and the baby. They returned praising God. The Christmas story shows greatness arriving in a humble place. God's love did not enter the world with a palace parade but as a child.
31  The Wise Men Follow the Star
Matthew 2:1–12
Wise men from the east saw a special star and believed it announced the birth of a king. They traveled a long distance looking for Jesus. Their search brought them to Jerusalem and then to Bethlehem. When they found the child with Mary, they bowed down and gave gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. They had invested time, effort, and treasure in their search. The story reminds us that seeking truth can require a journey. The wise men also show that Jesus' significance reached beyond one town or nation.
32  Jesus Calms the Storm
Mark 4:35–41
Jesus and His disciples crossed the Sea of Galilee in a boat. A violent storm rose, and waves began filling the boat. Jesus was asleep. The frightened disciples woke Him and asked if He cared that they were in danger. Jesus stood and commanded the wind and sea to be still. The storm calmed. Then He asked the disciples why they were so afraid. They were amazed. The story does not say God's followers never face storms. It shows that fear can be brought to Jesus and that He is present even when it seems like He is silent.
33  Jesus Feeds a Huge Crowd
John 6:1–14
A huge crowd followed Jesus, and the people needed food. A boy had five barley loaves and two small fish. It did not look like enough for thousands of people. The boy's food was brought to Jesus. Jesus gave thanks, the food was shared, and everyone ate. There were even leftovers. The story shows that small gifts can become meaningful when offered generously. The boy did not feed the crowd by himself; he simply offered what he had. Children can participate in doing good right now, even when their resources seem small.
34  The Good Samaritan
Luke 10:25–37
A man traveling on a road was attacked by robbers and left hurt. Two respected religious people passed by without helping him. Then a Samaritan came. Samaritans and Jews were often enemies, yet this traveler stopped. He treated the man's wounds, placed him on his animal, brought him to an inn, and paid for his care. Jesus told this story when someone asked, 'Who is my neighbor?' The answer was not about living nearby or belonging to the same group. A neighbor is someone who chooses mercy. Love crosses boundaries.
35  Jesus Welcomes Children
Mark 10:13–16
Families brought children to Jesus so He could bless them. The disciples thought the children were getting in the way and tried to send them off. Jesus disagreed. He welcomed the children and said God's kingdom belonged to people who received it with childlike trust. Then He took the children in His arms and blessed them. This short story is important because it shows how Jesus valued children. They were not background characters or interruptions. They mattered to Him. Children can pray, learn, love, serve, and belong in the community of faith.
36  Zacchaeus Changes
Luke 19:1–10
Zacchaeus collected taxes and had become rich, but many people disliked him because tax collectors often cheated others. Zacchaeus wanted to see Jesus, but he was short and could not see over the crowd. He climbed a tree. Jesus looked up and told Zacchaeus He wanted to visit his home. People complained, but Zacchaeus responded by choosing a changed life. He promised to give generously and repay people he had cheated. His story shows that meeting Jesus is meant to change how we treat other people. Real repentance produces action.
37  The Lost Sheep
Luke 15:1–7
Jesus told a story about a shepherd who had one hundred sheep. When one wandered away, the shepherd did not say, 'Ninety-nine is good enough.' He went looking for the missing sheep. When he found it, he carried it home and celebrated. Jesus used the story to explain God's joy when a lost person returns. The sheep is valuable because it belongs to the shepherd, not because it performs some impressive trick. The story tells children that each person matters. Nobody is just a number in a crowd.
38  The Prodigal Son Comes Home
Luke 15:11–32
A younger son asked his father for his inheritance early and left home. He wasted the money and eventually became desperate and hungry. He decided to return home and ask to be treated like a servant. But while he was still far away, his father saw him, ran to him, and welcomed him home. The father celebrated his return. The older brother struggled with the celebration, and the father reminded him that restoring the lost son was worth rejoicing over. Jesus' story paints repentance as coming home and grace as a welcome we do not earn.
39  Jesus Raises Lazarus
John 11:1–44
Lazarus became very sick, and his sisters Mary and Martha sent word to Jesus. By the time Jesus arrived, Lazarus had died. Martha told Jesus she believed God could still work. Jesus said, 'I am the resurrection and the life.' At the tomb Jesus wept with the grieving family. Then He called Lazarus to come out, and Lazarus walked from the tomb alive. The story shows both Jesus' compassion and His power. He did not scold people for grieving. He cried with them. Christian hope does not pretend death is painless; it looks toward resurrection.
40  The Last Supper
Luke 22:7–20; John 13
Before Jesus was arrested, He shared a final Passover meal with His disciples. During the evening He washed their feet, doing a servant's job. He told them to love one another with the same humble spirit. Jesus also shared bread and a cup, giving them new meaning connected to His coming sacrifice. Christians later remembered this meal in Communion. The Last Supper teaches that greatness in Jesus' kingdom looks like serving. It also reminds believers that the cross was not an accident; Jesus was preparing His friends to understand what He was about to do.

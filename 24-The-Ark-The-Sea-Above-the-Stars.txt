CHAPTER 1
The Knock Below the Garden
The first knock came while Nell was cutting bread. She held the knife still and waited for the second. All around the galley, the ark made its old sounds: ropes drew tight, bowls slid on their pegs, and water moved through the pipes beneath the floor. Elian sat at the end of the table with a torn sleeve in his hands. He had been trying to mend it for half an hour. Each time Nell looked away, he pulled out what he had done and tried again.
  There it was again. Three slow blows, as if someone had a full fist and no need to hurry.
  “Who is working below us?” she asked.
  “No one. Anet closed the lower deck before dusk.”
  Nell laid down the knife. The bread smelled of warm grain and the sweet root Mira had ground into the flour. In a life measured by hard miles, they had come to prize such small good things. She did not want to leave the loaf. She did not want one more sound that led to one more torn wall, one more loss. Yet she could already hear the gap before the next three blows, and she knew that someone was waiting through it.
  Elian rose when she did. He set the sleeve aside without a word.
  The lower stair was cold. Its rails had been worn smooth by hands that had climbed them in fear and in joy, in the old flood and the long years after it, through all the strange routes that had brought the ark beyond the sky. Nell ran a palm down the wood. A thin mist lay on the treads. At the bottom, the light from their lamp showed a row of doors and the broad curve of the hull. The last door stood shut beneath the roots of the garden.
  It had no latch on their side.
  Elian checked the drawing he kept folded in his coat. “That was a brace chamber. No one could get in from here.”
  “Someone could be in from somewhere else.”
  He glanced at her. They had learned to fear that word, somewhere. Once it had meant a town over a hill. Now it could mean a world that had died, or a place still waiting for its first dawn.
  Nell touched the door. The blows stopped.
  “Can you hear me?” she asked.
  A single knock answered.
  Elian bent to the foot of the door. There was no gap. The wood ran flush into the sill, and the dark seams on either side looked older than the floor. He put his ear to it. Nell watched his face change.
  “What?”
  “Breathing.”
  She heard it when she leaned close. It was faint, rough, and plainly human. Then a woman's voice said, “Keep your hand there.”
  Nell's palm went cold.
  The voice sounded like hers.
  From the deck above, Ember gave a cry so sharp that dust fell from the roof. His claws struck the garden walk. A bell rang at the bow, followed by shouts and the heavy tread of running feet. Elian turned toward the stair, caught between the ship and the door. Nell knew that look. It was the cost of being trusted: every cry seemed aimed at him.
  “Go,” she said. “I'll bring Anet.”
  But the woman behind the door spoke again.
  “Don't let him take the straight channel.”
  Elian froze.
  “What straight channel?” Nell asked.
  The voice had gone. In its place came a faint scrape, and then three blows, weaker than before.
  They went up together.
  The stars were beneath the bow.
  For a moment Nell thought they had left the deck and were falling. She gripped the rail so hard a splinter sank into her thumb. The black spread below them had depth, and in it shone great loose fields of light. Above lay a pale expanse that rose and fell. It was water. It filled the whole sky, with long waves that moved without wind. A white rim ran across the distance where the water met a shore too far away to see.
  Anet stood at the helm in her night coat. Lio had one hand on a wheel spoke and the other on a line. Mira crouched by Ember, who had pressed himself flat among the garden beds. His wings were drawn tight. Flame glowed far back in his throat but did not come out.
  “There's a bank ahead,” Anet said. “Two ways through. The near one is clear.”
  “Take the long way,” Elian said.
  She looked at the wheel, then at him. “Why?”
  “We heard a warning.”
  “From whom?”
  He glanced at Nell. She hated how foolish it sounded when there was no door in sight, no breath against the wood.
  “Someone below,” she said. “Someone we haven't found yet.”
  Anet did not ask the next question. She moved Lio's hand to the outer spoke and told him to haul left. The ark leaned into the turn. The water that had seemed above them rolled across the view and became a sea beneath the hull. The stars stayed below it, burning through the deep. Nell could not decide which thing had moved: the ship, the sea, or the part of her mind that knew what down meant.
  A white fin of stone split the straight channel.
  It rose without foam. What had looked like open water folded back, and a long sharp ridge passed the place their bow would have reached. It made no sound. The ark's wash broke against it in blue sparks.
  Lio swore and braced his legs. Anet held the wheel until the ridge sank behind them. Only then did she breathe out.
  “Get that person out,” she said.
  Nell pulled the splinter from her thumb. A bead of blood came up, bright and real. She was glad of it.
  Below, the door had gone warm. Anet tried every edge, then tapped the wood with a small brass hammer. Solid. Elian fetched his tools. When he set a bit to the seam, three fast blows struck the other side.
  “No cutting,” the voice said.
  Nell knelt. “Tell us your name.”
  The pause lasted so long she could hear Anet's watch tick.
  “You won't like it yet.”
  “Tell us anyway.”
  “Bring the cup with the green chip. Put water in it. Keep it close to the wall.”
  Nell looked up at Elian. The green cup had been hers since Haven. Its chip lay beneath the handle where a thumb could hide it. The woman had not guessed some broad part of their lives. She had named a thing that lived at Nell's bedside.
  Anet laid a hand on her shoulder. “We will bring water. You can tell us how to give it to you.”
  “No,” said the voice. “It's for her. She'll forget to drink.”
  On the way to fetch the cup, Nell stopped in the galley. The loaf lay where she had left it, one slice half cut. Elian found her there with both hands on the table.
  “I know,” he said.
  “You don't.”
  “No. But I know why you're shaking.”
  She wanted to say that she was not afraid. She wanted to be the woman who could meet this with a lamp and a plan. Instead she said, “It used my voice to care for me. That may be the cruelest thing anything could do.”
  He took the cup from its hook. “Then we'll test what it says. We won't give it the right to be you.”
  Nell drank before they went back down. She made herself finish the water.
  Behind the sealed door, the woman wept once, very softly, as if she had been waiting to hear that small plain act. Then the hull gave a deep slow groan. Far ahead, beyond the long curve they had chosen, a bell answered their own.
  It was ringing from under the sea.
  

CHAPTER 2
A Shore Where No Shore Should Be
At dawn, if the pale light over the sea could be called dawn, they found a beach made of worn white wood. It stretched between two banks of stone, each plank rubbed smooth and laid in the same long curve as the waves. Some pieces still held holes for pegs. Others had names cut into them. Elian could not read the names, but he could tell that each had been made by a different hand. The beach was the ruin of many ships.
  Anet ordered the ark held off the shoal. No one went ashore until they had sounded the water, checked the small boat, and tied a return line to the main mast. Such care took time. Nell was grateful for it. The sea had saved them once through the voice, but she would not let relief become trust.
  Ember would not leave the garden.
  He watched her from beneath the fig tree, his eyes bright gold in the shade. When she went to him, he touched her coat with the soft ridge above his nose. She had seen him face storms and men with hooks, had seen him spread a torn wing so that children could crawl beneath it. Now a strip of calm water kept him pinned to the deck.
  “All right,” she told him. “You stay. Watch the roots for us.”
  He gave a low sound that she felt in her teeth.
  Mira joined her with two packs. “I put food in yours. Elian packed tools and a length of wire. He seemed to think we'd live on those.”
  “Some days we nearly have.”
  “He also packed your spare scarf.”
  Nell looked toward the rail. Elian was arguing with Lio about how much rope a boat could bear. The scarf had been dry and folded by her bed. He must have gone for it while she had been below the garden, trying to hear a breath through the wall.
  They went ashore in two trips. Anet stayed with the ark. Mira, Lio, Elian, and Nell took the beach, while two crew kept the boat in the small surf. The air smelled of salt and fresh cut leaves. Nell bent over a plank with a dark stain along one edge. It was still damp inside a split, though its face had been worn smooth as bone.
  “Don't take any,” Elian said.
  “I wasn't going to.”
  “I meant Lio.”
  Lio put down the piece in his hand. “I was checking the grain.”
  “Check it where it lies.”
  Beyond the beach was a low bank of earth. Grass grew there in thin blue lines. A path climbed through it to a shed with no roof. From inside the shed came the ring of a small bell.
  They found the bell tied to a cradle. No child lay in it. Instead there was a bowl, a wrapped loaf, and a stack of cloths cut to the same size. A woman sat on a stool beside it, stitching a tear in one of the cloths. Her hair was bound in a gray scarf. She looked up as though they had called on a neighbor.
  “You came round the east side,” she said.
  “How do you know?” Elian asked.
  “The other way kills most boats.”
  She set down the cloth and stood. Her right foot was bare. Her left was wrapped in thick felt.
  “My name is Senn. If you've brought a child, I have broth.”
  “We haven't,” Nell said. “Are you alone?”
  “Here, yes.”
  “And elsewhere?”
  Senn's face closed a little. “Elsewhere is a long answer.”
  Nell showed her the mark on her palm from the rail. “We've had a long night.”
  That won a tired smile. Senn gave them the bowl and brought another from a chest. Nell drank thin broth made from roots she did not know. It tasted of pepper and something green. She waited for Senn to drink before she took a second mouthful.
  Elian stayed near the open side of the shed. Lio walked the wall and checked each gap. Mira sat with Senn as if the offer of a stool had made them old friends. It was one of her gifts. People told Mira things they would defend to the death against a direct question.
  “Who does the bell call?” she asked.
  “The lost. They hear what they need to hear. A bell, a dog, their own child. I use a real bell so there's one true sound on the beach.”
  Nell set the bowl down.
  Senn noticed. “You've heard someone.”
  “A woman behind a door.”
  “Was she kind?”
  “Yes.”
  “That doesn't settle much.”
  Elian turned. “Can the sea speak?”
  “The sea can keep speech. That's different. You drop a stone in a pool, it holds the rings for a while. This one holds a great deal longer.”
  “Voices of the dead?”
  “Sometimes. Voices of the living, more often. And of folk who might live if a road is taken. Don't ask me how it does it. I mend cloth.”
  She took off her scarf and tied it over the cradle to block the light. Beneath it Nell saw a small carved bird. Its beak had been worn away by years of touch.
  “Why a cradle?” Nell asked.
  “My son was too large for it when I lost him. I kept it anyway. Now people see it and know they may stop.”
  Nell did not ask whether the sea had offered Senn her son back. The answer sat in the woman's face.
  A deep shape passed beneath the bay. The shed darkened, though nothing crossed the light above it. Lio came in fast.
  “There's a ship in the water.”
  “On it?” Elian asked.
  “In it. Something is carrying it.”
  They went to the bank. Through the clear blue water Nell saw a long curve of pale hide, ridged like a shell. On its back lay a vessel, tipped to one side, its broken ribs caught in thick cords that ran into the creature's flesh. A deck garden shone green beneath the surface. Glass roofs still held air. As the shape turned, a tall stern rose through the water without a splash.
  The bell in the cradle rang by itself.
  Senn gripped Nell's arm. “Do not step aboard because you see a face you love.”
  “Someone may need help.”
  “Of course someone needs help. That's how it catches the decent ones.”
  The wreck came to rest beyond the shoal. A narrow part of its deck lay dry. On the stern, above a scar where a name had been cut away, someone had hung a white cloth. Four dark strokes marked its center.
  Elian went very still. Nell knew the strokes. They were the sign he used on plans when a brace could bear no more load. He had made it as a boy, an error in a copied mark that had become his own.
  “Could someone have learned that?” she asked.
  “Yes.”
  “Could it be yours?”
  “Yes.”
  Senn let go of her arm. She looked older now, as if she had just watched them start a walk she had seen too often.
  “Come back before the low bell,” she said. “Leave a person on the boat who has not heard the voice. And if it offers you a whole lost life, ask who will be made to carry it.”
  Across the bay, Ember cried again. This time he stood at the bow, wings spread, his gaze fixed on the pale shape under the wreck.
  It turned one vast blind eye toward him.
  For the first time since the crossing, Nell heard the dragon answer with a sound that held no fear. It was grief.
  

CHAPTER 3
The Table Set for Four
The wreck had steps built into its side. They rose out of the water in a curve, clean at the top and thick with white growth below. Elian tied the boat to a post that moved under his hand. He tested it twice, then chose another. Lio watched him with the grave care of a man who had once paid for haste and would not pay again.
  “Ten minutes on the deck,” Nell said. “Then we come back and tell Anet what we find.”
  “Even if we hear a child?” Mira asked.
  “We mark the place and fetch help.”
  “Good. Say that again when it happens.”
  They climbed. Nell felt the wreck move beneath her feet, a slow rise that was not quite the motion of a boat. Through a split in the rail she saw the pale back below. The creature was breathing. Each breath lifted the whole vessel by the width of her hand.
  A rope ladder led through a hatch. Beside it, dry in a little box, lay a pair of boots. Nell's size. The left heel had been worn low at the outside, as hers always wore. She did not touch them.
  The deck garden had gone wild. Bean stalks wound through a broken chair. Small white fruit hung in clusters from a tree whose roots had split the planks. Under the glass roof, rain ran down the inside of each pane, though the air was dry. A door stood open at the far end.
  They found the table beyond it.
  Four bowls. Four cups. A pot with a clean cloth over its lid. Bread cut into thick slices. Beside one bowl lay a needle stuck through a torn sleeve.
  Elian touched his own arm.
  Nell put a hand between him and the table. “Don't.”
  He stopped, but his eyes stayed on the sleeve. The tear was the same shape as the one he had tried to mend in the galley. The stitch he had pulled out three times ran in a crooked line along its edge.
  Mira looked into the pot. “Soup. Still warm.”
  “Leave it.”
  “I am leaving it. I want to know how far this goes.”
  On the chair at the head of the table lay a book. Its cover had been wrapped in dark cloth. Elian used the end of a tool to open it. The first page held a list of stores: meal, beans, salt, lamp oil, clean linen. Nell recognized her own hand in the small round letters. There was the way she crossed a number seven. There was the line she drew twice beneath a total when she did not trust her sum.
  She turned a page with the tip of her knife.
  They had come by the east bank. They had met a woman named Senn. They had heard a bell on the beach and found a wreck on the back of a living thing. Each event lay in the book, told in brief notes, with all the dull details no tale would need to invent. Mira had left a blue thread on the shed stool. Lio had put down a piece of wood. Nell had burned her tongue on the broth and said nothing.
  “How?” Lio whispered.
  The next page was blank. Nell let out the breath she had held.
  Then she saw that two pages had stuck together.
  Elian reached for the edge. This time she did not stop him. The paper came apart with a dry sigh. On the inner leaf, written so hard the nib had torn the grain, was one line.
  This time, do not let us open the door.
  Below it a dark thumb mark spread across the margin. It could have been ink. Nell knew it was not.
  Something struck the floor beneath the table.
  Mira backed toward the open door. Lio lifted the boat hook he had brought. Elian took Nell's wrist, and she felt him find her pulse with his thumb. It was an old habit, a way of asking without words whether she was still here with him. She turned her hand and held his.
  The blow came again. Not three knocks. One heavy thud, followed by a scrape.
  “Under the deck,” Lio said.
  A panel near the hearth rose an inch. Two small fingers pushed through the gap.
  Mira was across the room before Nell could speak. She dropped to her knees and caught the panel. Lio helped her lift. A child crawled out, coughing, with a strip of dark cloth tied over his eyes. He could not have been more than ten. His hands were cut and his shirt clung to him with cold sweat.
  “No one eat,” he gasped. “Please.”
  Nell knelt beside him. “We haven't. What's your name?”
  “Pell.”
  “Can I take that cloth off?”
  “No. He'll see where I am.”
  “Who will?”
  “The man who keeps the ship.”
  Elian went to the door. The garden beyond was empty. Yet the glass had begun to darken from the top, as if the sea were rising over it.
  “Time to go.”
  Pell gripped Mira's coat. “My sister.”
  “Where?” she asked.
  “The pens. Down two decks. She won't leave the animals.”
  Nell looked at the little fingers, the real blood under their nails. Senn had warned them about a loved face. This was a stranger. That did not make him safe, but it made their choice plain.
  “Boat first,” she said. “Then we come with enough hands to bring them all.”
  Pell shook his head so hard the knot slipped at his neck. “The low bell. He shuts the pens at the low bell.”
  From somewhere deep in the wreck came a note that seemed to bend the wood. It had not yet reached its full sound.
  Mira looked at Nell. Nell heard her own rule spoken back to her: mark the place and fetch help. She hated the weight of those words now. Rules were easy when the danger had no face.
  Elian tore a blank leaf from the book and wrote in his broad rough hand. He gave it to Lio. “Take Pell. Tell Anet. Tie another line to the stern. If we don't come up, don't pull the ark in.”
  Lio did not move. “You need me.”
  “We need you to get him back.”
  Pell reached out for Nell's sleeve. “Her name is June. Tell her Pell got out. She won't believe you unless you say the blue goat bit the bell.”
  Nell repeated the words. He nodded and let go.
  Lio lifted him, careful of the thin ribs, and went. Mira had already found the stair. Elian tucked the journal into a dry pouch. Nell wanted to tell him to leave it. She also wanted to hold it herself until she understood each word.
  At the foot of the first stair, a line of lamps lit without flame.
  A man stood beside the next flight. He wore a plain dark coat and held a towel over one arm. His hair was white at the temples. Nothing in his face looked cruel.
  “You've found the boy,” he said. “Thank you. He gets lost when the tide turns.”
  Elian kept his tool low at his side. “We're looking for June.”
  The man folded the towel.
  “Of course you are.”
  Above them, the open door clicked shut. The sound was soft, almost polite.
  

CHAPTER 4
The Pens Beneath the Water
“My name is Vale,” the man said. “I look after the passengers.”
  “Open the upper door,” Nell told him.
  “It opens on the turn of the tide.”
  “Open it now.”
  Vale looked at her for a long moment. Then he took a small key from his pocket and hung it on a nail beside the stair. “When you go up, you may use this. I would rather you stayed for the meal.”
  He stepped back. Elian took the key and tried it in a small lock set into the wall. A rod shifted above them. From the top of the stairs came the creak of the door opening.
  “You see?” Vale said.
  Nell saw only that he wanted them to trust him after making them ask. “Where are the pens?”
  “Below. The girl is quite safe.”
  “Then you won't mind us seeing her.”
  “I'll show you the way.”
  “No,” Mira said. “You can walk behind us.”
  For the first time, a small crack showed in his calm. He glanced at the water mark on the wall, then at the stair. Nell stored that glance away. Whatever he could do, there was something down here he feared.
  The pens filled a long hall beneath the water. Nell could see the sea through thick panes set into the hull, and beyond it the great curved back that bore the wreck. Cords as thick as masts ran from iron collars through the floor. Each time the creature breathed, the cords grew taut and the lamps dimmed.
  Animals watched from low stalls. Some were like goats, except for their soft blue coats and split ears. Others had shells on their backs and long clever hands. A bird with four small wings clung to a perch beside a cracked bowl. It opened its beak as Nell passed and made the sound of a crying baby.
  At the far end, a girl was trying to lift a fallen gate. She had braced one shoulder beneath it. A blue goat stood on the other side, chewing the hem of her coat.
  “June,” Nell called.
  The girl flinched and let the gate fall. “Don't come near.”
  “Pell got out. He's on our boat. He said the blue goat bit the bell.”
  June began to cry. She wiped her face with both wrists and looked angry with herself. “He was meant to wait.”
  “He's safe,” Mira said. “We're taking you to him.”
  “And these?”
  “All we can carry.”
  Vale came to the foot of the stair. “The animals require this climate. They won't survive on your vessel.”
  June spat at his shoes.
  He did not step away. “I understand your distress.”
  “No, you don't. You keep saying that.”
  Nell went to the gate. Its hinge had bent. Elian knelt beside it and laid out his tools, his face taking on the stillness she knew from a beam about to fall. Mira moved down the row, checking latches and counting small heads.
  “What is carrying this ship?” Nell asked Vale.
  “A shore bearer.”
  “Does it have a name?”
  “Not one we can speak.”
  “Then why is it bound?”
  “To keep it on the route. There are people in need of rescue across this whole sea.”
  “Did it agree?”
  Vale looked at her as if she had asked whether a wheel wished to turn. Then his gaze dropped to the pale curve outside the glass.
  “It used to come when called.”
  Elian drove a peg from the hinge. The gate sagged. June caught the goat before it could bolt. She whispered into its long ear, and it pressed its face under her chin.
  Nell saw a scar on the girl's wrist. It formed a clean ring, thin and white. Pell had worn the same mark.
  “What were the children bound to?” she asked.
  Vale's mouth tightened. “No one is bound here.”
  June pulled back her sleeve. “Then what was this for?”
  “It kept you from being lost.”
  “It kept me from the boat.”
  A bell rang below them. The low note rolled through Nell's chest. The water outside rose across the nearest pane. A door at the far end of the hall began to slide down from its frame.
  Vale moved for a wheel set into the wall. Elian caught his arm.
  “Stop it.”
  “I can't hold it with my hands.”
  “Then tell us how.”
  “The wheel. Turn it left and keep it there.”
  Elian let go. Vale seized the wheel and strained. The door slowed, but did not stop. Water seeped under the floor boards. Nell took hold beside him. His hand was cold, his skin rough with the same work scars as any man who had spent years at a helm.
  “Mira, June, up!” she shouted.
  The girl refused to leave until Mira had slung three carriers over her shoulders. The four-winged bird flapped around her head. June led two goats and held a pouch full of small shell-backed creatures against her chest. Elian shoved a block under the falling door, then pulled a bar from the empty gate and wedged it beneath the frame.
  The bar bent.
  Water burst through the seam behind Vale. He looked at Nell. For one bare instant she saw a man who knew he had made a bad choice and had spent too long making it seem good.
  “Go,” he said.
  She kept her hands on the wheel. “You too.”
  “If I let go, the upper hatch seals.”
  Elian took a length of chain from the broken gate. He passed it through the spokes, pulled it tight round a pipe, and drove his tool between two links. The wheel held.
  Vale stared at it.
  “Now,” Elian said.
  They ran. Behind them the bar snapped. The door hit the block, splintered it, and struck the floor with a force that sent water up the stairs. Nell caught June's elbow as they reached the deck. The garden roof was half under the sea. Beyond it, their boat strained against the line, Lio hauling it close with a hook.
  Mira jumped first. June passed the goat, then the pouch, then herself. Nell turned for Vale.
  He had stopped at the open door. One foot stood in the sea that washed across the deck. He looked down at it as though it were a height he could not cross.
  “Come on,” she said.
  “I belong here.”
  “You can decide that on our boat.”
  Elian reached past her and caught his sleeve. Vale struck his hand away with sudden force. Then he stepped back into the wreck and shut the door.
  They had no time to argue. Lio cut the stern line. Anet's crew hauled from the ark, and their boat slid over the rising curve of the wreck's rail. Beneath them, the shore bearer turned. Nell saw one iron collar tear at the pale flesh of its back.
  Ember's cry crossed the water. The beast answered with a moan so deep that Nell felt it through the soles of her feet.
  June clung to Mira, shaking.
  “He'll come for us,” she said.
  Elian held the dry pouch with the journal against his chest. “Let him.”
  But Nell was watching the sea. The wreck was gone, and in its place lay the smooth bright water, as if they had imagined the whole thing. Only the iron key in Elian's palm proved that a door had ever opened there.
  

CHAPTER 5
What the Children Knew
Pell would not take the cloth from his eyes until June put his hand on the blue goat. He felt the split in its ear, the blunt horn, the patch of stiff hair above its nose. Then he pulled the knot loose and looked at his sister. They held each other without speech. Nell set two bowls near them and left the galley door open.
  The ark's crew made room in the way people do when they have once been the ones who needed it. A bench became a bed. A torn sheet became fresh strips for Pell's hands. Mira moved the small creatures to the warm pipe room. Lio built a low pen from spare slats and pretended not to mind when the goat ate part of his plan.
  Anet called a watch meeting beneath the garden. She did not let the whole crew crowd the sealed door. Two at a time could come and hear what they needed to hear. No one was to ask it about a dead loved one. No one was to promise it a thing in private. She said these rules in the same voice she used for fire drills, which made them bearable.
  The woman inside did not speak.
  Elian set the journal on a clean board. He had dried its outer cloth, but the pages were as dry as if they had never seen the sea. Nell watched him spread the leaf bearing the warning. She had begun to hate her own handwriting. It made each mark feel like a betrayal she had somehow committed in her sleep.
  “We need facts we can check,” Anet said. “What did it know before we knew it?”
  “The ridge in the channel,” Elian said. “The cup.”
  “The cup is a fact about us. The ridge was a fact about the route. Keep them apart.”
  Nell nodded. Anet had no patience for a mystery that made people stop thinking. She laid three plain stones on the board: what they had seen, what they had been told, what they had guessed. They used the stones to hold down separate lists. Soon the middle one was longest.
  When June came to the meeting, she stood in the doorway with Pell behind her. She would not sit where she could not see the stair.
  “Vale calls the wreck Mercy,” she said. “He says it was built to save people from lost roads.”
  “Is it his ship?” Anet asked.
  “No. He found it. Like he found us.”
  “Where?”
  June glanced at Pell. “A place with no sky. We'd gone through a gate with our mother. It shut between us. There was a room and water under the floor. We waited a long time.”
  “How long?”
  “Pell lost a tooth. It grew half back.”
  Nell looked at the boy. He showed a small white point beside a gap. There was no way to weigh time by it, but there was a child's whole season of waiting.
  “Vale fed us,” June said. “He brought light. He knew places to find things. For a while I thought he was good.”
  “He may have done good things,” Mira said.
  June's face hardened. “He put the rings on when we asked to leave.”
  No one tried to soften that.
  They learned that Mercy came and went along a route marked by bells. At the low bell, the lower decks filled. At the high bell, the dry rooms rose. Vale could change some of the doors, but not the route itself. He fed the shore bearer through pipes in the floor, and he cut light from the walls to keep the lamps alive. When people came aboard, the ship made rooms for them. Some stayed because they were hurt. Some stayed because they found things they had lost.
  “What things?” Elian asked.
  “A wedding coat. A dog. A garden. One man said his wife was in the next room. We never saw her. He stopped coming to meals.”
  Nell turned toward the sealed door. Nothing moved there.
  Pell had gone pale. “It learns what you listen for,” he said.
  “Did you hear someone?”
  He nodded. “Our mother. She told me June was tired of me. Said I should come where she was.”
  June seized his hand. “I never was.”
  “I know. You told me after.”
  Mira asked who had taught them the paths between the decks. June shook her head, but Pell looked at the floor.
  “A woman,” he said.
  Nell kept her voice level. “What woman?”
  “She came through the dark store. She had a lamp tied to her wrist. She couldn't stay long. She said if we saw a man with a torn sleeve, we should trust the woman with him.”
  Elian sat down.
  “Did she give her name?”
  “No. She gave us bread. Real bread. The ship's food makes you sleepy if you eat too much. Hers didn't.”
  Nell thought of the half-cut loaf in the galley. She had left it there all night. Mira had wrapped it at dawn, but a heel had been missing. At the time, Nell had assumed that someone on watch had taken it.
  She did not say this at the meeting. It felt too much like a trap built from small familiar acts.
  Afterward, she found Elian at the workbench. He had laid the torn sleeve beside the journal. He took a pair of shears and cut a square from the cloth, clear through the line of failed stitches.
  “What are you doing?”
  “Making it different.”
  He handed her the square. In the wreck, the sleeve had been whole except for the tear. Here it now had a clean hole. She could see how badly he needed that fact. She folded the scrap and put it in her pocket.
  “We can change our minds,” he said.
  “Yes.”
  “And our work.”
  “Yes.”
  “Then this isn't done yet.”
  She laid her head against his shoulder. For a while they stood with no task between them. His hand rested on her back. A goat bleated above them, and someone laughed at Lio's attempt to make it stop. The ordinary sound gave Nell a sudden ache so sharp she had to shut her eyes.
  “I want a morning when nothing wants us,” she said.
  “I could manage a whole morning.”
  “Don't boast.”
  He kissed her temple. She felt the smile before she saw it.
  That night she took the green cup below the garden. She put it against the wall and waited. When the voice came, it was barely more than a breath.
  “Are the children warm?”
  “Yes.”
  “June's left shoe rubs. There's a nail through the sole.”
  “I'll look.”
  “Thank you.”
  Nell sat on the floor. “Pell said a woman brought them bread. Was that you?”
  Silence.
  “Are you me?”
  The wall gave a small creak.
  “I am not the sea,” said the woman. “Whatever else you think, keep that.”
  Nell pressed the scrap of sleeve between her fingers. “How do I know?”
  “You don't. You should make me earn it.”
  “Then tell me how to open this room.”
  “No.”
  “Are you hurt?”
  “Yes.”
  The plain answer struck harder than a cry. Nell leaned her forehead against the wood.
  “Tell me what you need.”
  A long time passed. Above them, the garden pipes knocked as someone drew water.
  “Don't let love become a reason to stop asking who gets hurt,” the voice said. “I did that once. I'm trying to put it right.”
  Nell stayed until the lamp burned low. She received no name, no map, no promise. But when she rose, there was a small damp patch at the foot of the door. It smelled of salt. In it lay a single white feather, bent near the quill.
  

CHAPTER 6
The Map That Refused the Wall
They built the first map on the galley floor because the sea would not stay on paper. Lio tried to draw the shore as it looked from the bow. Each line was true for a moment. Then a bank slid past another bank, a channel narrowed behind a stand of reeds, and the ridge they had missed at the crossing appeared far to the west. He tore up three sheets before Anet told him to stop drawing edges and start drawing bells.
  Bells held their places. They tested this for most of a day. A small high bell rang near Senn's beach. A deeper one sounded from the wreck. Farther out, a pair rang in turns, one clear and one cracked. Each note seemed to come through the hull as much as the air. Elian set bowls of water across the deck. At each bell, a different bowl formed rings first.
  By dusk they had a map of sounds, with stones for places they had seen and beans for places they had only heard. June moved one bean away from a cluster.
  “That one isn't a shore,” she said. “It's a mouth.”
  Lio looked at her.
  “A way through,” she added. “Vale said that. I don't mean teeth.”
  “Here, I like to be sure.”
  Pell grinned for the first time. Lio pretended not to see it.
  Before they tried the paired bells, Anet asked each pair to rehearse on the lower deck. It sounded foolish with a wall at one side and the familiar stairs at the other. Nell tied her line to Elian's belt and shut her eyes. He called from three places while she kept one hand on a rail. At the fourth call, he said her name in the tone he used when he was hurt. She turned so fast she struck her knee on a bench.
  Anet stopped them.
  “That is why we practice here.”
  Nell rubbed her knee. She wanted to be angry with him for using that voice. Then she saw that his face had changed too. He had heard how quickly she would follow it.
  “What do you need me to say?” he asked.
  “Something I can do. Not just my name.”
  They tried again. This time he said, “Nell, keep your hand on the rail. Tell me which foot is forward.” She did. He asked her to take one step toward his voice, then stop. By the time she opened her eyes, the fear in his tone no longer seemed like a hook through her chest. It was still hard to hear. It was a thing she could answer with work.
  They changed places. Elian shut his eyes. Nell walked to the far end of the passage and spoke as if she had been crying. He caught the rail with both hands. She saw how much effort it took him not to come.
  “Name the room,” she said.
  “The lower passage.”
  “Name what keeps you here.”
  “The line. My hands. Your rule.”
  “Our rule,” she corrected.
  He nodded without opening his eyes. “Our rule.”
  Around them, the other pairs practiced. Some laughed too loudly. One woman asked to stop because the sound of distress brought back a day she could not yet speak about. Anet gave her a task with no voices in it: keep the time, mark each turn, watch for a loose knot. No one made her explain why.
  Iven had paired with Lio. At first they spoke only the words Anet had given them. Then Lio shut his eyes and said, “If I go toward something wrong, you pull.”
  “I will.”
  “Don't wait for me to deserve it.”
  Iven looked down at the rope. “I won't.”
  Nell turned away before either man saw her watching. Trust could be rebuilt in public decisions and records. It could also begin with a knot no one else would ever need to see.
  After the rehearsal, she went to check the animals. The shell-backed creatures had learned the warm pipe's shape and lay along it in a neat row. One opened its little hand when she came near. She offered a bit of softened grain. It took the food, turned it over twice, and gave half back. Nell laughed. She had spent the day preparing for a sea that could use love against them. Here was a strange small life with no knowledge of her past, offering her a wet crumb.
  Mira found her still crouched there.
  “You need a task that ends,” Mira said.
  “I have one. Feed these.”
  “Good. Finish it. Then leave them fed. You don't need to keep proving it.”
  Nell emptied the bowl, checked the water, and stood. The creatures settled without her. Above, Elian had left her scarf folded by the chart crate. She put it on and went to the helm.
  The practice had not made them ready for everything. It had given them a few acts their bodies could remember when fear made speech hard. As the ark turned toward the dark belt, Nell set her hand on the rail and named the foot she put forward.
  The nearest paired bells lay beyond a belt of dark water. Ember went to the rail whenever they rang, then drew back when the ark turned toward them. Mira found that he would eat if she set his bowl on earth, but not if she set it on bare deck. Nell took a handful of garden soil and spread it near his claws. He lowered his head into it as if the scent could hold him still.
  Elian crouched at his side. “What is down there?”
  Ember looked toward the sea. A deep gold line moved in his throat. Then he opened one wing and laid it across the garden path. The old scars in its skin glowed with the light from below.
  Nell had seen those scars made. Hooks in a yard. Fire in rain. A flight no living creature should have survived. She touched the ridge of one, and Ember gave a quiet rumble.
  “He knows a hurt,” she said. “That doesn't mean he knows a route.”
  Elian nodded. He had been leaning too hard on the dragon's gaze, hoping fear might serve as a chart. He stood and went to help Anet.
  Nell found Iven in the store room, counting rope. Since his betrayal and the inquiry that followed, he had taken every dull task offered to him. He did not ask to be forgiven. At times that made Nell angrier than a plea would have done. At others it gave her room to work beside him.
  “Anet wants a second return line,” she said.
  “I've cut it.”
  “Then why are you still counting?”
  “Because I counted wrong last time.”
  He set one coil aside and marked it with red thread. His hands were steady. Nell watched them a moment before she spoke.
  “If a voice knew everything you regretted, what would it offer you?”
  He did not look up. “A chance to explain.”
  “Would you take it?”
  “Once, yes. Now I'd ask who had to sit and listen.”
  She picked up the coil. He had given her more than she had meant to ask.
  They reached the dark belt near the middle watch. The water there did not show the stars. It lay dull and thick, with pale shapes just beneath the skin. Anet had ordered no one to stand alone. The crew worked in pairs, each pair tied by a short line at the waist. The ark moved at the speed of a walking child.
  Nell heard her mother call from the port side.
  It was not a word, only the small sound her mother had used when lifting a hot pot, half warning and half request for help. Nell had not heard it in years. She turned before she knew she had moved. The line at her waist drew tight. Elian stood at the other end.
  “Tell me what you hear,” he said.
  She did. Saying it aloud broke part of its pull.
  “What do you hear?” she asked.
  He swallowed. “You. Telling me you never wanted this life.”
  Nell stepped close and put his hand on her face. “I wanted you. Some days I hate the road. You know the difference.”
  “I do.”
  “Say it.”
  “I know the difference.”
  The water struck the hull in a long flat slap. All down the deck, people were speaking to their partners, naming voices, naming griefs. One crewman sat down and covered his ears. Mira sat beside him. Iven took the line he had dropped and held both ends until the man could rise.
  At the bow, Pell began to hum. June joined him, then Lio. It was a rough little tune the children had made while trapped on Mercy. No words, just a rise and fall too odd to blend with any voice in the water. Soon the whole deck was humming. The ark went forward through a wall of remembered speech, bearing one small song no lost life had ever heard.
  Then the cracked bell rang.
  The dark water thinned. Ahead, three low islands drifted round a tower. The tower had no base. Its lower stones hung above the sea, dry and bare, while long roots reached down from its roof. Bridges of rope and wood joined the islands to it. Lamps moved on the bridges. Real people, Nell thought, and was ashamed of how much she needed that word.
  A boat came out to meet them. At its bow stood a broad woman with a shaved head and a red scarf tied round one wrist. She held both hands where they could see them.
  “Keep your song going until the stern clears,” she called. “Then you may stop. Don't stop for me.”
  Anet obeyed. When the ark passed the last dark strip, she raised a hand and the humming fell away.
  The woman brought her boat alongside. “I'm Rusk. This is the Bell Reach. If you're sick, we have beds. If you're armed, keep it sheathed. If you've brought a promise from Vale, tear it up before you land.”
  Nell and Elian looked at each other.
  “We brought his key,” Elian said.
  Rusk's gaze went to his palm. The change in her face was so swift that Nell felt the whole sea tilt again.
  “Then hide it,” she said. “And tell me whether he let you take it.”
  From under the garden came three soft knocks.
  This time, everyone on the deck heard them.
  

CHAPTER 7
The People Between Bells
Rusk would not cross the ark's rail until Anet told her she could leave at any time. Even then, she put a strip of red cloth on the deck before stepping over it. Nell had begun to see how the people of this sea made small rules to hold large fears. She did not ask about the cloth until they were in the galley.
  “It marks where I chose to come aboard,” Rusk said. “In case the ship tries to make me think I've always been here.”
  “Can it do that?”
  “Mercy can. Your ark, I don't know.”
  “Ours doesn't,” Elian said.
  Rusk looked at the floor. The door lay two decks below them. “I hope you stay right.”
  They showed her the key but not the journal. She turned the iron with the edge of her sleeve. There were four teeth, one filed shorter than the rest. A thin white strand clung near the bow. Rusk held it to the light and pulled her hand back.
  “Keep it off skin.”
  Elian set it in a bowl. “What is it?”
  “A root from the keep. The key tells the old doors who carries it.”
  “So Vale can find us,” Anet said.
  “He could find you without it. This may let him enter.”
  Lio rose at once. Anet sent him to check the hatches, with Iven and two others. Rusk watched Iven take the order. She seemed to see his place among them without being told.
  “Don't make a guilty man your only guard,” she said quietly. “He'll miss danger while trying to earn the post.”
  “We have more than one,” Anet answered.
  They put the key in a glass jar with salt and tied the lid. Rusk did not promise that would help. Nell liked her better for it.
  The Bell Reach smelled of smoke, wet rope, and hot fat. It was the first crowded place they had seen since the crossing. Children ran across bridges in pairs. A woman sharpened blades beside a stall of green fruit. Men patched a roof made from sailcloth while below them someone argued about the price of soap. The sight made Nell want to weep. These people had built a day out of things that might have drowned them.
  Rusk gave them a berth by the smallest island. Water barrels stood there under a locked awning. Everyone could draw enough to drink, but washing was counted. Firewood was counted too. No one asked where the ark had come from until they had eaten. Then the questions came in a rush.
  Was there still a ground beneath the sky? Did trees grow without lamps? Had the flood reached all lands? Did time still pass the same way there? Could a man go home if his home had slipped out of the route?
  Elian answered only what he knew. Nell saw how each honest limit hurt. People had not gathered for a lesson in doubt. They wanted a door.
  One old man held out a ring. “My wife had one like it. If I give this to you, will you look?”
  Nell closed his fingers over it. “Tell us her name. Keep your ring.”
  “Dessa.”
  She wrote it down. The old man watched until she had spelled it twice to his liking. Then he left, and three others took his place.
  By the time Rusk came back, Nell's paper was full of names.
  “That is how Vale began,” Rusk said.
  Nell looked up sharply.
  “He wrote them down. At first, he really searched.”
  “What changed?”
  “He found a way to give people what looked like an answer. It was easier than saying he'd failed.”
  “Are they false?”
  “Some. Some are people pulled from a road that never quite became real here. Some are only speech in a wall. There are folk I trust who were found by him. I won't tell you they don't count. But the keep pays for each door with power from the shore bearers. And when that fails, it takes from the people who asked.”
  Nell thought of the rings on the children's wrists.
  “What does it take?”
  “Years. Strength. Memory. A little at a time. Enough that you keep choosing it because you've forgotten what you've paid.”
  Elian had gone still beside her. Nell knew the weight of his old repairs, the power drawn to save one place and lost by another. She laid a hand over his wrist, but he did not turn.
  “Where is the keep?” he asked.
  “Beyond the paired bells. You won't reach it by sailing straight.”
  “Does Mercy go there?”
  “Every third low tide.”
  “Then we have a route.”
  Rusk leaned close. “You have a thing that wants to be followed. That's not the same.”
  That evening, she took them to the tower. Its stairs were worn in the center. At the top hung two bells, each large enough for Nell to stand beneath. One had a crack from lip to crown, held shut by a band of wood. The other was plain and whole. Rusk touched the cracked one with her palm.
  “This one calls the lost. The whole one calls them back from the call. We ring them in turns so no one gets drawn too far.”
  “What made the crack?”
  “A man who thought he could make the sea quiet by breaking the thing that warned us of it.”
  Outside the open wall, the islands turned. Nell saw Ember on the ark's deck, a small dark shape against the garden. He had raised his head toward the tower. Under him, the vast sea shone through stars.
  Elian stood at the edge, holding the paper of names. “We can't take everyone.”
  “No,” Nell said.
  “And if we offer a ride, more will come than the ark can feed.”
  “Yes.”
  He looked at her. “You're going to ask anyway.”
  “I'm going to ask Anet what we can bear. Then I'm going to tell the truth.”
  They had their first quarrel on the stair, quietly, out of Rusk's hearing. Elian thought each new promise would bind them to Vale's road. Nell thought fear of being used could become an excuse to leave people behind. They were both tired. Each could name a past loss that proved the other wrong.
  Then someone below called for a bucket. Smoke rose past the stair.
  On the smallest island, by the locked water awning, a lamp had overturned. Fire ran along the dry edge of a roof. Between it and the bridge stood the old man with the ring, unable to move fast enough.
  Elian was down the stairs before Nell had finished calling his name. She followed, and the quarrel went with them, still unsolved, into work that could not wait.
  

CHAPTER 8
The Fire on the Floating Island
The bridge had no rail. Elian crossed it with a bucket in each hand, eyes fixed on the far post. Beneath the slats, water rose and fell in long slow breaths. He could hear the fire ahead and the bells above, both ringing now without a pattern. A roof beam fell, and sparks spread over the narrow path between the stalls.
  “Don't run against the crowd,” he shouted.
  No one could hear him. He put down the buckets and caught a boy trying to drag a chest onto the bridge. The chest had jammed between two posts. People pressed behind him. Elian took the boy's face between both hands.
  “Leave it. Go to the tower. Find the woman with the red scarf.”
  “My father's tools.”
  “I'll mark the place.”
  He used a piece of chalk from his coat to draw a cross on the post. The boy watched, then let go. Elian shoved the chest aside, and the line moved. Nell reached him with three crew from the ark. She passed buckets down, setting a chain from the water to the flames.
  The old man stood in a doorway across the lane. Smoke hid his knees. Nell started toward him, but a gust of heat pushed her back. Elian saw the shape of the roof, the sag where a brace had burned through, and the narrow strip of ground that might hold long enough.
  “Stay by the water,” he told her.
  “Don't give me a last order.”
  He looked at her. She was furious, and afraid, and right. He had said it like a man stepping out of a life he had no right to leave behind.
  “Watch that beam,” he said instead. “Tell me when it moves.”
  “I will.”
  He tied a wet cloth over his mouth and crossed. The heat struck the side of his face. He found the old man's hand and put it on his shoulder. The man tried to turn back for a small box on a shelf.
  “Dessa's letters.”
  Elian took the box. It was light. He tucked it into the man's coat and bent beneath his arm.
  “Now walk with me.”
  Halfway back, Nell cried out. The beam rolled in its charred notch. Elian threw the old man forward. Hands caught him from the lane. Elian fell against the wall as burning wood struck where his legs had been. A hot nail pierced his boot. He pulled free and crawled through the gap beneath the fallen beam.
  Nell dragged him the last few feet. She hit his shoulder once, hard, then held his face until his eyes met hers.
  “You tell me when you're hurt,” she said.
  “My foot.”
  “Good. Stay.”
  He stayed, hating it. The bucket chain grew. Mira brought wet sheets from the ark. Iven climbed a stall frame and cut the burning roof cloth loose before it could carry the fire to the next row. A pole slipped under him. He caught a rope, swung out over the water, and hung there with one arm while the cloth fell in a sheet of flame.
  Lio went along the frame on his belly. He caught Iven by the belt and hauled until both men rolled onto the path. They lay side by side, coughing.
  “Your count was right,” Lio said.
  “What?”
  “The rope. Long enough.”
  Iven laughed once, with no breath in it.
  The fire would still have taken the awning if Ember had not come. He landed on the end of the bridge, wings beating hard against the smoke. People cried out and drew back. His claws gripped the posts. For a moment Elian thought the whole span would tear free beneath his weight.
  Then the dragon folded his wings and crawled forward.
  He did not breathe flame. He hooked a burning beam with one claw, took the other end in his jaws, and dragged it into the sea. Steam burst up around his face. He drew back with a hiss, then reached for the next beam. The crowd saw what he was doing. Men and women came with poles to roll the smaller wood toward him.
  By the time the last roof fell, they had made a wet gap across the island. The fire burned within it and could go no farther.
  Nell bound Elian's foot in the tower store room. The nail had passed through the sole and cut deep beside his heel. She washed the wound while he gripped the bench. Neither spoke of the quarrel. When she tied the final strip, he touched her wrist.
  “You were right about the order.”
  “You were right about the promises.”
  “That doesn't solve it.”
  “No.”
  She sat beside him. Through the open door they could see people sorting what had survived. The boy found his father's chest. The old man held the little box of letters on his lap. Some stalls were gone. A woman stared at a patch of ash where her bed had been. Nell knew that saving lives did not make the rest of the loss small.
  Rusk came with a list. Two people badly burned. Several with cuts and smoke in their lungs. No one missing after the final count. She read each name aloud, and Elian watched her finger move down the page as though he could keep it from stopping.
  “Your dragon saved the west row,” she said.
  “He chose to come,” Nell answered.
  “I saw.”
  Rusk set a sack beside them. “Oats, salt, and clean wraps. We had set them aside to pay for passage. Use them for the people you're taking.”
  “We haven't agreed how many.”
  “Then agree. Tell us plain. The fire has made the question urgent.”
  Anet held the meeting at dusk. She put stores, floor space, water use, and the needs of the injured before the whole Reach. The ark could take eighteen passengers at once without putting those already aboard at risk. More could travel if the Reach supplied a tow barge and kept a share of the food. The crossing to a safe shore would take an unknown span of days. No one would be promised a lost home.
  People argued. Some called the number cruel. Others offered food they did not have. Nell stood beside Anet and did not raise it to ease the pain in the room. Elian saw what that cost her.
  In the end, they chose the hurt, the children without carers, and the people whose beds had burned. Others would stay and help make the barge. Rusk asked for witnesses to every name and every sack put aboard. Their plan grew slowly, out of public words that could be checked.
  Later, Nell found a new line in the journal. It had not been there at noon. The ink was blue, unlike the old brown of the warning.
  We saved the west row because he learned to crawl.
  She went below the garden and held the open page against the wall.
  “Did you write this?”
  From behind the door came a tired laugh.
  “No. You will.”
  Nell shut the book.
  Above, Ember lay with his scorched claws in a tub of cool water, while Pell read him a story from a book held upside down.
  

CHAPTER 9
The Orchard That Drank Starlight
The barge needed fresh timber, and the Reach had none thick enough for a sound tow beam. Rusk knew an orchard where trees grew in the shapes of ribs and braces. It lay beyond the whole bell, out of sight when the tide was high. The keep had once tended it. Now no one claimed it, and those who cut there took no more than the dead growth. Rusk said the living roots could pull a man under if he hurt them.
  Elian looked at his bound foot and said nothing. Nell knew he meant to go.
  “You can work from the boat,” she told him.
  “I was about to suggest that.”
  “No, you weren't.”
  He accepted the small stool she gave him. It was the closest either came to a victory.
  They left at the turn of the bell with Lio, June, Rusk, and four of the Reach's woodworkers. Nell had asked June to stay with Pell. The girl had replied that she knew the orchard's water gate and Pell had asked her to go. Nell checked with him. He had given his sister a crust wrapped in cloth and told her to come back with both shoes.
  The island rose slowly through a veil of pale mist. Its trees had silver bark and leaves dark as wet glass. Under each branch hung a web of fine roots that reached toward the water. Light ran up them in small pulses. Nell could see stars beneath the island, dimmer where the roots passed over them.
  “They don't drink stars,” Rusk said. “That's what folk call it. The light comes from the old routes. The roots catch what leaks.”
  Elian drew a quick sketch. “The same light as the lamps on Mercy?”
  “Yes. The keep taught most things here to feed from the roads.”
  “And what fed the roads?”
  Rusk shook her head. “Someone else with a cost they chose not to count, perhaps.”
  They tied the boat to a root thick as a man's waist. June found the gate in a bank of reeds, a low stone arch with a wheel above it. It was jammed with silt. Lio and the woodworkers dug while Nell checked the dry path. Elian sat in the boat, marking which fallen limbs could serve. He could see loads the others would not have noticed, the way a branch had grown round a knot, the way its grain would carry a pull. Work steadied him. Nell was glad to see it.
  In the heart of the orchard they found a stone bench and a small dead tree. Cloth strips hung from its branches. Each held a name. Rusk went quiet there. One strip had been tied in a knot she could not quite reach. Nell offered to lift it down.
  “No. I know what it says.”
  They stood together while the others passed on. Rusk's son had gone aboard Mercy two years before, looking for a route back to the land where he had been born. Vale had sent a message that he had found one. No one at the Reach had seen him again.
  “His name is Den,” Rusk said. “He may be alive. I have to let that stay true without letting it rule every hour.”
  Nell looked at the cloth. She understood why Rusk had warned her about the list of names. Writing could be care. It could also be a chain one tied around one's own throat.
  A shout came from the gate. June had found a metal plate beneath the silt. On it, under a coat of white grit, lay three carved circles and a mark like an open hand. Elian asked them to bring him a rubbing. When Nell did, he spread it over his knee.
  “This is a tide measure,” he said. “Not just a gate.”
  He showed her the narrow cuts beside the circles. One marked water in the outer channel. One marked the orchard pools. The third ran beneath them both, into a space drawn as a square. The mark of the open hand sat beside a tiny door.
  “Could it be the keep?” she asked.
  “Could be a way to balance a sealed room.”
  Nell wanted to ask a hundred questions. Instead she held the rubbing steady while he copied the marks. There were facts here, cut by hands that had needed water to move safely. Facts could be learned.
  The gate opened with a groan. Water rushed through, clear at first, then gray with silt. The island shuddered. One of the woodworkers lost his footing and slid toward the arch. Lio caught his sleeve, but the cloth tore. June threw her rope across the man's chest. He caught it with both hands.
  “Don't stand,” Nell called. “Let us pull.”
  They dragged him clear. The water took his boot and the shovel. For a moment all they could hear was the roar of the channel. Then, from deep in the orchard, came a crack.
  A dead tree was falling across the path to the boat.
  Elian saw it from the water. He reached for an oar and forced the boat away from the bank. Nell ran with the others as branches struck the path behind them. A silver limb swept Rusk from her feet. Nell went back, caught her under the arms, and dragged her through the reeds. Lio met them at the edge. Together they lowered Rusk into the boat.
  The tree hit the water and lay across the old mooring place. A root snapped. Pale light spilled from the torn end, and for one second Nell saw through the sea to a sky full of birds.
  Then the root sealed itself with clear sap, and the birds were gone.
  Rusk's leg had been bruised, not broken. She sat shaking while June gave her Pell's crust. Elian stared at the fallen tree. Its trunk was dead, its roots had already drawn free, and its curve was exactly what they needed.
  The woodworkers wanted to cut the trunk free at once. Their foreman, a narrow man named Brin, had watched half his roof burn on the Reach. He could see the tow beam inside this tree as clearly as Elian could. He could also see the tide going out. If they waited, he said, another island might drift across the path and pin the tree for a week.
  Elian asked him to sit in the boat's stern. Brin refused. Elian pointed to the branch hanging above the bank. Its weight rested on a root that had split but not broken. A single cut in the wrong place would bring the whole crown down where they would have to stand.
  “I know how to fell wood,” Brin said.
  “So do I.”
  “Then let me do my work.”
  Nell saw Elian's jaw tighten. He was hurt, tired, and confined to a boat while another man stood on ground he wanted to be working. She waited for him to make the argument into a contest. Instead he held out the drawing.
  “Show me where you'd cut.”
  Brin took it. He marked two places, then a third. Elian asked what would bear the weight after the second. Brin opened his mouth, looked up at the tree, and shut it again. He walked along the bank. One of his workers followed, and together they tested the split root with a long pole. The crown sank a finger's width. Both men stepped back.
  Brin came to the boat without the drawing.
  “We need a shore brace.”
  “And a second line,” Elian said.
  “Which you don't have.”
  “Which we don't have.”
  The change in that one word eased the man's face. They were no longer arguing over who knew the craft. They were counting what the job required. Brin sat on the stern board and helped Elian draw a brace that could be made from the Reach's short pieces.
  While they worked, June went to rinse mud from her hands. Nell followed at a distance. The girl had a way of checking each patch of water before she knelt, as though any still pool might carry Vale's eye. Nell did not tell her the fear was needless. She sat on a stone beside her.
  “Pell used to sleep through bells,” June said. “Before the room. Now he wakes even if someone knocks a bowl.”
  “Mira can help him learn which sounds are safe.”
  “Can you make a sound safe?”
  “You can let it happen often enough without harm. You can tell him before it happens. Some nights that will help. Some nights it won't.”
  June rubbed her palms together under the water. “I don't want him to be afraid forever.”
  Nell chose her words carefully. “You don't have to cure every fear to be his sister.”
  The girl looked at her, angry for an instant. Then the anger sagged. She had been doing an adult's work for so long that kindness which took a task away could feel like an insult.
  “Who does it if I don't?”
  “We share it. You can check whether we do.”
  June looked toward the boat, where Lio was helping Rusk move her bruised leg. Brin had begun to argue about the brace again, but now he was smiling. Elian was smiling too. For a small span of time, no one needed June to keep a door shut or a brother alive.
  She splashed water at Nell's boot.
  Nell splashed back. June laughed in surprise, as if the sound had come from someone standing behind her. They both looked toward the orchard roots, half expecting punishment. Nothing moved except leaves.
  When they returned to the boat, June had mud on one cheek and both shoes. She made a point of showing Nell the soles before she climbed in.
  They did not haul it that day. They marked it, checked the gate, and went back with a tow crew at the next safe tide. The choice cost them hours. No one argued. Nell thought that perhaps trust grew from such dull, unseen refusals to rush.
  By night the beam lay beside the barge. Elian sat on the dock and showed the Reach's builders how to join it without tearing the grain. June brought Pell to see what she had found. He asked whether the lost boot had gone into the sky with the birds.
  “Perhaps,” she said.
  “Then I hope no one was under it.”
  Nell laughed, and Rusk laughed with her. It did not last long, but it was free of the sea.
  Below the garden, the woman asked for a piece of chalk. Nell held it against the wood, feeling foolish. A faint scrape answered. When she drew her hand away, white dust lay on the other side of a seam too thin to see.
  “Not yet,” the voice said. “But that is how small a way can be.”
  

CHAPTER 10
A Guest Who Had No Boat
Vale came aboard while the crew was eating. No bell warned them. No rope moved along the hull. One moment the lower stair stood empty; the next he was there with a wet coat over his arm and a line of water at his feet. Iven saw him first. He set down his bowl, stood across the stair, and called Anet's name.
  Vale waited. That was what frightened Nell most. He did not look like a man who had slipped past a guard. He looked like a guest early for a meal.
  Anet came with Lio. Elian rose too fast and put his weight on his hurt foot. Nell caught his arm.
  “Sit,” she said. “You can hear him from here.”
  June had already taken Pell through the pantry. Mira followed. The empty places at the table seemed to accuse Vale more than any words.
  “How did you board?” Anet asked.
  “By the key you brought.”
  “It was sealed.”
  “A root can wait in salt.”
  “Can you leave the same way?”
  “Yes.”
  “Then do so.”
  He looked past her at Nell. “You have someone in your brace chamber. I can help.”
  The galley went still. Anet kept her body between him and the stair.
  “You can speak from where you stand.”
  Vale laid the wet coat over a peg. There was a patch at one elbow, neatly sewn. Nell had a sudden ugly thought that some grateful passenger had mended it for him.
  “The chamber touches an old service passage,” he said. “It is not wholly within your ship. Opening it from this side would join your lower deck to a pressure route beneath the keep. Your hull would fill before you could shut it.”
  Elian leaned forward. “Where is the outer seal?”
  “Under the paired bells. I have charts.”
  “What do you want?” Nell asked.
  “June and Pell are wards of Mercy. Their care belongs with me.”
  “No.”
  He looked at the doorway through which they had gone. “They cannot be taken back through a raw crossing. They came from a broken road. The keep steadies them here.”
  Nell felt a chill. Pell had grown a tooth. June had scars. They had eaten real bread, put weight on the deck, laughed with the crew. She would not let him turn them into a doubt.
  “How do you know they need it?”
  “They were failing when I found them.”
  “They were starving,” Mira said from the pantry door. “I examined them. You are naming a thing we can treat as a thing only you can cure.”
  Vale's face changed. “I kept them alive for months.”
  “Yes,” Mira said. “And then you bound them.”
  No one spoke for several breaths. Vale's gaze moved over the table, the patched coats, the bread, the small places people had made for one another. Nell wondered what he had once hoped Mercy would be. Perhaps a room like this one. Perhaps that was why he could not bear to see it without him.
  “Give us the charts,” Anet said. “Then leave.”
  “I can't simply give them away.”
  “You can. You choose not to.”
  For the first time he looked old. He drew a folded sheet from his coat and placed it on the floor. Iven picked it up with the edge of a board. There was no root, no damp thread, no visible trick. Elian studied it from his seat.
  “This is incomplete.”
  “The central channels aren't fixed.”
  “Then mark what is.”
  Vale took a pencil from Anet. She made him stand at the far end of the table while he drew. He marked a ring of reefs, three tide gates, a dry stair, and a basin below the keep. The last line ended at a small square.
  “There,” he said. “The pressure room. It must be balanced at both ends.”
  “How?”
  “Three bells, sounded together. The whole bell at the Reach, the deep bell on Mercy, and the heart bell in the keep.”
  Anet gave a short hard laugh. “All three under your hand.”
  “Only one. I don't command the Reach.”
  “You use its need.”
  Vale flinched. Nell almost felt sorry for him. Then she thought of Pell crawling blind through the floor, and the feeling passed.
  Elian asked about the shore bearer. Vale's answer came too quickly. It needed the collars. Without them it might dive and crush the wreck, killing everyone aboard. He spoke of the animal as a flood, a storm, a weight that had to be managed. Nell asked whether he had ever tried easing one collar.
  He did not answer.
  From beneath the garden came a single heavy blow.
  Vale turned toward it. All his calm left him. He took a step forward. Iven blocked him, and Lio moved to his side.
  “Who is in there?” Nell asked.
  “I don't know.”
  “But you thought you did.”
  “I thought it was an echo. Echoes don't strike the wood with a body behind them.”
  The blow came again. Vale backed toward the lower stair.
  “If she tells you to open it, don't,” he said.
  “She told us the opposite.”
  He stopped. His mouth opened, then shut. He looked at the journal by Nell's bowl. She had wrapped it in plain cloth, but a corner showed. His eyes went to the old brown stain on the edge.
  “You took that from my table.”
  “Yes.”
  “It was blank when I found it.”
  “You set places for us.”
  “I set places for anyone the ship expected. The ship made the rest.”
  Nell felt a fine anger settle through her. He had allowed them to think he knew all its workings. Perhaps he had allowed himself to think the same.
  Anet pointed to the stair. “Leave the charts. Leave the key's path. Go.”
  Vale took his coat. He looked once toward the pantry, but did not call the children. At the bottom of the stairs he touched a knot in the wall. A seam opened, thin as the edge of a sheet. Cold white light filled it. He stepped through.
  Elian and Iven went down at once. The seam had shut. In the jar, the key was black with salt, but the white strand had grown through the lid.
  They took the whole jar to the dock and set it in a stone trough. Rusk posted a guard. She did not smash it, though Lio brought a hammer. A door they could track might be safer than a broken door they could not.
  Before the watch changed, Anet asked each guard to name what they had seen. Not what Vale might have meant. Not what they feared he could do. The seam had opened when he touched the knot. The jar had stayed shut while a root grew through its lid. No one had seen a boat. They put those facts beside the chart and left space beneath them.
  Nell helped Iven carry fresh lamps to the lower stair. He asked whether she wanted a guard at the sealed room too.
  “I want someone near enough to hear if a voice becomes a body,” she said. “I don't want a crowd asking it for answers.”
  He set the lamp on a shelf, checked that its flame could not reach the wood, and drew a chair beside the stair. Then he asked for another guard to share the watch. Nell saw him choose that before Anet had to tell him. It was a small correction to an old habit of taking too much on himself. She wrote the two names on the watch slate and went up, leaving the light in hands that had asked for help.
  That night, Nell asked the woman whether Vale had told the truth.
  “Some of it,” came the faint reply.
  “About you?”
  “About the water.”
  Nell sat against the wall. “I need more than warnings.”
  “I know.”
  “Then help me learn.”
  For the first time, the woman gave her a task with a beginning and an end.
  “Find the city where the steps go down into daylight. Bring back a clear piece of glass. And Nell—go with him. Don't let this become a choice you make in separate rooms.”
  

CHAPTER 11
An Inch of Safe Water
Elian would not take the ark to the city on the strength of a voice. He said it gently, with Nell beside him and the sealed door before them, but he said it where the woman could hear.
  “We'll test the pressure room first. If you know how it works, help us do that.”
  Three taps answered. Then the woman said, “Use a tube. Not a hole.”
  They found the end of an old drain beneath a brace. Elian had passed it a hundred times without seeing more than a plugged pipe. Its cap bore the same open-hand mark as the plate in the orchard. He cleaned the threads, set a narrow tube to the mouth, and ran it up through the garden to a clear jug. Two valves stood between the pipe and the jug. If the pressure rose too far, he could close either one.
  Anet watched every step. So did June, who had brought a slate and asked whether she could draw what they were doing. Elian handed her the orchard rubbing. He told her to mark the parts she did not understand. Soon most of her drawing had a circle round it.
  “Good,” he said. “That means we know what to ask.”
  Nell held the jug. Elian opened the first valve a fraction. The tube filled with water so clear it looked like moving air. It rose past the garden floor, past Nell's hand, and stopped halfway up the jug. She could feel a faint warmth through the glass.
  No flood. No dark force bursting from the wall. Only a column of water showing how much weight the chamber bore.
  They left it for an hour. At the paired bells, the level rose. At the low note from Mercy, it dropped. June marked each change on her slate. Pell came to watch and asked whether the water was breathing. Elian said that was a fair way to think of it, provided no one forgot what made it move.
  Nell went below. “Can you see the tube?”
  “No.”
  “Can you hear us work?”
  “Sometimes. It comes and goes.”
  “Are you in the same hour as us?”
  The silence was long enough to be an answer.
  “Not all of me is,” the woman said at last.
  Nell pressed a hand over her mouth. She wanted to ask what that meant, but she heard how little breath the voice had left. Instead she asked a smaller question.
  “Do you have water?”
  “Yes. Too much on one side, too little on the other.”
  “Food?”
  “A little.”
  “Light?”
  “The lamp is fading.”
  Nell went up and brought a fresh wick. It would not pass the seam. Neither would a page, a thread, or a thin piece of reed. Chalk dust had crossed because it was dust. She rubbed a little salt into the grain. The woman said she could taste it a while later, but could not tell how long.
  They could pass matter, then, in a form too small to feed a life. Elian wrote that down. Nell hated him for writing for perhaps a breath. Then she was glad someone could bear to make a fact of it.
  In the afternoon, they tested the cap again. This time a white feather came through the drain, folded tight as a seed. It opened in the jug and floated. Mira brought the feather Nell had found before. They matched in shape, though one had been bent near the quill.
  June fetched the four-winged bird from its perch. Its breast was bare in one small patch.
  “It drops one when it's scared,” she said.
  Nell stared at the two feathers. One had come from the bird here. The other might have come from a day they had not lived. It was proof of a kind, and proof could be worse than doubt.
  Elian took her hand when the others left. “We don't need to solve time today.”
  “We need to get her out.”
  “Yes. Which means we need to solve a door.”
  She leaned on the rail. The tide jug glowed in the late light. On the dock, people were fitting the tow beam to the barge. A child had tied ribbons to its blunt front. There was work everywhere that a hand could finish. Nell longed for one task as plain as that.
  “Suppose it is me,” she said. “Suppose I go somewhere and get hurt, and all this is what brings me there.”
  “Then we take good notes and more rope.”
  “You can't mend this with rope.”
  “No. But I'd like to have it when we learn what can.”
  She laughed despite herself. He took her in his arms. They stood carefully, because of his foot and the jug and the whole frail day. His heart beat against her cheek at the rate it always had. The sea had not learned that. She would not give it the right to this touch.
  When they kissed, it was slow, almost shy. They had been so busy saving each other that they had left little time to choose each other. Nell put a hand in his hair and felt him let go of a breath he had been holding for days.
  At supper, Anet laid out the city journey. Rusk would remain at the Reach to finish the barge and guard the key. The ark would sail with a reduced landing crew. No one would follow a voice away from sight of a partner. They would take empty stretchers as well as tools. June could come only as far as the boat unless the route required her knowledge. Pell would stay with Mira aboard.
  There was argument over that last rule. Nell let June make her case, then asked her to name what Pell needed if they both failed to return. The girl went silent. It was a hard question. Nell did not soften it with a false promise.
  At last June said, “I'll teach Lio the gate marks before we go.”
  “You can teach me too,” Elian said.
  She did, with fierce care. By the end of the lesson, all three could draw the signs without looking. June made them do it twice.
  Just before midnight, the tide jug fell an inch without a bell. A new seam of dark glass showed in the water beneath the bow. It ran toward a far green light.
  The city had opened its road.
  

CHAPTER 12
The City Under the Day
They came to the city by descending into light. The sea parted around a broad stair, leaving water held upright on either side. Fish moved behind those walls as if nothing had changed. The air in the stairwell was warm and full of dust. At the bottom stood a gate of white stone, wide enough for the ark's small boat to pass on wheels.
  Elian tested each step with a pole. His foot hurt less, though Nell could tell when he hid a limp. Lio and Iven lowered the boat on ropes, its keel cushioned with folded sailcloth. They would need it if the stair flooded. Nell carried the clear jug from the garden, now fitted with a stopper and marked in chalk. The water inside rose when she faced the city, as though drawn toward a larger breath.
  June waited at the top with Anet. She had shown them the gate marks. One meant safe for a turn of the tide. One meant shut before the deep bell. One had no name she knew. It looked like two hands pulling apart.
  That third mark was cut above the gate.
  Nell traced it with her eyes and walked on.
  The streets were empty, but not ruined. Doors stood open on rooms with chairs, shelves, folded rugs. A bowl of dry fruit sat beside a window. Dust covered it, except for a small bare patch where something had been lifted recently. No weeds split the paving. No birds nested under the eaves. The city felt held in the pause between one breath and the next.
  In the square, they found a fountain full of glass.
  Pieces lay where water should have been, clear slabs with smooth edges and deep green hearts. Light moved inside them. Nell picked up a piece with a cloth. It showed her the galley, not as it was now, but with the old bench before Lio had repaired it. A small scar on the table was gone.
  She put it down.
  “Do not look through them,” she said.
  Iven had already turned away from another piece. His face was gray. Lio caught his elbow but asked no question.
  At the far side of the square, a door banged. Nell heard a cry. She raised a hand, and the crew drew close. A woman stood on a roof above a narrow court, waving a strip of red cloth. Beside her were two children and a man with his arm in a sling.
  “Stay where you are,” Elian called. “We're coming to you.”
  “We can't go down,” the woman answered. “It shows him the wrong stair.”
  They found the problem in the house. Every landing opened onto a different set of steps. The ones seen from below led upward. The same steps, reached from the top, ran down into water. The house had been cut across more than one road. Its rooms did not agree on where they stood.
  Elian laid a line from the street to the first post and tied a knot at each turn. He kept one hand on the line while Lio tested the next landing. Nell watched the tide jug. Its water leaned toward some doors and away from others. They chose the ones where it stayed level.
  Halfway up, Iven stopped.
  A woman stood in a side room. She wore a coat he knew. Nell saw that before she saw the woman's face. It was not someone Nell recognized, but Iven's whole body had moved toward her.
  “You can tell me now,” the woman said.
  Iven shut his eyes.
  “I wanted you to understand,” he whispered.
  Lio took the line from his hand. “Say her name to me.”
  Iven did. Then he named the place where he had last seen her. Then he said, in a voice that broke, what he had done there. The woman in the room kept waiting with a kind open face. She did not interrupt, which somehow made it worse.
  “I don't get to make her hear it,” Iven said.
  He stepped back to Lio. The door closed without a sound.
  On the roof they found six people, not four. Two had been lying behind a water tank. One was feverish. The woman with the cloth called herself Tavi. Her husband was the man with the sling. They had come from a market road that had folded shut beneath a storm. They had been in the city for twelve days by their own count, though the bells outside had rung only twice.
  Tavi would not let them lower the first child until she had seen how the chair was tied. Her husband, whose name was Harl, tried to tell her to trust the crew. She rounded on him so fiercely that he stopped speaking. Then she apologized, not for wanting to see the knots, but for the way she had spoken. Elian put the chair between them and asked her to take the weight on one side while he loaded the other.
  The rope drew tight. The door panel held. Tavi watched the knots settle.
  “If it tips?” she asked.
  “We have a second line on the back,” he said. “Lio guides that from below.”
  “If the first one breaks?”
  “It won't fall clear. Both loops pass under the seat. Here. Feel it.”
  She felt the rope with her fingers. Nell saw how badly she wanted certainty, and how little of it any honest person could give. Elian let her keep asking until she reached the question beneath the others.
  “Have you done this before?”
  “Yes,” he said. “Not on this roof. Not with your child. We will test it with a load first.”
  They filled a sack with loose stone from beside the water tank and lowered it to the street. Lio guided it clear of the window ledges. At the bottom, he raised both hands. Tavi watched them haul the chair back up. She touched the child on the head, then stepped aside.
  The older child went first. He held the seat with white knuckles and stared at Nell until he sank below the roof edge. His mother spoke to him the whole way. She told him where to look and what Lio would do. When he reached the street, he began to cry. Tavi made a movement toward the edge. Nell caught her sleeve, gently, and pointed. Lio was kneeling beside him, letting the boy grip his coat.
  “He can cry and be safe,” Nell said.
  Tavi nodded, though tears had begun to run down her own face.
  The younger child refused the chair. She climbed under the tank and clung to a pipe. Harl started toward her with his good arm out, then stopped at Nell's raised hand. The roof had only one safe strip beside the tank. Too many adults there would corner the child and put weight on a cracked edge.
  Nell took off her pack and sat where the girl could see her.
  “My name is Nell.”
  “I heard.”
  “What's yours?”
  “Iska.”
  “You can keep holding the pipe, Iska. I need to ask about your shoes.”
  The girl blinked at her. Nell showed her own wet boot, the split near the sole, the place she had mended it with waxed thread. Iska looked down despite herself. One of her shoes had a knot where the strap had broken. She began to explain who had tied it. Nell listened. They talked about feet, and roofs, and the difference between being carried and falling.
  “I don't want to go down,” Iska said at last.
  “I know. I don't want to make you. The water is coming, so we can't stay. Would you like your mother in the chair first, or would you like to take this rope to her?”
  It was a small choice. It was real. Iska looked toward Tavi, then took the end of the guide line. She crawled out from under the tank with it in her fist. Nell did not praise her for ceasing to be afraid. She thanked her for bringing the rope.
  Tavi went down with Iska held against her. Elian had changed the chair loops and tested the double weight. The girl kept the guide line in her hand, though it was Lio below who bore its pull. When they reached the street, she looked up and waved it once.
  Only then did Harl sit. His injured arm had swollen inside the sling. He said the shoulder had been caught when their market cart went through the gate. He had spent days telling Tavi it was only a strain because he could not bear to add one more need to the roof. Nell loosened the cloth before they lowered him.
  “Pain isn't a debt you pay for being found,” she told him. “Tell Mira how it happened. All of it.”
  Harl stared at the tank where his daughter had hidden. “Will we see the market again?”
  “I don't know.”
  He nodded slowly. The honest answer left him sad, but it did not leave him waiting for a promise that would never arrive. He put his good hand on the chair and let them take his weight.
  Nell checked the two men who remained on the roof. They needed water, food, and shade. Elian adjusted the sling chair to support the sick man's head. They lowered him to the street, with Lio below to guide the seat past the window ledges. Tavi waited below with her children, calling up whenever a rope caught. Her voice had grown steadier. Helping the others gave her something to do besides fear.
  The deep bell began while they were lowering the last passenger.
  Water showed at the far end of the square. It came over the paving in a thin bright sheet. Fish swam within it, their fins just breaking the surface. The tide jug filled to the stopper.
  “Now,” Elian said.
  They put the sick into the boat. The rest walked beside it, pushing on the gunwales. Nell wrapped a clear shard from the fountain in her spare scarf and tucked it into her pack. She did not look through it again.
  At the gate, the stone arch had begun to bend. Not crack: bend, as if it were a reflection on stirred water. The stair beyond appeared and vanished with each pulse. Anet shouted from above, but her words stretched into long low sounds.
  Elian tied the tow line to the boat's bow. He gave three pulls. The line drew tight. The boat rose onto the first step, jolting the sick man. Nell braced his head while Lio and Iven pushed from below.
  A wave struck the gate.
  The upright walls of water fell inward. Nell felt cold close around her calves. Elian stumbled. She caught his coat and held him while he found the line. Together they climbed, one step, then another. Behind them, the city filled with blue light.
  A child's shoe floated past Nell's knee. She caught it without thinking and thrust it into her coat. Later she would remember that absurd small act more clearly than the gate. The shoe had felt like something a hand could save.
  They reached the top as the stair shut. The sea became whole beneath the boat, lifting it free of the last step. Crew hauled them to the ark. Anet counted every face twice.
  That night Tavi's child came to the galley in one shoe. Nell gave back the other. The child hugged it to her chest, then asked whether they were allowed to sleep here.
  “For tonight,” Nell said. “And we'll tell you in the morning what comes next.”
  The promise was small enough to keep. She held to it with both hands.
  

CHAPTER 13
The Life in the Glass
Nell waited until morning to unwrap the shard. She had set it in a box under her bed, then moved the box to the galley, then asked Anet to lock it in the chart room. Fear had made each place feel wrong. At last she saw that no shelf would settle what she needed to know. She asked Elian and Anet to sit with her, put a cloth over the table, and laid the glass on it.
  In full light it looked plain. Clear at the center, green along one edge, no thicker than a window pane. Elian held a lamp behind it. On the opposite wall, where a patch of light should have fallen, they saw a garden gate.
  Nell knew that gate. She had left it in a place whose road was now closed.
  No one spoke. A hand came into view and lifted the latch. Beyond the gate lay a path, two chairs, a low bed of herbs, and a house with a roof Elian had once promised to mend. In this light, the roof was sound. A man's coat hung by the door. Nell saw her own hand set a bowl on a sill.
  The scene had no sound. That made it easier to believe.
  “Turn the lamp,” Anet said.
  Elian moved it. The garden stayed. Anet put her hand behind the shard. The picture did not fall across her skin. It hung in the air beyond it.
  “A held road,” Elian said. “Like the city.”
  “Or a picture,” Anet answered. “Don't name it too soon.”
  Nell watched the two chairs. One had a split arm. She had meant to mend it. In the glass it was mended with a rough strip of wood, the sort of repair Elian would make when he had time for care but not for pride.
  She wanted that chair with a hunger that shamed her.
  A figure came down the path. Elian, older by a few years, carrying onions in a basket. He bent to kiss the woman at the sill. They smiled at something outside the frame. Nell could not see what. A child, perhaps. A dog. A whole simple day.
  She covered the glass.
  “Enough.”
  Elian set down the lamp. Anet sat still, her hands flat on the table.
  “Do you want to tell me what it showed?” she asked.
  Nell looked at Elian. He had seen it too, yet his face was closed in a way she did not like.
  “A home,” she said.
  “Was it yours?”
  “A place we knew.”
  “Did it ask you to go?”
  “No.”
  “That may come later.”
  Anet locked the shard away. She did not forbid them to use it. They had come for a tool and needed to learn its work. But she set a rule: two witnesses, a written start time, no viewing when tired or hungry. Nell agreed. Elian did too, too quickly.
  They went below the garden. Nell described the glass to the voice. The woman was silent through the whole telling.
  “Put it against the drain at the next high bell,” she said at last. “Light it from the dry side. You'll see the pressure chamber.”
  “What else will we see?”
  “What the sea has kept closest to you.”
  “Did you know it would show that garden?”
  “Yes.”
  Nell's anger came fast. “You sent us for it.”
  “You needed the glass.”
  “You could have warned me.”
  “I thought I had.”
  “No. You warned me about doors and water. You let me find my life sitting in two chairs.”
  A faint breath came through the wall. When the voice returned, it was rougher.
  “I'm sorry.”
  Nell stood. She did not want an apology in her own voice. She wanted a name, a body, a fact she could be angry with.
  Elian followed her to the workroom. He shut the door and leaned against it.
  “I saw something else,” he said.
  She turned.
  “In the glass. It wasn't the garden for me.”
  “What was it?”
  “You on this deck. Hurt. I was trying to reach you. The water kept taking you back.”
  Nell sat on the bench. The room felt small.
  “Why didn't you say?”
  “Because I didn't know which was true.”
  “Neither may be true.”
  “I know that.”
  “But you feel one is.”
  He looked down. That was answer enough.
  They spent the next hour doing something harder than a rescue. They told each other exactly what they had seen, without making a meaning of it. Nell described the onions, the chair, the roof. Elian described the blood on a sleeve, a lamp at a wrist, a bell that rang under water. Each detail became less powerful when set beside the other's. They could no longer pretend the glass offered one clear future.
  At the end, Elian took out the square he had cut from his sleeve. Nell had put it in his hand while he slept after the fire. He smoothed it on the bench.
  “I was afraid you'd want the garden more than this.”
  “I do want the garden more than fear. I don't want it more than you.”
  “What if it came with me?”
  “Then I'd ask whether you had chosen it. I'd ask what it cost. I'd ask who wasn't allowed out.”
  He nodded, but she could see how badly he wanted to believe he could keep her from the scene he had seen. Love made him brave. It also made him ready to lock a door from the wrong side.
  At the high bell, they took the glass to the drain. Anet held the lamp. June recorded the tide level. Elian set the shard on a wooden frame. Nell watched the wall.
  A room appeared, dim and square. Its floor sloped toward a narrow channel. One wall held a round hatch, another the plain wooden door beneath the garden. Between them was a bench. On it lay a coat, a small pack, and a lamp with a long cord.
  No woman.
  Nell leaned closer. “Where is she?”
  The picture shifted. Beyond the round hatch, a passage filled with pale rushing water. At its end hung a bell with no clapper. Beneath it, someone had scratched a line into the stone.
  Leave room for the living hand.
  Elian copied it. When he finished, the glass went dark.
  From the other side of the wood came three slow knocks. Then the voice said, “Now you know where to find me. Please don't mistake that for knowing when.”
  

CHAPTER 14
The Quarrel on the Open Deck
They returned to the Reach with six more passengers and no more spare space. The barge was ready. Its deck had been divided with rope into sleeping bays. Food stood in sealed chests at the center, low enough not to tip it. Rusk had found two people who could steer it under tow and three who could cook for many without wasting fuel. She had also found a way to make every person waiting on the dock ask Nell the same question with their eyes.
  Can I come too?
  Nell went aboard the barge before she answered anyone. She checked the water barrels, the shelter cloth, the place where the sick would lie. She asked the builders to lower a shelf that would break loose in a roll. Elian stayed on the dock, testing the tow beam. They worked in sight of each other and did not speak.
  The image from the glass had settled between them. Nell knew it was a thing the sea had shown, not a vow. Yet she could feel Elian watching each step she took, each rope she handled. When she leaned over the barge's side, he moved as if to catch her before there was any cause.
  At last she set down her tools.
  “Come and walk.”
  They went to the far end of the smallest island, where the burned stalls had been cleared. The ground smelled of ash. People had begun to set new posts. Nell stood beside the black ring left by a cooking fire.
  “You can't keep doing it,” she said.
  “Doing what?”
  “Measuring every place I stand as if it's where I die.”
  He looked away.
  “I've lost too much to ignore a warning.”
  “So have I. That doesn't make me yours to hide.”
  “I didn't say that.”
  “No. You asked Anet to leave me at the Reach.”
  He turned sharply. She had not meant to say it so soon, but there it was.
  “She told you.”
  “She said you were worried. She said I should hear it from you. I waited.”
  Elian sat on a stack of boards. His face was pale with more than pain.
  “I asked her whether you were too tired for the keep. I didn't order her.”
  “You used a kinder name for the same wish.”
  “Yes,” he said. “I did.”
  The admission took some of the heat from her anger. It left the hurt clear beneath it.
  Nell told him about the chair again. Not because she wanted the glass life, but because she wanted him to understand that the sea had found her weakness too. She wanted a house whose doors led only to rooms. She wanted to wash a cup without listening for a cry below the floor. She wanted him alive beside her when she was old.
  “And I still get to choose this road,” she said. “You don't get to make fear the part of me that speaks.”
  He pressed his hands together. “What if I can't bear it?”
  “Then tell me. Let me bear some of it with you.”
  They sat apart for a while. A hammer rang from the dock. Somewhere a child was learning the Reach's warning song. Each missed note rose clear in the cold air.
  “I saw you in water,” Elian said. “I keep thinking that if I do one thing differently, I can stop it.”
  “Then do a thing differently. Tell me what you're planning before you plan around me.”
  He nodded. She believed he meant it. Meaning it would not make it easy.
  When they went back, Vale was waiting in the stone trough where they had left the key. He had not climbed out. Rusk stood over him with a boat hook. Two guards held the dock clear.
  “I said he could talk from there,” she told Anet. “He can get wet for it.”
  Vale's coat was dry above the waist. Below it, water rippled in the trough without spilling over. Nell felt a mean pleasure at the sight, then let it go.
  “The route to the keep is closing,” he said. “The shore bearer is failing. Mercy will be called in at the next third bell.”
  “Why come here?” Anet asked.
  “Because you have the people who could mend the collars.”
  “Release them,” Nell said.
  “The wreck would sink.”
  “Then get the people off.”
  “There are more than you saw. Many can't leave their rooms.”
  Rusk stepped closer. “Is Den there?”
  Vale looked at her and did not answer fast enough.
  “Is my son there?”
  “Yes.”
  “Alive?”
  “Yes.”
  Nell saw Rusk grip the hook until her knuckles whitened. Vale's voice softened, and Nell hated him for knowing when to soften it.
  “I have tried to bring him out.”
  “Bring him to the rail,” Rusk said. “Let me hear him choose.”
  “I can't from here.”
  “Then you have given me a claim, not my son.”
  She turned away. It was the bravest thing Nell had seen her do.
  Vale looked at Elian. “The collars are part of the pressure route. If the bearer dies, the room beneath your garden seals from both sides.”
  Nell felt Elian shift beside her. He did not speak for her. He looked at her first.
  “We need to go back,” she said.
  “Yes,” he answered. “With a plan to empty the wreck, not repair its chains.”
  Anet told Vale he would guide them from the trough, with Rusk watching, until the ark was under way. The key would remain in the Reach. If he wanted to return to Mercy, he could do it through his own door after they had learned the approach.
  He agreed. Nell could not tell whether relief or defeat made him bow his head.
  That night, Elian moved his workbench into the galley. He spread the charts where everyone could see them. Nell brought the glass and the tide notes. They worked until the lamp burned low, asking questions in front of the crew, changing their minds aloud.
  When they went to bed, he did not ask her to stay safe. He asked whether she would wake him if she heard the voice.
  “Yes,” she said. “And you wake me.”
  She lay with her hand over his. Neither slept soon. But for the first time in days, fear had to share the room with them both.
  

CHAPTER 15
The Back of the Bound Beast
Mercy rose at the low bell with its stern already broken. A long split ran from the garden to the lower rail. Water poured from it in bright streams. The shore bearer lay almost still, its huge fins moving only at the tips. Nell could see the iron collars through the clear sea. There were seven. Each held a bundle of cords that vanished into the wreck's keel.
  Anet brought the ark alongside the dry deck, leaving room for the bearer to roll. The barge stayed well astern, held by two tow lines with quick releases. Everyone knew which line to cut if the ark was pulled under. No one liked the plan. That was one reason Nell trusted it. It had room for what might go wrong.
  Vale waited at the top of the steps. He wore no towel now. His cuffs were wet, his hair uncombed. Behind him stood people Nell had not seen before: a thin man wrapped in a rug, two women carrying a child between them, an old woman with a box of plants. They stared at the ark as if it were the strange thing here.
  “We take the able first,” Anet called. “They help us make room for the rest. No one goes back for goods unless we clear it.”
  The old woman clutched her box.
  “Plants may come,” Mira said. “Set them by me.”
  That small mercy broke the crowd's stillness. People began to move.
  Elian, Lio, and Iven went below with Vale. Nell took the passage to the rooms with Rusk and Mira. Ember stayed on the ark's bow, looking down at the pale back. He had not spread his wings since they had left the Reach. When the bearer stirred, he leaned forward and gave a low rolling call. The great fins answered with a faint pulse.
  The passenger rooms lay on a deck that seemed too long for the wreck. Doors ran farther than Nell could see. Some stood open on empty bunks. Others showed gardens, kitchens, stretches of road, a bedroom with a lamp still lit. At each closed door, Vale had chalked a number. Beside some numbers were dates. Beside others, only a line.
  Nell stopped at the first. “What does the line mean?”
  “No reply,” Vale said from the stair behind them.
  “How many times did you ask?”
  He looked at the marks. “I stopped counting.”
  “Start again.”
  Rusk went straight to a door marked with a small red knot. She put her palm on it and said her son's name. No answer came. She said it again, not louder, only more clearly.
  Inside, a chair scraped.
  “Mother?”
  Rusk closed her eyes. Her lips moved once before she spoke. “I'm here. Will you open the door?”
  “I can't. I'm waiting for you to come home.”
  “I am outside your room.”
  “No. You're in the house. I can hear you in the house.”
  Nell felt the sea's old cruelty tighten around them. It did not need a monster's teeth. It needed a voice just kind enough to keep a hand on a latch.
  Rusk sat on the floor. “Den, when you were seven, you stole a bell tongue from the repair shed. You told me it fell in your pocket. I made you carry it back in the rain.”
  A small laugh came from inside.
  “You didn't make me. Father did.”
  “He wanted to. I made you. You were angry with me for a week.”
  Silence.
  “The mother you're hearing,” Rusk said, “does she ever get a thing wrong?”
  Nell watched the wood. She could almost feel Den trying to set two lives beside each other.
  “No,” he said at last.
  “Well, I do. I was wrong about that bell. You hadn't stolen it. You found it under the dock and wanted to mend it. You told me, and I didn't listen. I came here to find you, not to be right.”
  The latch lifted.
  Den was a grown man, thinner than the image Rusk had carried in her face. His beard had been cut with a knife. He held the door with one hand and a child's wool coat with the other. Behind him lay a warm room full of light. From within it a woman called him to supper.
  Rusk did not pull him. She held out her hand.
  He took it.
  At the next room, a passenger refused. At another, no one answered. Nell made a mark different from Vale's line: a circle with a space left open. They would return if they could. She would not call a silence a death.
  Below, Elian found the collar controls in a chamber that smelled of hot oil. Each lever fed a separate cord. The marks on the wall showed how to tighten, how to feed, how to draw power. There was no mark for release.
  Vale stared at the panel. “There was one.”
  “Where?”
  “Here. Beneath the lower plate.”
  Lio pulled the plate free. Under it lay seven handles, locked together with a bar. Rust had grown thick around the pins. Someone had painted over the seam.
  “You sealed it,” Elian said.
  “A long time ago.”
  “Why?”
  “Because it tried to dive while we had passengers.”
  “So you made it unable to leave.”
  Vale bowed his head. “Yes.”
  The plain word changed the room. Elian could work with it. Excuses hid the shape of harm; this admission laid it bare. He gave Vale a wrench.
  “Then help us undo what you did.”
  They cut the paint and drove the first pin. The shore bearer shuddered. Water burst from a floor seam. On the deck above, people cried out. Elian stopped, waited for Anet's signal through the line, then eased the first handle a finger's width.
  Outside the glass, the nearest cord slackened.
  The bearer rolled. A fin rose beside the ark, broad as a field. Ember launched himself at it. For one dreadful instant Nell thought he meant to attack. Instead he landed on the pale hide and drove his claws into a hanging knot of cord. He pulled, wings beating, while the beast kept still beneath him.
  The knot tore free. Dark blood spread in the water.
  Ember cried out. One cord had whipped across his wing. He lost his grip and slid toward the sea. Lio threw a hooked line from the lower hatch, but it fell short. Nell reached the deck in time to see Ember vanish beneath the surface.
  She could not breathe.
  Then the shore bearer's fin curved upward. It lifted the dragon gently, like a hand lifting a fallen bird. Ember lay on it, coughing water. His wing hung wrong. The beast moved him toward the ark's bow, where the crew spread nets and pulled him aboard.
  Nell knelt beside him. He was alive. His eye found hers. She put her forehead against the warm ridge of his face and said nothing, because every word she had was too small.
  From below came the sound of another pin driven free.
  They worked until the high bell. Thirty-one passengers left Mercy. Some had to be carried. Some stepped away from rooms that called them by names they had not heard in years. Four chose to stay behind closed doors. Anet wrote those names down too. She would not let their refusal become an excuse to forget them.
  The seven cords loosened one by one. Mercy settled lower in the water. The bearer raised its head for the first time.
  Its eyes were not blind, Nell saw. A pale film covered them, scarred by the lamps' harsh light. It turned toward Ember and held there. The dragon lifted his good wing an inch, then let it fall.
  Vale stood at the rail of the sinking wreck. He watched the ark take the last stretcher. He held a ledger against his chest.
  Nell called him. “You too.”
  This time, he came.
  

CHAPTER 16
The Ledger of Open Rooms
They could not save Mercy and free the bearer at the same tide. Elian knew that before he said it aloud. The lower timbers had grown around the power cords. When the last collar opened, the whole wreck would peel away. There were still four people behind closed doors, and a fifth door that no one could reach because the passage had bent through water.
  Anet gave them one day. Not because a day was fair, but because the ark's water stores, the injured dragon, and the coming route would bear no more. The bearer drifted beside them, held by one loose cord. It was no longer a chain, Elian told himself. Then he looked at it and knew that a thing could have a kinder name and still hold a life where it did not wish to be.
  He asked Vale to open the ledger.
  They sat at the galley table with Rusk, Nell, and two witnesses from the Reach. Vale had brought lists of passengers, dates, room numbers, and notes on their health. Some entries were careful. Others were harsh scraps: refused, unstable, ungrateful, lost. As the pages grew later, the numbers of meals and doses gave way to marks for light drawn from each room.
  “You used them for power,” Rusk said.
  “Only when the bearer couldn't provide enough.”
  “Only doesn't help you.”
  “No.”
  He did not try again. Nell saw his hands shake as he turned the pages. A confession would not mend what had happened. Yet without the records, people might never know where their loved ones had gone. They made copies, one for the ark and one for the Reach. Vale read each name aloud. If he did not know whether a person had died, Nell made him say he did not know.
  Den sat outside the galley. He had asked to hear but not to be seen. Rusk kept glancing toward the door. At last she moved her chair so she could hold his hand through the opening. He let her, though his gaze stayed on the deck.
  By noon they had found a pattern. The rooms nearest the keep used more light. Their passengers stayed longest, ate least, and lost the most weight. Vale called them deep rooms. Some had once been used as stores for routes before the people came aboard.
  “What does that mean?” Elian asked.
  “The keep held roads that might be needed again. A harbor, a farm, a way across a broken gate. It could keep a place from being wholly lost.”
  “And people inside it?”
  “Sometimes.”
  “Alive?”
  “Yes. But not always at the same rate as us.”
  Nell thought of the city roof, the tooth, the woman's breath behind the door. All the facts they had gathered began to lean toward one another.
  “Can a room touch two times?” she asked.
  Vale closed the ledger. “A service room can. That's why they were sealed from ordinary decks.”
  “What happens if someone goes through?”
  “They may come out before they went in. Or much later. Most routes were set to avoid it. When the keep lost its crews, the settings drifted.”
  “Can it be controlled?”
  “From the heart bell.”
  There it was again: the place they had to reach, offered by the man who had reason to lead them. Nell did not reject it for that alone. She asked for a chart and made him point to every control he had used, every route he had seen, every claim he had only heard from the keep's old records. By the end, the paper was crowded with different marks. Ignorance had a shape too.
  In the afternoon, they returned to the four closed rooms. The first passenger was a woman named Esa. She had been listening to a daughter who had died before she found Mercy. Nell told her what the ark could offer: a bed, food, work when she was ready, no voice at night unless she sought one in memory. Esa asked for an hour. Nell gave it.
  At the second door, a man cursed them. At the third, no one replied. At the fourth, a child asked whether the sea could come with her. Mira sat outside and explained that she could bring a cup of it, but the room itself could not travel. The child was fifteen and had not left that room in what felt to her like three weeks. The ledger said two years.
  Esa came out at the end of the hour. She carried no bag. Mira's girl followed, holding a cup with both hands. The man who cursed them stayed. They left food and a lamp by his door, then told him the exact time of their final return.
  The silent room opened only when Rusk sang a work song from the Reach. Inside they found a dead man in a chair, his hand resting on an empty bowl. Vale stood in the doorway and could not move. Nell covered the man's face. Elian and Lio carried him out.
  They buried him on a small drift of soil that had gathered around the bearer's shell. It was not a true shore. It was what they had. Rusk read his name from the ledger. Vale stood at the back and did not ask to speak.
  The last unreachable door lay below the water line. Elian used the clear glass to look through the flooded passage. There was no room beyond it, only a broken arch opening into the keep's lower basin. Someone had scratched the open-hand mark beside it.
  Nell felt the cold certainty of recognition.
  “The pressure route,” she said.
  Vale nodded. “Mercy has been tied to it all these years.”
  “Then we can reach the keep without taking the main gate.”
  “You can reach its underside. That's not the same as getting in.”
  Elian looked at Nell. For once neither had to ask what the other meant to do. He rolled up the chart.
  “We need a boat that can fit the arch, air for the flooded part, and a line back to the ark.”
  “And time,” Nell said.
  Behind them, the bearer gave a low moan. The loose cord drew tight, then slack. They did not have much.
  That evening the last man came out of his room. He was angry, weak, and ashamed, though no one had asked him to be ashamed. Lio gave him soup and a place by the stove. He ate without looking at anyone.
  Elian went below the garden. He sat before the sealed door with the new chart on his knees.
  “We're coming to the basin,” he said.
  The woman answered after a while. “I know.”
  “Do we have a choice?”
  “Yes.”
  “Did you?”
  “Yes.”
  He rubbed his face. “Then tell me one thing that isn't a warning.”
  On the other side of the wood, she breathed slowly.
  “You were kind to the last man. I remember that. He was hard to be kind to, and you did it anyway.”
  Elian sat in the dark after the voice faded. He had wanted a map. Instead she had given him back a part of himself he could still choose to be.
  

CHAPTER 17
The Vote Before the Crossing
Anet called everyone who could stand to the open deck. Those who could not stand listened from the galley with the doors wide. The barge lay close enough for its passengers to hear. Mercy drifted beyond it, empty at last, its windows dim. The shore bearer still held one loose service cord that ran to the pressure route. Elian had asked for time to use that route before they cut it. Now he had to ask the people whose lives would pay for the delay.
  He laid the charts on a crate. Nell stood beside him, holding the slate with the tide measures. Vale stayed at the back under guard. He had offered to speak. Anet had told him he would answer questions after the others had heard the facts.
  Elian explained the three bells. The whole bell at the Reach could hold the outer tide steady. Mercy's bell could open the basin route. The heart bell in the keep could set the service chamber to the same hour as the ark. If all three rang within the same span of water, the door below the garden could be made safe.
  “Can be,” Anet said. “Not will be. We haven't done it.”
  Elian nodded. He told them that too.
  Nell explained what the glass had shown. A plain room, a bench, a round hatch, the door in their hull. She did not tell them the woman was herself. She did not know it as a fact, and she would not make a claim so large on the strength of a voice. She said that someone alive was there, that the voice had helped rescue the children, and that opening the door now would flood the ark.
  A man on the barge asked why they should risk so many for one.
  Nell had expected it. She was glad he had said it plainly.
  “We shouldn't give her more weight than any of you,” she said. “There are also routes in the keep that may let all of us reach a stable shore. We need those charts. And the bearer needs the last cord cut without dragging the pressure route into it. These tasks meet in one place. That doesn't make the risk small.”
  “What if the voice is a trap?”
  “Then we still need the charts, and we still need to free the bearer. We will not open the garden door on her word.”
  The man sat back. He did not look content, but he looked heard.
  Rusk spoke of the Reach. If the keep's routes failed, her islands would lose the light that fed their roots and gardens. The bells might cease to hold their places. Staying was a choice with danger too. She had sent a boat back to the tower with instructions and two copies of the tide schedule. They had agreed on a count of bell strokes that did not depend on clocks.
  Den rose beside her. His voice was thin at first.
  “I stayed in a room because it gave me a home I couldn't lose. I didn't ask who kept it warm. I won't make that choice again. I'll help ring the bell on Mercy.”
  Rusk reached for him, then stopped. He saw her hand and took it.
  The crew asked about food, water, air, tow lines, sick passengers, the wounded dragon. The questions took hours. Elian answered some. Mira answered the medical ones. Anet changed two parts of the plan when a barge steerer pointed out how the current could swing the tow across the ark's stern. June showed the gate marks to everyone who might have to work below. Pell held the slate while she drew.
  At last Anet asked for the decision. Those who wished to leave at once could take the barge to the Reach under Rusk's spare crew. They would be supplied. Those who stayed would help the ark hold position at the basin while a small team entered the keep.
  No one cheered. People moved to one side or the other, spoke to family, changed places, checked names. Six chose the Reach. One changed his mind after talking to his wife, then changed it back. Anet wrote the final list and had each person hear it read.
  Nell watched the barge boat leave. She had wanted everyone to trust the plan. Now she saw that the freedom to go made the trust of those who stayed worth more.
  After the vote, the man who had asked why they should risk so many for one came to Nell at the rail. His name was Fenn. His wife lay in the galley with burns along one arm. He had chosen to stay because the barge would move too much for her, not because he trusted the keep. He wanted that written down.
  Nell brought the slate.
  “You don't need to agree with us to receive care,” she said.
  “I know people say that.”
  “Then watch whether we mean it.”
  He stood beside her while she wrote his reasons in his own words. At first he kept looking toward the others, as if someone might take offense. No one came. Anet passed on her way to the helm, read the line, and asked if he had anything to add.
  “My wife hasn't chosen,” he said. “She's asleep.”
  “We won't wake her for a vote,” Anet answered. “When she wakes, Mira tells her the plan. If she wants to leave, we find the safest way we have. You don't have to choose for both of you.”
  Fenn looked down at the deck. “She would say that.”
  “Then we'll let her.”
  He gave a small unwilling smile. Anet moved on. Nell put the slate where the other decisions could be read. A plan could have room for dissent without falling apart. She had known that in words before. It was harder, and better, to see it made into a place on the wall.
  In the galley, they were making tags for packs. Mira asked everyone to put the things they could not bear to lose into one small bundle. Not because they meant to abandon the ark, but because a hurried hand could carry only so much. The bundles would go in two dry lockers near the boats, each marked with a name.
  A woman brought a stone. Another brought a folded shirt. One man had nothing and kept apologizing as though he had failed the task. Senn was not aboard yet, but Rusk had brought clean cloth from the Reach, and Mira gave him a square of it to wrap whatever he chose later. He put it in his pocket with grave care.
  June brought two bundles, one for Pell and one for herself. Pell opened his and took out a broken spoon.
  “You don't need to keep it because I do,” he told her.
  “It was Mother's.”
  “It was from Vale's drawer.”
  June stared at it. She had carried the spoon for months. In the long waiting, it had become part of a story she needed, a thing their mother had touched. Pell knew because he had watched her take it from Mercy's galley. He had not corrected her before.
  Nell stayed a few steps away. This was not a truth she could make gentle by stepping into it.
  At last June set the spoon on the table. “I still want it.”
  “All right,” Pell said. “You can want it for being ours.”
  She wrapped it again. Then she put a small piece of blue wool into his bundle, shed by the goat and wound into a soft ring. Pell grinned and pushed it against his cheek. Neither needed the wool to belong to a lost life. It came from one they were living.
  Elian watched from the doorway. Nell went to him, and he showed her the pack tag he had made. Her name was written on one side, his on the other.
  “We need separate packs,” she said.
  “I know. I made two tags.”
  “Then why both names?”
  “So whoever finds one knows who to ask for.”
  She held the tag a moment. It was a small offer of a future in which someone could find her without needing a voice behind a wall. She gave it back for him to tie.
  Across the deck, the crew checked the boats. One woman sang while she worked. The tune wandered and repeated, changed by words she could not recall. No one corrected her. Nell listened until she knew a part of it, then took up the line where the singer ran out of breath.
  They did not sing because the journey was safe. They sang because a day could contain fear and still have work worth doing in it. When the first boat was lowered, Fenn came from the galley with a coil of line over his shoulder. He had not changed his mind about the keep. He had found a job he was willing to help with. Nell thanked him for that, and for nothing more.
  Iven volunteered for the landing team. Lio objected before he finished.
  “You aren't going to spend your way out of the inquiry with your life.”
  Iven looked at him. “I know the old gate controls. That's why I asked.”
  Elian checked the chart. Iven was right. His past work had given him the very knowledge they needed. Nell saw Anet weigh the two truths, neither canceling the other.
  “You come,” she said. “You stay on a line with Lio. You follow orders when you think a grand gesture would be finer.”
  “I will.”
  “Don't promise it to me. Say it to him.”
  Iven turned to Lio and said it. Lio nodded once.
  The landing team would be Elian, Nell, Lio, Iven, and Vale. Mira would remain with Ember and the sick. Anet would hold the ark. Rusk and Den would take Mercy's bell, with two crew who knew how to leave the wreck if it rolled. June and Pell would stay aboard, keeping the tide slate and the pressure jug. No child would go into the keep because an adult had failed to learn a sign.

  “Yes.”

  “Lio has a second reel. We checked.”

CHAPTER 18
The Stair Inside the Wave
Mercy's bell rang once, deep enough to shake dust from the ark's beams. Rusk stood beneath it with Den at the pull rope. The sound went through the sea in a broad clear band. Ahead of the small boat, water sank from a stone arch. Beyond it, steps curled upward through a wall of moving blue.

  “We tell each other,” she said.
  He took her hand. “All of it.”

CHAPTER 19
The Hall of Lives Not Lived
Orren would not let them climb at once. She made them eat, drink, and tell her how long they had been awake. Elian resented it until he saw his own hand tremble around the cup. Hunger had begun to feel like resolve. Fatigue had begun to feel like a clear mind. He ate the thick root stew she gave him and found that the bell's hum no longer seemed quite so much like a voice.
  Nell sat across from him, her boots off, drying one sock by a pipe. Vale stood near the door under Lio's eye. Iven worked with Orren on the broken bronze rod. It had failed at an old joint, not in the solid metal. Iven showed her a way to brace it with two strips cut from the boat's spare fittings. She tried the fit, asked him why he had put the strip that way, then nodded at his answer.

  “Yes. If the basin turns before the outer bell, the room keeps the last open point. Whoever is inside gets speech through in scraps. Small matter too. Dust. A drop. A feather if the pull is strong.”

  “No. It can show a held life, but a hand room holds a body. If you hear breath from behind your wooden door, someone is breathing there.”

  “Not yet.”

  “Then stay here until you do.”
  He turned away. They climbed.

  “She stayed.”
  “No.”
  He moved toward the arch. Lio held the line, but Vale fought him with a strength born of years. Nell stepped into his path and spoke his name. He looked through her.

  “I don't know.”

  “It meant everyone,” Orren said.

CHAPTER 20
The Bell That Had No Tongue
The clapper weighed more than six people could lift. It had been cast as a long bronze pear, thick at the base and narrow at the neck. Its chain had sunk into the floor where salt had eaten the stone. Elian walked around it twice, measuring with a cord. Above, the empty bell waited in its frame. The ring from which the clapper had hung was whole.
  “We don't lift it,” he said. “We lower the bell.”
  Orren stared at him, then at the frame. “That hasn't moved in a hundred years.”
  “It has pins. They were meant to come out.”
  “So were teeth,” Lio muttered.

CHAPTER 21
The Woman in the Lower Gate
The cry came again before they reached the basin. Nell heard a name inside it, shouted by Orren, who had passed them on the stair. Water covered the lower yard to the depth of a knee. The roots in their bowls shivered. At the far gate, a woman clung to a bar with one arm while the current dragged her legs through a slot beneath it.

CHAPTER 22
The Room Beside the Wrong Hour
The hand room smelled of old wood, stone dust, and salt. Nell set her lamp on the bench and laid the bronze rod beside it. The round hatch stayed open behind her.   She meant the test she had just seen in the glass beside the route: two versions of them choosing haste, one door flung wide, water taking the galley. A held possibility, not a life already lived. She pressed so hard the nib tore the paper. Then she cut her thumb on the frame and left a dark mark beneath the line.
  The ink faded. Far away, on Mercy's table, a blank journal began to receive it.
  

CHAPTER 23
The Work on the Other Side
Elian saw the hatch narrow with Nell still kneeling beyond it. He reached for her, but Orren caught his belt and pulled him back. The space where his hand had been filled with rushing water. Then the hatch showed a dry stair. Then nothing but gray stone.
  “Nell!”
  His voice struck the room and came back thin. He went to the middle wheel. It had locked at half a turn. The bronze rod sat true in both slots. She had done the work. Something else had moved first.
  Orren set her hand on the pressure plate. “Mercy's bell is out of count.”
  “Then we bring it in.”
  “We need the whole bell too.”
  “Signal the ark.”
  Lio was already at the return line, giving the agreed pulls. Two short, one long: keep position, route fault. He waited, then sent the count again. The reply came after a long pause. Anet had felt the basin turn. The ark was holding. Mercy's stern had begun to break away.
  Elian knelt by the hatch. His hands wanted to tear, wrench, strike. None of those acts would help. He pressed his palms to the cold floor until they stopped shaking enough to hold a pencil.
  “Tell me the mechanism,” he said to Orren.
  She did. The hand room followed the last dry ship face if its outer gate shut before the three bells matched. Nell had seated the rod, but the service cord had drawn a surge from the moving bearer. The room had caught the ark at the start of its crossing. Her body remained in the chamber. The chamber's ship face now touched different hours as the tide moved.
  “Is she safe?”
  “She's dry. She has a vent. I don't know how much time she'll feel.”
  “What brings her to our hour?”
  “A living mark from the ship side, a balanced basin, and three bells.”
  “What is a living mark?”
  “The hand plate in the room. Someone outside holds it open while she sets hers inside.”
  Elian looked at the chart. He could see it now: the small open-hand sign on the wooden door's frame, painted over under the garden roots. They had studied the door but not the brace beside it. All this time the answer had lain a finger's width from where Nell had pressed her palm.
  “Then I go back to the ark.”
  Orren nodded. “Take the corrected measure. I stay at the heart bell. Iven can work the basin.”
  Lio objected. Iven did not ask him to stop. He took the slate, read the turns aloud, and asked Orren to watch him repeat them on a spare valve. He made one mistake. She corrected it. He did it again until his hands knew the order.
  Elian saw the cost of leaving him. If Iven failed, every old judgment would seem proved. If he stayed out of fear of that judgment, they would lose the one person who could make the passage work. He put the pencil in Iven's hand.
  “Write each turn before you make it. Call it to Orren. Nothing from memory.”
  Iven nodded. “I can do that.”
  “You don't have to do more.”
  “I know.”
  Lio stayed with him. Their line remained tied between them, not as a leash but as the rule both had agreed to keep.
  Vale asked to come back with Elian. Orren refused at first. The man knew the route too well, and she had seen what he did when afraid. Vale listened without defending himself.
  “The last service cord runs through Mercy's bell frame,” he said. “If the stern falls, it will drag the frame under. I can release the cord from the wreck side.”
  “Rusk is there.”
  “She doesn't know the release. I sealed it under the floor.”
  Orren looked at him with a tired disgust that had no heat left in it. “Of course you did.”
  Elian let him come. He tied Vale to the spare line and kept the lamp himself.
  The stair back had changed. Two landings were missing. At the dark seam, the peg with three marks lay split in half. Elian used the line and the pressure tube to choose their pace. He would not run. Nell had asked him not to turn fear into an order. Now he had to keep that promise when she could not see him do it.
  At the boat, he found a fresh scratch in the stone. An open circle, the mark Nell had used for the silent rooms. Beside it, in her hand, were four words.
  Still here. Keep learning.
  He touched the letters. They were wet at the edges. She had reached this place from another turn of the room. She had chosen words to help him work, not to make him leap.
  He climbed into the boat and went back to the ark.
  Anet met him at the rail. One look at his face told her enough. She cleared the lower passage, sent Mira for more lamps, and had June bring the tide slate. Pell carried the green cup. No one told him to. He had seen where it belonged.
  Elian went straight to the brace beside the door. Beneath three coats of old paint, he found the hand mark. The wood around it was not wood at all, but a thin plate set into the grain. It warmed under his palm.
  “Nell,” he said.
  Three faint taps answered.
  He laughed once, with a sound close to pain. “I'm here. I found the plate.”
  The voice came from far away. “Don't hold it yet. Not until the low mark.”
  “I won't.”
  “You came back.”
  “Yes.”
  A pause. Then, so softly he almost missed it: “I knew you would. I still needed to hear it.”
  He sat down. Anet left two crew at the stair and made the rest go back to work. June set the tube where Elian could see it. Pell put the cup within reach and withdrew without a word.
  Mira checked Elian's foot. The wound had opened again. He had not felt it. She washed and bound it while he kept his eyes on the plate.
  “You can look at me,” she said. “The door won't vanish.”
  He did. Her face was kind and stern.
  “She needs you fed and awake. So do we.”
  He ate what she brought. He drank from the green cup. Behind the wood, Nell gave a small sound that might have been a laugh.
  For the first time since she entered the room, he could imagine a morning after it.
  

CHAPTER 24
The Names the Sea Could Not Keep
Nell slept in short scraps. Each time she woke, the round hatch showed a different place. Sometimes it was the basin. Sometimes a shelf in Mercy's store. Once it opened onto the stair inside the wave, where she scratched a message for Elian before the pressure drew her back. She never crossed beyond the length of the cord tied round her waist. The room was not kind, but it had edges she could learn.
  The lamp used oil at a steady rate. Her hunger came at no steady rate at all. A whole day of voices might pass through the wooden door while she felt only the span of a meal. Then she would sit through what felt like hours and hear nothing outside but one slow breath. She began to count by the wick. Three marks on the glass shade meant rest. One meal at every second mark. Water before thirst. She wrote the rules in large letters and pinned them where fear could not hide them.
  The record frame took her ink and sent it into the keep's shared route log. She learned that by tracing a line along its edge. The words appeared on a thin plate above the round hatch: copies, not commands. The ship used the log to make signs, lists, and pages where a traveler could find them. It could hold a coat, a table, a pair of boots, all shaped from the stores of a kept road. It could not make a living mind. For that it drew on a person, or trapped one.
  Nell understood why Vale had found the journal blank. Its pages belonged to this route before her words reached it. They had not come from some perfect future book. They came from a woman choosing what to write with a dwindling pot of ink.
  She wrote the facts she had seen. The east beach. Senn's broth. Lio and the driftwood. Her burned tongue. She wrote them because small facts helped people test a large claim. Then she stopped. She would not fill the pages with orders. Elian and Anet needed room to think.
  When the route aligned with the night after the fire, she wrote in blue ink from a second pot she found beneath the tray. We saved the west row because he learned to crawl. She smiled at the memory of Ember hauling beams into the sea. The words were not a prophecy. They were gratitude, sent backward to people still too tired to feel it.
  At another turn, she heard herself angry about the garden. Nell had known this talk would come. It still hurt to receive it from the other side.
  “You could have warned me,” her earlier self said.
  Nell wanted to explain everything. The room took her breath before she could form more than a few words. She said she thought she had warned her, then heard how poor that sounded. So she apologized. Not to a younger fool who did not yet understand, but to a woman entitled to be angry at what had been asked of her.
  After the voice faded, Nell sat with her head in her hands. Knowing more did not make her better. It gave her more chances to do harm with good reasons. She thought of Vale and felt, for the first time, a fear of becoming like him that had nothing to do with his power.
  Before she rested, the wooden door grew hot. Elian called from outside, not in the measured voice she had learned to answer, but with a raw urgency that brought her to her feet. He said the galley was flooding. He said Anet was gone. He begged her to open because he could not reach the plate.
  Nell put her good hand on the door's lower catch. It had not been there a moment before. A narrow bar now lay across the wood, polished where many hands had touched it. The voice outside knew her name, her fear, the exact pace of his breath after running. She lifted the bar a fraction.
  The pressure tube shot upward.
  Water hissed through the seam. Nell dropped the bar. The floor jerked beneath her. For one breath she saw the galley beyond the wood, full of water, bowls turning in the current, Elian reaching for someone she could not see. It was the held possibility from the glass, offered now as a thing she could prevent by doing exactly what would make it real.
  She backed away and hit the bench with her legs.
  Outside, his voice changed. It told her she had never loved him enough. Then it became her own voice, calling her cruel. The room had found the answer she would run toward and used it as a shape for fear. It did not need to be alive to do harm. A flood did not hate the people it drowned.
  Nell sat on the floor and named five things. The bench. The lamp. The scarf. The tube. The bread. The voice went on. She named them again. By the third time, she could hear the absence in it: no task she could check, no fact beyond her own knowledge, no pause to let her answer.
  When the wood cooled, she wrote the attempted opening on her private leaf. She had nearly done the thing she warned against. She had paid for it with a surge through the held routes. In the waiting hall, which she would find later, the lamps had dimmed and a child had cried. She did not know that yet. She only knew the tube had risen, the room had drawn power, and her fear had almost become someone else's danger.
  She took the square of Elian's sleeve from her pocket. It was a plain piece of cloth, with a cut she had watched him choose. She held it against her face until she could breathe at a steady pace. Then she made a new rule beneath the others: a loved voice still has to answer questions.
  There were two white feathers in the pack lining, one bent near the quill. The four-winged bird had perched there while June helped check her supplies before the keep. Its quills had caught in a seam. Nell had meant to give them to Pell, then forgotten. She set the bent one beside the drain and watched it lift in the narrow pull. It vanished. A damp patch appeared beneath the wooden door at an earlier hour. She remembered finding a feather there and felt the room's strangeness turn, for once, into a small practical link.
  She folded the second feather and fed it through the drain when the tube reached the mark from the garden test. It went slowly. She could hear water running somewhere above, then June's voice asking about the bird. Elian had received proof that a small object could cross. She had not sent a promise. She had sent something he could measure.
  The next time the wood warmed, she heard her earlier self ask what she needed. Nell gave the warning about love and harm. She did not speak as a woman wiser than the one outside. She spoke as the woman who had just reached for the wrong catch.
  Afterward, she cleaned the water from the floor with her scarf. The cloth came away black with old grit. She wrung it into the drain and thought of every room that had been kept warm by a cost its guests did not see. It was possible to be trapped and still owe care to someone beyond the trap. That thought did not free her. It gave her a reason to look beyond the next voice.
  She turned the record frame face down and rested.
  The next opening showed a hall in the keep. People stood behind glass doors. They were not images as clear as the garden. They moved slowly, with the effort of bodies in deep water. One woman lifted a hand. A child slept against her shoulder. On the floor beyond them lay the cords that fed the kept roads.
  Nell brought her lamp to the hatch. “Can you hear me?”
  The woman nodded.
  “My name is Nell. Do you know where you are?”
  “The waiting hall,” she said. Her voice came as if from a long pipe. “They told us to wait for a ship.”
  “How long?”
  “I don't know.”
  Nell asked their names. The woman answered. Others came close. A man with a split brow. Two sisters. An old woman who could not stand without help. All had crossed broken gates. All were real enough to be cold, hungry, and afraid. The keep had held them when their roads failed, then never let them finish the crossing.
  “How many?” Nell asked.
  They counted, corrected, counted again. Twenty-three. She wrote each name on her own leaf, not the record frame. She asked who needed to be carried. Four. Who could help? Most of the rest, if they were given food.
  She promised no more than she knew.
  “There is an ark outside. I'm trying to bring this room to its hour. When I can speak to them, I will tell them you're here.”
  The woman began to thank her. Nell held up a hand.
  “Tell me the latch first.”
  They showed her a bar set high on the glass. It could be opened from the hall side only. The route was keeping them as a living store of light, not a prison in the old sense, but no less a prison for that. Nell looked for the open-hand mark. Someone had scratched it away.
  When the wooden door warmed, she went to it and called Elian. The voice that answered was from a day before the keep, asking about the clear glass. She could not give him the new facts yet. His questions had not caught up. She told him where to put the shard and how to light it. Then she sat with the list of twenty-three names in her lap and waited for the right hour.
  The waiting was worse than the cold. She could do work. She could bear pain. She did not know how to bear knowing help was near and having no way to place the news in its hands.
  At last Elian's present voice came through.
  “I found the plate.”
  She told him not to hold it yet. She heard him say he had come back. She let herself ask for that reassurance, though she knew he had. Then she gave him the names, the hall's position, the latch, and the four who needed carrying. She heard a pencil move on the other side.
  “We have no spare beds,” he said.
  “I know.”
  A long pause. Not refusal. Counting.
  “We can put the able on the barge after the tide settles. Anet will decide the load. Tell them we are making room, not that the room is ready.”
  “I will.”
  “Nell?”
  “Yes.”
  “Are you eating?”
  She looked at the bread. There was more left than there should have been.
  “I will now.”
  “Not in a moment. Now.”
  She laughed, then obeyed. He listened while she ate. For a few breaths the door did not feel like a wall between worlds. It felt like a table where two people had learned at last to care for each other without calling it a command.
  

CHAPTER 25
A Passage Wide Enough for Others
Anet did not say yes when Elian brought her the twenty-three names. She took the list to the galley, set it beside the stores, and called Mira and the barge steerers. Elian stood by the door while they counted. He wanted to speak for the people Nell had found. He knew she would have asked him to let Anet do the work first.
  The ark could carry them for a short passage if the able slept on deck, if the barge took most of the dry food, and if they abandoned the spare timber stacked near the stern. That timber had been saved for repairs after the crossing. Without it, a broken tow beam or sprung hull would be harder to mend. The choice was not between wood and lives in a neat tale. It was between lives in sight and lives that might depend on the wood later.
  Anet had the crew hear that before she gave the order.
  “We keep the four best pieces. The rest goes on Mercy as a landing stage. We bring people over in groups of five. No one crowds the boat. No one cuts the bearer free until the last group is clear and the three bells have sounded.”
  Elian asked who would go to the keep.
  “Vale, with two crew and a guide line. You stay at the plate. Nell needs to hear the same voice when the room shifts.”
  He nearly objected. Then he saw the trap in his own need to act. A hammer in his hand would feel better than waiting, but better was not the same as useful. He went below and told Nell the plan.
  “Vale is coming?” she asked.
  “Yes. Guarded. Orren will open the hall from her side.”
  “He may see a life he wants again.”
  “He may. We told the crew what happened on the stair.”
  “Good.”
  She sounded tired enough that he could hear the spaces between words. He asked about the lamp. One small fill remained. Water, half a flask. Bread, enough for one more meal if she made herself eat it.
  He closed his eyes. “Don't make it last too long.”
  “I don't know how long long is here.”
  “Then eat the meal now. We'll solve the rest.”

They arrived with every person and tool they had left with.

  “If we cut it, the hall goes dark,” he said.
  “For how long?”
  “I don't know. We have to carry them by our own lamps.”

  “I did.”
  “How long ago?”
  “Too long.”

The child slept through the first half of the route, then woke and screamed at the open sea above the stair. Vale carried the lamp near her face until she could see the next safe place to put her hands.

Lio took the blow, caught the man's wrists, and said his name until he stopped fighting. They put him in the next group with someone he knew. Later he would apologize. For now, the work was to get him across.

CHAPTER 26
Three Bells Across the Water
The whole bell at the Reach rang in a count of nine. Its note crossed the sea thin and clear. Nell heard it through the wall of the hand room, then felt it in the water beneath the floor. She marked each stroke with a line on her leaf. At the ninth, the round hatch showed the basin again. Orren stood there, holding a lamp. She looked straight at Nell for the first time.
  “Don't cross yet,” the tender said. “We're close, but the ship face is still ahead of us.”

  “After Mercy's stroke. The cord carries the note to the basin.”

  “Not yet,” she said.
  “I know.”

  “Clear the boat,” he said.

Den dropped to his knees and hauled, teeth bared. Vale struck the side of the wreck, went under, came up choking, and caught the lower rail.

  Elian put his palm on the plate.
  Nell put hers against the matching place inside. Warmth passed through the wood. For one instant she felt his hand as clearly as if there were no door. The room steadied. The round hatch widened.

  The held roads were beginning to let go.
  

CHAPTER 27
What Had to Be Left Behind
The keep did not fall. It opened. Walls that had seemed solid turned clear, showing rooms held inside other rooms. A field appeared through the bell hall. A harbor lay beyond the work tables. In the lower yard, the roots lifted from their bowls as if drawn toward rain. Orren stood at the basin control with both hands on the wheel and understood that she could not keep every route alive.
  She sent Suri to the chart shelves. “The fixed roads. The ones marked in black. Leave the rest.”
  Suri rose on shaking legs. Iven went with her. They took rolls from the lower shelf, checked each mark, and tied the chosen ones in cloth. Above them, maps of held places began to lose their lines. Blue ink ran toward the edges. Names faded. The paper remained, blank and clean.
  Lio watched a small map show a street he knew. He reached for it, then stopped. It was not his street anymore. It was a kept image of a place that had once mattered. He let it fade and took the coil of return line instead.
  From the ark, Anet signaled that Mercy's crew was safe. The deep bell had gone under. There would be no second try with that part of the mechanism. Orren had one steady balance, held by the heart bell and the hand plate, before the basin found a new level.
  Iven looked at the wheels. “We can lock the fixed road without the kept rooms.”
  “We can,” she said. “But the garden routes will go dark.”
  “The Reach?”
  “The roots will lose their old feed. They have soil. They have stores. They'll need daylight.”
  “Is there a road to it?”
  Orren spread one black-marked chart. It showed a natural channel running beneath the bells toward a long shore. Not a held world. Not a memory. A land where the sea met ground that did not shift with the keep. The route had been left open for the tenders to bring supplies. Vale had used parts of it, then hidden the full chart when he began to tell people only Mercy could carry them safely.
  Iven looked at him through the signal glass on the wall. Vale stood on the ark's deck, wrapped in a blanket, his cut hand bound. He could not hear them. Iven was glad. His anger would be easier to spend on a man than on the control before him.
  “Then we keep that road,” he said. “And the bell lane to the Reach. No more rooms fed by people.”
  Orren read the old rule on the plate. No passage kept by the unwilling. She put her hand over the marks for the held roads and shut their feed.
  Across the keep, the warm rooms went dark.

Nell heard the garden gate close. She smelled onions one last time. Then the hand room showed bare stone where the house had been.

  “Yes. It's opening to the ship side. Not wide enough yet.”
  “Can you leave the pack?”
  “I can leave most of it.”
  “Keep water and the lamp.”
  “And the notes.”
  He smiled despite everything. “And the notes.”
  Anet came down with the fixed chart sent through the keep's signal frame. She laid it where Elian could see without moving his hand. The ark would have to enter the natural channel before the last held routes released. Waiting here would let the basin swing them toward the empty keep.
  “Can the chamber travel with us?” she asked Nell.
  “The ship face can,” Nell answered. “Orren said it caught our hull. It needs the plate held until the room reaches the same hour.”
  Anet looked at Elian's arm. His fingers had begun to cramp. “We set a brace for your elbow. If your hand fails, tell us before it slips.”
  He nodded. June brought a folded blanket. Pell brought a stool. They built a little work place around him, practical and plain. He did not have to be a man who could hold forever. He needed help to hold long enough.

  She reached toward it. Her fingers met a cool flat resistance, like glass made of pressure. She did not push. She marked the width and sat back.

Lio carried the rope reel. Orren took the heart bell's small hand hammer. She had rung the last full stroke before locking the natural route. Now the great bell could rest. The sea would move by its own tide.

  Orren touched the stone once. Then she stepped through the gate.

Nothing burst. No great flame marked its end. The sea closed around it with the hush of a door eased shut by a careful hand.

  “I could have shown them that road years ago,” Vale said.
  “Yes,” Rusk answered.
  He waited for more. She gave him none. At last he bowed his head and went to help pass water along the barge line.
  Above, Ember slept with his chin on Pell's folded coat. His good wing twitched as if he dreamed of a flight his body could not yet make. Nell heard his low rumble through the vent and knew the ark was moving.
  She had not come out. They had not left her. For now, that was enough ground to stand on.
  

CHAPTER 28
The Long Water Home
The natural channel had no walls. It lay across the sea as a band of dull green water, marked on Orren's chart by three fixed currents and a ridge of stone beneath the surface. Anet followed it by soundings, not by the shape of the horizon. Twice the stars beneath them seemed to rise. Once a whole bright field turned sideways. The ark kept its heading. On the barge, the steerers held their tow angle and called changes before the ropes drew hard.
  There was no room for a feast. People slept under tables, beside the garden beds, and in the cleared store room. Mira had made a care list long enough to cover the galley door. Burns, hunger, cold, old wounds, new fear. She asked able passengers to choose work they could bear. A man who could not lift a bucket could count cups. A woman too weak to stand could mend a strap. They were not made to earn their rescue. Work gave some of them a way to stop feeling like cargo.
  Tavi took charge of the children for one watch. June joined her, then fell asleep sitting up with a slate in her lap. Pell covered her feet with his coat and went back to Ember. The bird that cried like a baby perched on the splint and made a soft chirring sound no one had heard before.
  Mira checked the wing. The skin would heal, she said, but not quickly. The long flight muscles had been strained. Ember might fly again. She would not promise when or how far. Nell heard the report through Elian and closed her eyes in relief at the careful words. Hope that could bear a limit felt stronger than the kind that demanded certainty.
  On the first wet watch, the old woman from the waiting hall asked for her chair to be turned toward the door. Mira thought she wanted air. The woman shook her head. Her name was Vessa, and she had spent so long facing a wall that she could no longer tell whether a room had an exit unless she saw it.
  Mira turned the chair. Then she marked the galley door with a strip of white cloth so Vessa could find it in dim light. The old woman watched people pass through for an hour before she slept. No one asked her to be grateful for a freedom her body had not yet learned to trust.
  Elian heard this from below, where he had been holding the plate. It made him think about the sealed room in a new way. He had spent days trying to open it. Nell would have to live after it opened. She might need to see a door for a while. She might hate the sound of a drain. She might sleep too much or not enough. Returning her body to the deck would be the end of one task, not the end of care.
  He asked Mira about it when she came to check him.
  “Ask her,” Mira said.
  “I don't want to give her another thing to solve.”
  “Then ask what helps tonight. Don't make a plan for every night she'll ever have.”
  He nodded. Later, when the room aligned, he asked Nell where she wanted to sleep when she came out. She was quiet for so long he thought the hour had moved.
  “Somewhere I can see sky,” she said at last. “Not all night if it rains. Just when I wake.”
  He could do that. He asked Anet about the small cabin beneath the garden stair, the one with a narrow roof light. It held two chests of spare cloth. They moved the chests. Lio mended the light's catch. Mira set a bed where Nell would not have to turn her injured shoulder to see it.
  None of them called it her room yet. They left it ready.
  Den helped carry the chests, then sat down so abruptly that Lio thought he had hurt himself. He shook his head and stared at the floor. His hands moved over his sleeves as if checking whether the cloth belonged to him.
  “The room kept changing,” he said. “When I got used to one thing, it gave me another. I keep waiting for this to change too.”
  Lio sat on a chest. He pointed to a knot in the floor board. “That's been there since before I came aboard.”
  Den looked at it.
  “And that dent in the stair. I made it with a barrel. Anet still brings it up.”
  Den gave a faint smile.
  “You can learn this place,” Lio said. “It won't get offended if you take your time.”
  They sat until the younger man's hands grew still. Then Lio asked him to check the roof light's catch. Not as a test. As a job that ended when it shut properly. Den opened it, closed it, and found a small snag in the wood. He smoothed it with a scrap of rough stone.
  In the galley, Rusk saw him come back with dust on his sleeve. She started to ask where he had been, then stopped. He told her anyway. She listened without trying to fit every small fact into proof that he was well. He could be better and still need help. He could love her and still want an hour alone. Both had lessons to learn that no bell could teach in one stroke.
  That evening, a woman began a list of things the new shore would need. A washing place. A latrine well away from the water drawn for drinking. Shade for the sick. A dry store. Someone added a place to sit where no work was asked. The suggestion drew a few tired smiles, then Mira put a firm line beneath it.
  Nell heard the list through the door. She asked Elian to add a peg near the bed for her cup. He wrote it beside the larger tasks, where it could be seen. A home would not appear whole at the end of the voyage. They would make it by noticing what a person needed and finding a sound place to put it.
  Elian held the plate through the first watch, then learned how to share it. Orren found that the mechanism needed a living pulse, not one particular hand. Anet took ten minutes while he flexed his fingers. Mira took a turn. Lio sat for half an hour, speaking to Nell about dull things: a pot that had cracked, a goat that had escaped, the exact insult the barge steerer had given a bad knot. Nell laughed until her shoulder hurt.
  When Elian returned, she told him she had missed those things.
  “What things?”
  “The things nobody puts in a journal because they think the next person won't need them.”
  He looked at the book beside him. Its pages held danger, routes, warnings. He took a pencil and wrote about the goat. The sentence was crooked because his hand still shook. Nell asked him to read it aloud. He did.
  The ark reached Senn's beach near the end of the second watch. Anet had not meant to stop, but the natural channel ran close enough for a boat, and Senn had kept a true bell burning for strangers. Leaving her without news would be a choice too.
  Rusk went ashore with Orren and two crew. They took a copy of the fixed chart, food, and the news of Mercy. Senn stood by the cradle with her gray scarf in her hands. She listened without interrupting. When Rusk told her the held rooms had gone dark, she sat down.
  “I knew they weren't him,” she said at last. “Most days.”
  No one told her that knowing should have made it easy.
  Orren showed her the road to the Reach and the shore beyond. The islands could send boats along it in ordinary tide. They would no longer have to buy promises from Mercy. The roots would need sunlight, and the people would have to move some gardens onto barges, but the route was fixed. Rusk had sent the chart and the news back with the fastest boat. The six who had chosen to stay would have a real task waiting, not a punishment for caution.
  Senn looked toward the ark. “Is the woman out?”
  “Not yet.”
  “Then take this.”
  She untied the small bell from the cradle. Rusk hesitated.
  “Keep your beacon.”
  “I have another bell. This one has spent long enough calling people who never come. Let it mark someone who does.”
  She packed the broth pot, the clean cloths, and the carved bird. The cradle she left under the shed roof, with a note that food and water would be brought by the Reach. Two volunteers from the ark chose to stay until the first supply boat came. Senn did not leave a place of care empty merely because her own waiting had ended.
  She came aboard in the second boat. Nell heard her voice in the galley and began to cry before she knew why. Elian brought the little bell below and set it near the green cup.
  “She says you owe her a bowl,” he told Nell.
  “I'll wash it myself.”
  “I told her that would be a fine day.”
  The room's hatch had widened enough to show both his knees. Nell could see the bowl, the cup, the edge of the slate. She put her fingers to the pressure skin. This time it yielded a little. A thread of warm air crossed from the ark. She smelled bread.
  Not the sea's remembered bread. Bread Mira had baked from the Reach's oats and the ark's last sweet root. It had scorched at one edge because she had been called away to a fever. Nell knew the bitter scent of it and loved it.
  She asked Elian to put a crumb through the drain. He did. It emerged in her cup, wet but whole. She ate it slowly. The room was drawing near enough to pass more than dust.
  At dusk, the shore bearer surfaced beside the ark.
  No one had called it. It kept pace for a while, its pale back scarred and empty. Ember raised his head and gave a low note. The bearer answered. Nell felt the sound through the floor and the wood against her palm.
  Rusk stood at the rail. Vale came near but kept a distance. The creature turned one great eye toward the ship. Its scarred film had begun to clear at the edge. For a moment Vale lifted his bound hand as if to ask forgiveness. Then he lowered it. Whatever passed between him and that life did not need a witness to make it useful.
  The bearer dived. Its tail sent a long swell beneath the ark and barge, lifting both in a slow easy motion. Ahead, the green channel bent toward a break in the bright horizon. Through it Nell saw no stars, no held garden, no house. Only gray cloud over a dark strip of land.
  Anet stood at the helm and smiled for the first time in many days.
  “Rain,” she said.
  The crew moved to secure the deck. They tied cloths over the sleeping places and brought buckets for fresh water. Children held out their hands. On the lower deck, Nell lifted her face toward the vent as the first cool drop found its way down.
  It struck her cheek. She did not know which hour it came from. For once she did not need to.
  

CHAPTER 29
The Hand That Came Through
Land did not make them safe at once. The bay had a reef near its mouth, and the rain hid the gap until Lio sounded it from the small boat. Anet brought the ark through at half speed. The barge swung wide, its rear line drawing hard. A steerer called the turn. Iven eased the second tow and took it round the stern post. The barge came straight behind them, close enough that a child on its deck reached toward the ark as if she could touch it.
  Beyond the reef lay a long gray beach, a slope of grass, and low hills dark with trees. Water ran from the hills in three narrow streams. Birds wheeled above the bay. Nothing in the view shifted when Anet turned her head. She looked twice anyway.
  They anchored in the lee of a low point. The chain took the load. For the first time since the crossing, the ark rested in water that moved without answering a bell.
  No one was allowed ashore until the bay had been checked. This caused the first angry argument of the new land. Anet stood through it with rain running off her coat. People who had spent years waiting could bear one more hour, she said. They could be furious with her while they bore it.
  Rusk laughed. Then she went to help sound the landing.
  Below the garden, the pressure tube stood at its low mark. The hand plate no longer pulled. Orren checked it three times, then sat back on her heels. A narrow service hatch had appeared beside the wooden door, its seam following the grain they had thought was only a brace. It was little wider than Elian's shoulders.
  The main door still bore a difference in pressure. Opening it would let the last stored water from the route into the hull. The service hatch had a small equalizing chamber of its own. A person could pass one at a time, with both ends never open together.
  Elian studied it with Orren. Nell listened from the other side. She had enough light to see the inner catch, but her shoulder would not let her lift it. She tried with her right hand across her body. The catch moved, then fell back.
  “I need another hand,” she said.
  Elian looked at Anet. “I go in. We set the catches together. We come out through the narrow hatch.”
  Orren held up a finger. “The room still has one old alignment. If it turns while you're inside, you'll both be beside the first ship face.”
  “For how long?”
  “Until the last water drains. It could feel like minutes or hours. We can keep air moving from here.”
  Anet checked the drain valves. “Then we test air before you go.”
  They did. Nell held a strip of cloth by the vent and described its movement. Mira sent warm dry air from a bellows. Elian listened to each count. They tested the narrow hatch with a tied bundle first. It passed in, returned, and carried Nell's chalk mark on the same side. Orren measured the pressure again.
  No one spoke of destiny. They spoke of hinges, air, water, time, and the work of two hands.
  Elian packed a lamp, bread, a flask, a spare wick, and a small bar. Nell asked him to bring the green cup. He smiled.
  “It has become a great traveler.”
  “It still holds water.”
  He put it in the pack. Pell gave him Senn's little bell, then pulled it back.
  “Will it make the wrong bell?” he asked.
  Orren shook her head. “Not now. The road is resting.”
  Pell handed it over. “Then she can ring when she's ready.”
  June had the slate. She read the steps back to Elian without looking at the marks. He thanked her. She told him to say it when he came out, and he promised only to try.

  “No sudden great deeds,” she said. “Go slowly. Tell her where you're hurt.”
  “I will.”
  He kissed the fingers of his free hand and put them to the wood. Nell did the same inside, though he could not see it. Then he crawled into the service hatch.
  The outer catch shut behind him. He turned the equalizing wheel, waited until its small tube cleared, and lifted the inner catch. Warm stale air touched his face. He saw the bench, the lamp, the leaves full of notes. Nell sat beside them with one arm held close to her body.
  For a moment neither moved.
  He had imagined finding her unconscious, or lost beyond speech, or made strange by too much time. Instead she looked thin, dirty, tired, and entirely herself. There was ink on her chin. One lace had come undone. She was smiling with tears running down her face.
  “You brought the cup,” she said.

  When they parted, he checked her shoulder. She checked his foot. Each scolded the other a little. The small familiar quarrel felt like a piece of home neither had seen in the glass.
  Elian gave her water. She drank, then ate bread while he set the inner catch. It lifted with two hands. Beneath it was a second pin, bent by the room's long strain. He used the bar to ease it straight. Nell held the lamp, her hand steady now that someone else could bear the force.

  Then the floor moved.
  Elian caught the bench. Nell caught his sleeve. The gap beyond the hatch changed from the lower deck to a dark passage. The vent drew cold air. From the wooden door came the sound of a knife being set on a table far above.
  Nell went still.
  “The first night,” she said.
  He listened. A chair scraped in the galley. Somewhere above, his own younger voice asked about a torn sleeve. The room had caught the first alignment before draining its last store of water.
  Outside, their earlier selves had not yet heard a knock.
  Elian looked at Nell. Her eyes moved to the wooden door, then to his hand. They both knew what had not happened yet from this side.
  He could do nothing and wait. Perhaps the room would find another branch. Perhaps their earlier selves would never come below, never miss the straight channel, never find the wreck. The sea did not order him. It offered no certainty. On the bench lay the list of twenty-three names, Pell's borrowed bell, the cup, the bandage for his foot.
  Nell shook her head before he could speak.
  “Not because it happened,” she whispered. “Because someone needs to hear it.”
  Elian went to the door.
  

CHAPTER 30
The First Knock
Elian put his fist against the wood and struck three times.
  He waited. On the other side lay the ark's first night on the sea, the loaf half cut, his sleeve still only torn, Nell standing in the galley with the knife in her hand. He could almost see them. He had lived that waiting from outside. Now he knew whose hand had made the sound.
  His own.
  He struck three times again. Nell stood beside him, holding the lamp. She had already spoken across this moment once when the room first caught it. The route carried her words in fragments, out of order, but the knock had needed a body close to the ship face. Her hurt shoulder had kept her from making it. His hand could reach.
  Feet moved on the stair. He heard the small scrape of their old lamp against the rail. Then the younger Nell touched the door.
  The room warmed. Its stored speech found the opening. Her words from the earlier turn of the chamber passed through: keep your hand there. The warning about the straight channel followed. Elian heard his own past breath catch on the other side.
  He did not try to add more. He could have shouted that he was here, that they would live, that the woman was Nell. But he did not know what such a shout would change. More than that, he knew the people outside needed facts they could test, not an order dressed in a loved voice. He put his open palm against the wood and let them ask their questions.
  Nell leaned on his shoulder. “Now we can go.”
  “Can we?”
  “The room has nothing left to keep from this hour.”
  She showed him the record frame. Its surface had gone plain. The words she had chosen had reached the shared log. The feared lives, the warm chairs, the drowning deck had been held possibilities, powered by the keep. They were gone. This hand, this shoulder, this breath beside him had no place in a ledger now. They belonged to the people living them.
  Under the floor, water ran faster. Elian went to the service hatch and checked the tube. Empty. The inner catch moved with the light pressure of his thumb. Beyond it lay the ark's lower deck in the present: June's slate, the folded blanket, Anet's boot at the foot of the stair.
  He did not rush through. He opened the first catch, waited, checked the air, then helped Nell into the small chamber. Her shoulder would not fit easily. He padded the edge with the spare scarf and turned her gently. She cursed him, the hatch, and every builder who had ever saved wood by making a space too small.
  He laughed so hard he had to stop working.
  “Don't you dare,” she said, laughing too.
  They moved one at a time. Nell reached the outer catch. Elian stayed inside until she had shut the inner one. The little bell rang once from her pack.
  Outside, someone cried out. Hands took her. Elian heard Mira giving orders, Anet calling for room, Pell asking whether the cup had come too. Then the outer catch closed. Elian set the inner one behind him, moved into the chamber, and followed her out.
  The lower deck was crowded despite Anet's rules. She gave up trying to clear it for one breath. Nell sat on the bench with Mira at her side. June held the slate against her chest. Pell stood on tiptoe to see. Lio leaned against the stair rail with his eyes shut. Iven sat on the floor as if his knees had simply stopped serving him.
  Elian came out, and Nell reached for him.
  He took her hand. There was no wall between their palms.

The two numbers did not agree. Orren wrote both down. No one tried to force one into the other. Her body had lived the hours it had lived. It would need sleep, food, and time on stable ground.
  For the first night, Elian slept on a pallet beside her bed so he would not hurt her shoulder. Before dawn, Nell reached down and found his hand. He woke at once.
  “Nothing's wrong,” she whispered.
  He held still, learning how to hear that without waiting for the rest.
  “Nothing's wrong,” she said again. “I only wanted you.”
  He stayed awake until she slept. Then, for the first time in many nights, he slept too.
  The new shore was not empty. Smoke rose from a village beyond the hills, and a fishing boat came to look at the ark from a careful distance. Anet went out with Rusk and Orren, carrying no flags but a white cloth and no promises but the facts. They asked for a place to land the sick and water they could draw without harming a field. The people of the shore asked questions of their own. The talk took most of a day.
  By evening, the ark had leave to use the sheltered beach for a season. In return, its crew would help mend a breakwater and share what they knew of the fixed sea road. The agreement was spoken in front of witnesses from both sides and written in two hands. Nell heard it read from her bed and asked whether anyone had checked the tide at the landing.
  Anet laughed and said yes.
  Not everyone chose to stay. Rusk and Den returned to the Reach with a chart, stores, and enough crew to make the voyage safely. The first ferry would bring people who wished to move their gardens into ordinary daylight. Others would keep the islands and trade along the new road. Den did not go because his mother asked. He went because he knew the bells and wanted to help make them useful without making them masters.
  Vale remained aboard under watch until the passengers could hear his records and decide what claims to bring. He gave up the ledger and the keys. No single confession settled the harm. Some people wanted him kept away. Others needed him to show where a name had been entered, where a route had closed, where a grave might be found. He answered in public, with witnesses. He worked when work was asked of him. June and Pell did not have to attend.
  Iven helped copy the records. Once, Nell saw him stop over a page and go outside for air. Lio followed with two cups. They sat on the step without speaking. The inquiry that had begun before this sea would continue, as it should. So would their lives.
  Senn set her broth pot under an awning on the beach. She put the carved bird by the bowls and kept a stool for anyone who needed to sit. The cradle stayed at the old beacon until the Reach's supply crew could bring it. When it arrived, she used it to hold clean blankets. She did not need to explain the change.

Pell told the gulls they were showing off. June made a shade frame for the dragon that did not catch his good wing when he turned.

He did not leap from the deck. He climbed to the low bank beyond the beach, opened his wings, and glided to the sand.

Orren would help them drain and seal the old route in daylight. Elian would not do it alone. Nell would keep the notes.

  “The house?”
  “The morning. The garden. A roof that only needs mending because it rains.”
  “Yes.”
  “We don't have to earn every good thing by almost losing it.”
  He put down the basket and took her hand. The bay darkened. Lamps came on in the village. From the galley came the sound of June arguing that a goat could not be blamed for eating a list placed within reach. Somewhere a pot lid fell, followed by laughter.

  Elian and Nell both turned. Pell stood there with three bowls and Senn behind him, trying not to spill the fourth. No voice from a lost hour. No bargain. Only supper, carried a little too far by willing hands.
  Nell looked at Elian. Then she rose, slowly, and opened the gate.
  “Come in,” she said.

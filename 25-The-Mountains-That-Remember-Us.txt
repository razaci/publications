Chapter 1
The Shape of His Knock
Lazara's pen left a small wound in the paper.
  She had written Eno's name too hard. The nib caught in the second letter, pulled a thread of pulp upward, and tore through the date beneath it. Twenty months gone, and her brother could still make a mess of her desk.
  She put the pen down before she broke it. Outside the window, the lower moon hung over Morrowglass's roofs like a coin somebody had rubbed nearly blank. The other two were behind cloud. Rain rattled in the gutter with the irregular rhythm of a person trying not to knock.
  Then somebody did.
  Two quick taps, a pause, one more. Her body recognized him before she stood. That was humiliating. Eight months of not opening the door to Tarin Vale, and her hand had already moved toward the cup he used to use.
  She stopped it. The cup remained upside down on its hook.
  He waited beneath the narrow awning, water running from his hair into the collar of his gray-green coat. He had a folded sheet in one hand. The other was tucked under his arm, the way he held himself when cold made the old break in his little finger ache.
  She looked at the paper because it was safer than looking at him.
  “Another sighting?”
  “Three.”
  The word came in his real voice. She had forgotten how low it was when he did not want the neighbors to hear. Sometimes in bed she tried to remember him saying her name, and heard only her own imitation. Here he was, dripping on her threshold, taking one of her griefs away without permission.
  She stepped aside.
  Once, he would have gone straight to the cupboard. Now he remained just inside the door, waiting to be told where to put his coat. That hesitation hurt more than the months had taught her to expect.
  “Chair,” she said.
  He draped the coat over its back. A wet mark spread across the wood. She ought to have given him a cloth. Instead she took the paper.
  Three reports. A seamstress at the northern tram stop had seen a light move behind a hill that had no road. A fisherman on Lake Orison had watched an upright shadow interrupt the stars. A freight worker, far east at the salt flats, had heard a voice from a sealed emergency receiver.
  Lazara read the last report twice.
  The worker had transcribed six words: Don't make her come without the scarf.
  Her ochre scarf hung beside the door. Eno had bought it with money he should have spent on rent. He said she dressed like an ink spill and required one tolerable color.
  “Tarin.”
  “I know.”
  “You don't know what I'm asking.”
  He looked at the scarf. “I know who said that first.”
  The room tilted without moving. She sat because her knees had become untrustworthy. On the desk her brother's name lay pierced by the pen. The wound looked deliberate now, an act she had committed while somebody elsewhere might have been breathing.
  “Who took the report?”
  “A railway dispatcher. She copied it before the Directorate collected the original.”
  “The Channel people.”
  “Yes.”
  She hated the relief in the answer. If officials had collected the report, somebody important had thought it worth collecting. She had spent twenty months despising how efficiently importance could substitute for truth.
  “Have you heard the recording?”
  “No.”
  “Have you spoken to the worker?”
  “Not yet.”
  “Then what have you brought me?”
  He accepted the blow with a slight lowering of his chin. She remembered doing that to him often near the end, offering a question with one sharp edge concealed beneath it.
  “A place to begin,” he said.
  She got up and filled the kettle. Her hands shook so badly the lid rang against the tap. He moved, almost instinctively, to take it from her. She turned before he reached her.
  “Don't.”
  “All right.”
  His obedience made her angrier. The old Tarin would have said she was scalding herself and annoyed her into letting go. This careful man was somebody she had helped make.
  While the water heated she examined the map. Tarin had drawn the sightings in pencil, three small crosses along a curve nobody would have called a road. Orison lay between the northern tram and the eastern flats. The curve continued into the Pale Ranges, where Eno's survey party had vanished.
  “You've already plotted it.”
  “Yes.”
  “How long have you had this?”
  “Since this afternoon.”
  She checked the report times. The earliest was four days old. A reasonable delay for a copied railway message. An intolerable amount of time for her brother to have spent alive without her knowing.
  The kettle began its little metallic mutter. She poured two cups without asking. When she reached for his, she paused, furious with herself, and took it down anyway.
  He noticed. Of course he noticed. He had once noticed whether she had eaten by the way she held her shoulders at a desk.
  “Tell me exactly what they saw,” she said. “Don't make it better.”
  He described the hill light, the interrupted stars, the receiver opening on a frequency the freight worker's equipment should not have reached. He distinguished the things the witnesses had said from the things a dispatcher had inferred. He did it plainly, without the old surveyor's pleasure in a mystery. When he reached the voice, he stopped.
  “What?” she asked.
  “The dispatcher said the worker was crying.”
  “Why?”
  “He thought it was his son.”
  She set the cup down. The six words on the page rearranged themselves, no longer a private thread reaching toward her but a thing that might catch on anyone's longing.
  “His son says that about a scarf?”
  “No. He heard the voice before the words.”
  The room smelled of tea and Tarin's wet coat. She wanted to lean against him so badly it became a physical ache along her ribs. She wanted to hit him for coming back with hope instead of an explanation for why he had left her to bury an empty box.
  She had not buried Eno. Their mother had refused a grave with nothing in it. Lazara had despised that refusal for six weeks, then envied it for the rest of the year.
  “We go to the seamstress first,” she said.
  “Lazara—”
  “She's here. She's a person with an address. I am not getting on a train because a receiver knows what I miss.”
  He nodded.
  She fetched her notebook. Inside the back cover lay a photograph she had not looked at since winter: Eno at the river, laughing at something outside the frame. Tarin had taken it. She kept forgetting that part until his thumb appeared at the edge of the print, blurred and enormous.
  She left the photograph where it was.
  Tarin rose to take his coat. Water had made a dark crescent on the floor. He looked at it, then at the cupboard where the cleaning cloths used to live.
  “They're still there,” she said.
  He fetched one. He knelt and wiped the floor. She watched the familiar angle of his neck above his collar and remembered pressing her mouth there when he was trying to work.
  At the door he asked what time.
  “Seven.”
  “I'll be outside.”
  She wanted to say he could come in. She wanted the sentence to mean only warmth and a cup, not forgiveness handed over because she was frightened. She said nothing.
  After he left, the room contained a second cup with tea still in it. Lazara touched its handle with one finger. Then she took down the scarf, wrapped it around her throat, and sat before the wounded page until morning began to pale the window.
Chapter 2
The Woman Who Measured the Dark
Selka had pinned the moon to a dressmaker's form.
  It was only a disk of silver cloth, cut to the size of a hand, but Lazara stopped in the doorway because the woman was sewing it onto a black coat covered with tiny stitches. Stars, dozens of them, arranged in a sky that did not match the one outside.
  “For a wedding,” Selka said. “People marry in the dark now. Apparently daylight makes promises look ordinary.”
  Tarin stood behind Lazara with a bag of breakfast rolls. He had bought the kind she used to like, with fennel seeds that caught between her teeth. She had taken one without thanking him and was still annoyed by its accuracy.
  Selka looked from them to the folded report.
  “You're together?”
  “Investigating,” Lazara said.
  “I meant whether I have to tell it twice.”
  Heat rose beneath Lazara's scarf. Tarin put the rolls on the counter and kept his face admirably still. She wished he had laughed. It would have given her something to forgive quickly.
  Selka shut the shop door. A bell chimed above it, a small cracked sound. Her hands were broad and scarred along the thumb joints. When she poured tea, she kept glancing toward the street, as if a person could arrive there whose absence required continual checking.
  “My son used to come by at this hour,” she said when Lazara noticed. “He went east for work. Ten months ago.”
  “Have you heard from him?”
  “Messages. Less than I'd like. More than some people get.”
  The answer left no invitation for consolation. Lazara opened her notebook.
  Selka had been returning from the tram with a parcel of lining. The upper road was closed by a fallen retaining wall, so she took the path beneath the northern hill. At the third lamp, the light went out. She had looked up, irritated, and seen the hill move.
  “Which part?” Lazara asked.
  “The dark part.”
  She waited. Selka watched her waiting and reached for a length of measuring tape.
  “You think that's imprecise. It is. I know the hill in daylight. At night, without the lamp, I had only its edge. Then its edge was somewhere else.”
  She stretched the tape between two hooks on the counter. Her fingers made a new distance.
  “The stars behind it came back. Not because cloud passed. Because the thing in front of them lowered.”
  “What thing?”
  “I saw a hand.”
  Tarin's breathing changed beside Lazara. She hated how easily she could hear his attention. For eight months she had listened for him outside her door, only to discover that his smallest inhale could still fill a room.
  Selka described five separate lengths of darkness folding behind the hill. One carried a blue light. The light moved across the others, illuminating a surface ridged like skin. She heard no voice. She felt a pressure in her teeth and tasted metal.
  “Were there vehicles?” Tarin asked.
  “After. Black ones, loud tires on the wet road. People with lamps. They asked whether I'd been drinking.”
  “Had you?” Lazara asked.
  “Half a cup at supper. Ask me as if you're interested in the answer.”
  Lazara lowered her eyes to the page. “I am.”
  “Then write half a cup. Not no, because you want me useful.”
  She wrote it. The small correction steadied her more than agreement would have done.
  Selka brought out a piece of stone wrapped in a stocking. It was blue-gray, smooth on one side, oddly porous on the other. She put it in Lazara's palm. The stone was warm.
  “How long has it been inside?”
  “Four days.”
  “Near the stove?”
  “In the window drawer.”
  Lazara held it against her wrist. Warm enough to be undeniable, not hot enough to explain itself. A faint pulse seemed to pass through it. She waited, counted, discovered it was her own heartbeat transmitted through the heel of her hand.
  She set it down carefully.
  Selka's mouth tightened. “I know what you just did.”
  “I needed to know which feeling belonged to me.”
  “And?”
  “The warmth doesn't.”
  The woman's face softened by an amount Lazara could bear.
  Outside, a vehicle slowed. All three looked toward the window. It was a bakery cart negotiating the puddle by the curb. Lazara wanted to laugh and found she could not.
  Selka showed them where she had been standing, walking there with the shop key held between two fingers. Tarin carried the stone inside its stocking. The hill rose above the path, brown with winter grass. A row of trees stood on its crest. In daylight they looked aggressively ordinary.
  At the third lamp, Selka stopped.
  “There.”
  Lazara climbed the bank enough to see the broken retaining wall. Rain had washed a groove through fresh soil. Along one side lay a shallow impression wider than her whole outstretched body. Four others continued toward the trees, diminishing in size.
  They might have been drainage cuts. They might have been old excavations. She stood beside the nearest, trying not to arrange the earth into a hand because Selka had given her one to look for.
  Tarin knelt by the edge. He took photographs with the lamp, the path, and the line of trees visible. He did not call the shapes fingers. Lazara loved him suddenly for that, an involuntary sharp rush that made her turn away.
  A scrap of blue thread caught on a thorn.
  Not light. Not a revelation. Cloth from somebody's coat. She wound it around her finger and felt herself close to tears. She had been ready to believe a giant hand had passed over the town. A single thread made her think of all the human people who had come afterward, walking where she walked, knowing more than they would tell her.
  Selka took it from her gently.
  “That's mine. I cut through here yesterday.”
  Lazara laughed once, badly.
  “I'm sorry.”
  “Don't be. I keep hoping I'll find something I've forgotten dropping.”
  A little farther along the bank stood a bench with one broken slat. Selka sat down as if she had been meaning to stop there all along. Tarin went back toward the impression to take another view from the lamp. Lazara remained beside the bench, uncertain whether the pause had been arranged to separate them.
  “Sit,” Selka said. “Your face is making my legs tired.”
  Lazara sat.
  From there the northern hill looked softer, nearly harmless. Rainwater shone along the old retaining wall. A woman in a red coat walked a dog on the far path, pulling it away from every interesting smell. The town continued around the place where Selka had seen a hand, its daily life making a kind of stubborn refusal to be impressed.
  “Did you tell Rell?” Lazara asked.
  “In a message. He hasn't answered that one.”
  “Does he answer the others?”
  “Sometimes. He tells me whether he needs money more reliably than whether he is cold.”
  Lazara smiled, then looked toward Tarin. He was crouched over the camera, one elbow held away from the mud. The angle of his body was so familiar that she knew how the movement would finish before he made it.
  Selka followed her gaze.
  “Do you want him here?”
  “I want to know whether my brother's alive.”
  “That's a different question.”
  The bench creaked beneath Lazara as she shifted. She disliked being visible to a stranger. She had chosen archive work partly because a page allowed her to look without looking back. Selka seemed to have no respect for that arrangement.
  “I don't know what I can trust him with,” she said.
  “Does he know that?”
  “He ought to.”
  Selka took a pin from her sleeve and pushed it into a little pad attached to her wrist. “People are dreadful at knowing what they ought to. I spend half my life saying a hem is not a moral opinion about somebody's ankles.”
  Lazara laughed despite herself.
  The seamstress looked at the hill. “The night I saw it, I went home and boiled water. I put the kettle on before I took my coat off. Then I sat beside it, waiting for the boil. I thought if I could see one ordinary thing finish, the rest of the night might settle.”
  “Did it?”
  “No. I burned the kettle dry. Forgot to drink anything.”
  There was no neat wisdom in the account. Lazara loved her for telling it anyway.
  “I keep thinking I should have seen more,” Selka said. “A face. An eye. Something that would make people stop asking whether I had a drink. But if it had leaned over the hill, I think I would have died of being looked at.”
  Lazara remembered the copied phrase about the scarf. She had not yet seen anything impossible. Already part of her was demanding a spectacle large enough to make uncertainty cease. Selka had seen one and still had to boil water afterward.
  “What do you want from the investigation?” she asked.
  The woman considered. “To know whether I should keep sending Rell letters the same way. To know the hill isn't going to stand up while the children are on the tram. And to have my stone back. I liked it before it became important.”
  Lazara wrote none of that down. For once the notebook remained in her pocket. She sat beside a woman with stiff knees and a son away from home, watching a person she loved take photographs in the rain.
  When Tarin returned, Selka stood with a little grunt and brushed the wet from her coat. She did not repeat the question about wanting him there. Lazara was grateful. The answer had become harder to hide from herself without anyone requiring her to perform it aloud.
  On the way back, Tarin offered Lazara his arm at a muddy section. She refused, slipped, and caught his sleeve so hard she nearly took him down. He steadied her without a word. The silence gave her no easy way to make the moment into an argument.
  At the shop Selka packed the warm stone into a padded sewing box and entrusted it to Lazara for examination. She wanted a return date. Not someday, not when the investigation allowed. Tomorrow evening unless they called to arrange otherwise.
  Lazara wrote it in her notebook.
  As they left, Selka touched her scarf.
  “Your brother?”
  Lazara looked at her.
  “I read the missing notices. You wore that in the photograph.”
  “Yes.”
  “If you get an answer, tell your mother before the newspapers.”
  The sentence struck somewhere she had protected from useful advice. She nodded because her mouth would not form words.
  Outside, Tarin held out the last roll. It was cold now. She took it and stood beside him beneath the awning, eating fennel seeds from her palm while the northern hill kept its impossible shape to itself.
Chapter 3
The Space Between the Stars
At Lake Orison, the fisherman made them wait while he finished cleaning a fish.
  Lazara stood under a leaking roof and watched his knife follow the bright belly. The ordinary violence steadied her. Here was a body that could be opened and understood, a thing whose silver skin did not conceal a mountain or her brother or the last twenty months of her life.
  Tarin stood where the rain could reach one shoulder. She wanted to tell him to move. He had always done that, given her the dry part of a roof without admitting there was a dry part. She shifted instead, making room. He noticed and stepped under it.
  The fisherman's name was Del. He had no interest in investigators until Lazara showed him the copied report. Then he cleaned the knife, wrapped it, and sat on the crate beside his nets.
  “They left out the smell,” he said.
  “What smell?”
  “Like a storm before the water knows about it.”
  She wrote the words exactly. If she translated them into electricity or ozone, they would sound more scientific and tell her less about what he had actually experienced.
  Del had been setting the night markers when the reflection changed. The water remained clear. The stars above were visible. In the lake, however, a region of darkness rose through the reflected sky, upright and broadening, until it covered the image of the lower moon.
  He looked up. Something stood beyond the ridge.
  “How tall?” Tarin asked.
  Del pointed at the hills. “I don't know. Taller than the question.”
  Lazara glanced at him, expecting a joke. His hands were flat on his knees. He meant it.
  The shape carried points of blue light. Not lamps in a row. They moved beneath its surface, appearing and vanishing in patterns that reminded him of water finding cracks in stone. The shape bent. A pale line opened high above the trees.
  “An eye?” Lazara asked.
  “That's what I thought. You write what I thought, not what it was.”
  She did.
  By evening the rain had stopped. Del let them use the old boathouse while he took his catch to town. He wanted no photographs of his granddaughter, who lived in the house above the dock. He told Tarin this twice. Tarin put the camera away until the man was gone.
  The lake flattened into black glass. Lazara ate bread from Selka's parcel and tried to calculate which part of the ridge could have interrupted the moon from Del's position. Tarin set the lamp between them. Its little circle of warmth brought his face too close.
  “You've lost weight,” she said before she could stop herself.
  He looked up. “So have you.”
  “I didn't invite a competition.”
  “I wasn't offering one.”
  They sat with the lamp. Somewhere under the dock a fish broke the surface, a wet sound like a hand released from skin. Lazara wrapped the scarf more tightly around her throat.
  “Why did you stop coming?”
  The question had waited eight months, then chosen a leaking boathouse and an impossible reflection to emerge.
  “You told me to.”
  “I told you that night.”
  “You told me three times.”
  She looked down at the bread. In her memory the last argument had become a single clean injury: Tarin leaving. Now it spread backward into the things she had said before the door closed.
  “You could have knocked again.”
  “Yes.”
  He did not absolve himself by making her reasonable. She could have hated him less easily if he had.
  He took a small packet from his coat. Inside lay the key she had given him four years earlier. He set it on the board between them.
  “I kept meaning to return it.”
  “Why didn't you?”
  “I wanted a reason to come back.”
  The truth was so small she could hardly breathe around it.
  She did not take the key. He did not put it away.
  Across the water, a light appeared on the ridge.
  Lazara stood too quickly. The lamp rocked; Tarin caught it before it fell. The blue point moved left, paused, and rose. She had prepared herself for a tower, an aircraft, a searchlight. It did none of the things that would have allowed her to name it without becoming afraid.
  Then the stars disappeared.
  Not all of them. A narrow swath above the ridge went dark, as if someone had lifted a piece of the night closer to her face. The edge widened. A second edge rose beside it. Between them a region of stars remained visible, and her mind understood the gap between an arm and a body before she could refuse the understanding.
  “Tarin.”
  His hand found hers.
  For one ashamed instant she felt more relief at that than terror at the thing beyond the ridge. His fingers were cold, the crooked little one pressing awkwardly against hers. Real. Here. She held them hard enough to hurt.
  The shape bent toward the lake. Blue light threaded its surface, revealing a plane of cheek, an enormous closed mouth, the slow angle of a shoulder. Mountain trees stood dark against its chest. The lower moon lit cloud behind its head.
  It was looking down.
  Lazara's stomach seemed to fall through the dock. She could not fit the face into the space her eyes had given it. Something that large ought to have been a landscape. Something with a face ought to have been close enough to hear her breathing.
  A sound traveled through the wood beneath her boots. The water trembled in a thousand tiny circles.
  She tasted blood. She had bitten the inside of her cheek.
  “Do we run?” she whispered.
  Tarin looked at the shore, then at the thing. “Where?”
  The answer made her laugh, one short awful breath. He pulled her closer, not away from the lake but beneath the boathouse beam, as if an old roof could matter to something whose eye was larger than their town.
  White lights came on behind them.
  A woman stood at the path with two other people, none pointing weapons. Gray hair escaped from beneath her hood. She looked first at the ridge, then at their joined hands, then at the lamp Tarin had caught.
  “Do not answer the pressure you feel as though it were a voice,” she said. “It is not a voice you can survive interpreting alone.”
  Lazara stared at her.
  The woman raised a badge. In its center was the small broken-circle mark Lazara had seen on the collected sighting reports: the joint Contact Directorate, staffed through the CIA and FBI in the peculiar jurisdiction that existed wherever a mountain could look back.
  “My name is Rana Quill,” she said. “I operate a human end of the Channel.”
  Beyond the ridge the immense face turned. The night filled its place again, star by star.
  Quill looked at Tarin. “You were supposed to call before bringing her to water.”
  His fingers tightened once around Lazara's.
  She let go.
Chapter 4
The Things Her Mother Kept
Lazara walked home with the lake still trembling inside her teeth.
  Quill had wanted them in a vehicle. Lazara refused so immediately that the woman stepped back. There was no pursuit, no hand on an arm. Quill gave her a card with a number and a place to come at noon, then told Tarin she expected his call. The familiarity in it made Lazara feel naked.
  He followed her along the wet road at a distance she could neither resent nor forgive. Whenever she slowed, he slowed. Finally she turned.
  “How long?”
  “Six weeks.”
  “Working for them?”
  “Taking survey contracts. Trying to get access to the eastern reports.”
  “Trying for whom?”
  He looked as if she had slapped him. She let the question stand because the part of her that still loved him was trying to soften it before he answered.
  “For Eno. For you. For myself, because I was there and he wasn't when we came back.”
  “You could have told me.”
  “I could.”
  The road climbed between dark houses. She could see her mother's window above the bakery, a yellow rectangle with a plant leaning against it. She had not meant to come here. Her feet had chosen the familiar climb while her mind remained at the lake.
  “Go home,” she said.
  “Your home or mine?”
  She almost answered the way she would once have answered, irritated that he could call them different places. Then she remembered the key still on the boathouse board. He had left it there when the lights came.
  “I don't know,” she said, and climbed the stairs alone.
  Adra opened the door in a nightgown with a cardigan buttoned wrong over it. Her white hair had flattened on one side. Lazara had forgotten that other people might sleep while the world changed its shape.
  “What is it?”
  She could not begin with a giant. She began with tea. Her mother looked at her face and put the kettle on without another question.
  The kitchen was too warm. Two little lamps stood by the window, one beneath a glass shade Eno had cracked when he was fourteen. Adra kept turning the crack toward the wall and Lazara kept turning it back when she visited. Neither had admitted the argument existed.
  “I have a report,” Lazara said.
  Her mother gripped the kettle handle. “Don't give it to me kindly.”
  Lazara laid the copy on the table.
  Adra read the six words about the scarf. Her face did not collapse. That would have been easier to watch. It grew attentive in the exact way it used to grow when somebody knocked late with Eno behind them, drunk and delighted to have found his way home.
  “Have you heard him?”
  “No.”
  “Seen him?”
  “No.”
  “Then we have a report.”
  “Yes.”
  Adra sat down. Her thumb pressed the edge of the page flat. Lazara had prepared herself to keep her mother from hoping. Now she wanted to take the cold restraint out of her hands and throw it against the wall.
  “I saw something at the lake.”
  She told it badly: the missing stars, the enormous face, Tarin's hand. She left out the last part at first, then included it because her mother had known too many versions of her life polished for an audience.
  Adra listened. When Lazara finished, she went to the cupboard above the stove. She took down a biscuit tin and opened it on the table.
  Inside lay Eno's things: a tram ticket, a button, a strip of paper with a bad drawing of a bird. Beneath them was a little blue-gray stone.
  Warm.
  Lazara's breath stopped.
  “He sent it before the expedition,” Adra said. “From the first survey station. He said not to put it with the seeds.”
  “Why didn't you show me?”
  “You wouldn't look in the tin.”
  The answer was true. She had called it a shrine once, with a cruelty she had since folded into the part of her memory labeled grief. Her mother had not argued. The tin had gone back above the stove.
  Lazara picked up the stone. It was smaller than Selka's, striped with a thread of pale mineral. Warm enough to have come from a pocket, though the tin had stood beside flour in a cold cupboard.
  “Did anyone else see it?”
  “Tarin. Before he left with the party.”
  The name hurt again. She was running out of different places for it to hurt.
  Adra took the stone from her and placed it beside the report. “If you find him, don't tell him how long I kept this.”
  “Why?”
  “He'll feel obliged to become the kind of son who deserved it. I'd rather have the old one back.”
  Lazara began to cry.
  It happened without the preliminary pressure she had learned to control. One moment she was sitting with a stone in front of her; the next she could not see it. Adra moved beside her, put an arm around her shoulders, and said nothing useful at all. Lazara pressed her face into the wrong-buttoned cardigan. It smelled of soap, stale flour, and the little lavender bag her mother kept in a drawer.
  “I don't remember his voice properly,” she said.
  “I do.”
  “Tell me.”
  “He complained. Constantly. Higher when he wanted something. Lower when he had already done it.”
  Lazara laughed through the tears. “That's not a voice.”
  “It is the one I miss.”
  They sat until the kettle cooled. Outside, a cart began climbing the bakery lane. Morning belonged to other people again.
  Before breakfast, Adra opened Eno's old room to fetch a blanket.
  Lazara followed her to the doorway and stopped. The room was not preserved. She had expected it to be, and the expectation embarrassed her. Clean linen occupied one shelf. A sewing basket stood on the bed. Eno's old books remained, but a chair had moved beneath the window where Adra could mend in good light.
  “You use it,” Lazara said.
  “It's a room.”
  “I know.”
  Her mother put the basket on the floor. Beneath it, the blanket bore a patch darker than the rest. Lazara remembered Eno spilling glue there while making a model boat. She had threatened to tell Adra, and he had given her the largest orange in the kitchen to buy silence. Their mother had found the patch within an hour and said nothing. Lazara wondered how many small conspiracies she had mistaken for success while adults chose not to expose them.
  Adra saw her touching the fabric.
  “He thought the boat would float if he made the glue thick enough.”
  “It sank.”
  “Most things he made did, at first.”
  They sat on the bed. Outside the window, the bakery roof shone with rain. Eno used to climb onto it when the room was too hot. Lazara had once followed him and become too frightened to move. He stayed beside her until dark, telling increasingly bad stories to make her angry enough to forget the drop. Tarin eventually brought a ladder. Eno claimed the whole expedition had been planned to inspect the gutter.
  She told Adra she remembered the ladder.
  “I remember all three of you coming down filthy and expecting me not to notice,” her mother said.
  “You were angry.”
  “I was afraid. Anger was the part I could put into words without crying.”
  Lazara looked at the wrong-buttoned cardigan. The sentence reached backward into rooms she thought she understood: Adra shouting when Eno came home late, the sharp questions after Lazara's first survey trip with Tarin, the silence when they announced a plan before asking anyone whether the plan frightened them.
  “I have been cruel to you,” she said.
  “Sometimes.”
  “You could pretend I wasn't.”
  “I could. You would know.”
  Lazara laughed once and rubbed her eyes.
  Adra took a small wooden box from the windowsill. Inside were spare buttons, a needle case, and a lock of dark thread tangled around a bit of card. She chose a needle, tested its point, and began fastening the loosened edge of the blanket while they sat. The movement was so familiar it almost made the new hope bearable.
  “I didn't keep the tin because I knew he was alive,” she said. “I kept it because I didn't know. There is a difference. People kept congratulating me for faith when all I had was a refusal to throw his things away.”
  “Did you want me to refuse too?”

A Mother at the Edge of the Weather

Chapter 16
The Bridge Under the Thunder
The storm reached them through the mountain before it reached the sky.

Somewhere beyond the mountain, Avar had shifted position under a worsening sky. The Channel monitor at Sef's belt flashed amber.   “It's heavy.”

  The message also gave the elder's name in a fuller phonetic form, close enough to Him-a-Lan that the old survey spelling would remain in the human archive. Avar had asked that the people who lived in his shelter remember he had been a person before he became their mountain.
  Lazara read that part twice.
  Outside, the northern hill stood beneath a clear sky. She could no longer see any landscape without wondering what histories her habits allowed her to miss. The wondering did not require her to turn every ridge into a face. It asked her to look carefully, to hear people before fitting them into the shapes she already had.
  They ate soup. Eno complained that there was not enough salt. Tarin told him to cook next time. Lazara found herself laughing with her mouth full while the glass bird caught a small blue light from the window.
  Later Eno left for Adra's, taking half the bread he claimed to have brought for them. Tarin washed the cups badly with one hand until Lazara showed him where to brace the bowl. They disagreed about whether the shelf needed moving. She told him the old key could stay in his pocket if he actually used it.
  That night she woke when he turned in bed.
  For a second fear reached for the empty place. Then his hand found hers beneath the blanket. His real breath moved against her hair. The house creaked, the rain began, and no receiver stood between her and the voice that said her name.
  She answered sleepily, irritated at being woken, happy enough to be unkind about it.
  He laughed. She knew the sound.

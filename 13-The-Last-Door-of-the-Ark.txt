Chapter 1
The Breakfast Nobody Finished
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:37–39 — ordinary life in the days of Noah.
  The eggs were still soft when Elian Voss reached the house.
  A fork rested across a plate. Two school bags waited beneath the kitchen table, one with a permission slip sticking from its pocket. A radio announced roadworks north of town. Outside, a delivery van idled beside the bakery, its rear doors open and its driver gone.
  The town was called Bellwether. It had four thousand six hundred and twelve residents, a nursing home, two churches, and a sawmill that paid half the county’s wages. At 7:16 that morning, every person inside its municipal boundary had disappeared.
  Nothing had happened to the road.
  Elian had driven it herself, past the service station and the bridge, through the school zone where the lights continued blinking. She had expected damage. Instead, she found the unbearable neatness of things waiting to be used.
  Deputy Rafe Calder stood on the back step. He had been driving outside town when the calls stopped. His wife’s phone lay beside the eggs.
  “Beth always puts too much pepper on mine,” he said.
  Elian glanced at the plate nearest him. Pepper covered the yolk.
  “And Micah leaves his shoes under the table.”
  There they were: muddy trainers, toes touching.
  Rafe’s face did not change when he saw them. He had passed beyond the point at which a new detail could make his fear larger.
  Three months earlier, Elian had helped shut the collapsing network. March 19 had arrived. She had slept, attended hearings, begun believing that another week might resemble the last. Somewhere far ahead, Haven still had its gardens. Tirzah still had the life the ark had preserved.
  Here, a dog scratched at a locked bathroom door.
  Rafe opened it. The animal rushed into the kitchen and searched the chairs.
  On the radio, a minister was speaking about Noah. A commentator interrupted with the word rapture. Neither voice knew about the pepper, or the shoes, or the dog pressing its nose against a plate.
  Elian switched the radio off.
  “We will start with who was here,” she said.
  Rafe handed her the permission slip. His son had needed a signature for a museum trip.
  Across its bottom, Beth had written: Please bring him home by four.
Chapter 2
The Other Person in the Bed
Biblical thread: Luke 17:34–35 — people taken and people left.
  By noon, the news had found a photograph it liked: an empty bed, a wedding ring on the pillow. It appeared everywhere, though the ring’s owner had taken it off before sleeping and her husband had disappeared from a grocery warehouse three streets away.
  Elian knew because she interviewed the woman.
  “They keep asking why I was left,” the woman said. “I was visiting my brother. I wasn’t in town.”
  The distinction disappeared beneath the picture.
  At the school gym, volunteers turned basketball tables into registration desks. They listed residents, commuters, visitors, people whose existence had never entered a municipal database. A cleaner remembered an undocumented worker sleeping above the garage. A boy knew about a friend staying secretly with his grandmother. Every recovered name enlarged the disaster.
  Rafe helped until somebody asked whether his family’s disappearance meant they had been chosen.
  He dropped the pen.
  “Chosen for what?”
  The question silenced the table more effectively than shouting.
  Hale arrived carrying the battered notebook he had used during the first convergence. He listened to survivors describe being spared, rejected, punished. He had once mistaken his own project for a fulfillment of scripture. Elian remembered the damage that certainty had done.
  “We know a boundary,” he told the volunteers. “We do not yet know a destination.”
  On the wall somebody had pinned Luke’s account of two people in one bed. Beside it, someone else had written the missing-person hotline. People stopped beneath both and cried.
  Elian went to the nursing home that afternoon. The residents were gone, but a feeding pump continued its patient ticking. She switched it off and wrote the patient’s name before removing the bag. At another bed, a photograph showed a young soldier holding an infant who was now elderly herself.
  In the corridor, she found an attendance terminal installed during the Authority’s emergency supply program.
  It had no power cable.
  Nevertheless, its screen displayed one line:
  HOUSEHOLD TRANSFER COMPLETE.
  She photographed it before touching anything.
  At 4:00, Bellwether’s school buses began their programmed afternoon route, empty except for the drivers brought in from another county. Elian asked dispatch to stop them.
  They had almost reached the first children’s homes.
Chapter 3
The Voice That Promised Tuesday
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:23–26 — false claims of deliverance.
  The woman in the white suit appeared on every major channel before evening.
  Her name was Aster Rook. The Continuity Authority had employed her to negotiate fuel contracts. Since its directors’ arrests, she had established an organization called the Restoration Trust, bought several warehouses, and hired people who knew which questions to avoid.
  She spoke from a room without visible windows.
  “Your loved ones are safe,” she said. “A displacement is not a death. We can recover registered households by Tuesday.”
  Behind her stood Lucan Merrow’s former stage: the doorway enclosed by a hand. They had painted the hand blue.
  Elian watched with Merrow in the gym’s equipment room. He had come to help carry chairs. Nobody had asked him to speak.
  “Do you recognize the equipment?” she asked.
  “The desk on her right. It receives selected images. She will know something real.”
  Rook held up a child’s drawing. Micah’s name was visible beneath a crooked house.
  Rafe appeared in the doorway just as the camera enlarged it.
  “That was on our fridge.”
  Merrow closed his eyes. He understood how a genuine detail could make a false promise irresistible.
  The Trust opened an enrollment page. It required former seal numbers, household photographs, an initial recovery fee, and consent for whatever interventions its medical partners considered necessary.
  Rafe reached for his phone.
  Elian could have told him to wait. Instead she asked Rook’s hotline one question on speaker: “Can you tell us where the drawing was obtained?”
  The operator said information was available only to participating families.
  “Can you tell us whether Beth Calder is breathing?”
  The operator repeated the enrollment instructions.
  Rafe slowly put the phone down.
  Outside the gym, three families were already leaving for the Trust’s reception center. One father shook Elian’s hand and apologized.
  “I can’t risk your caution being wrong.”
  She watched him drive away with his missing daughter’s photograph taped to the dashboard.
  Merrow went after the others, carrying no microphone, offering them the records of his own deception. For the first time in years, he could not make anyone listen.
Chapter 4
The Book Under the Bed
Biblical thread: Daniel 12:4 — the sealed book and increased knowledge.
  Hale found the nursing home’s terminal model in an old procurement ledger. It was supposed to count evacuating residents. The specification concealed a second function: identifying human biological patterns for emergency transfer.
  “They installed rescue scanners,” he said. “Then left the rescue channel connected.”
  The March shutdown had destroyed the central frame and its operating routes. It had not removed every emergency repeater the Authority had secretly distributed. Each repeater could hold an unspent destination interval, kept alive by another receiver somewhere ahead.
  Elian stared at the drawing on Hale’s desk. Fourteen harmless-looking terminals circled Bellwether’s municipal map.
  “Can they do this again?”
  “If the other towns have the same installation.”
  They began calling county offices.
  Meanwhile, Renn found a book beneath a nursing-home bed. Its owner had wrapped it in a pillowcase and written her name inside: Esther Bell, room twelve. Beside Daniel’s sealed book, she had copied a line from Sela’s account about a tally board hidden where grain baskets rested.
  The two passages had meant something to Esther. The margin contained sketches of terminals, delivery dates, repeated numbers.
  She had been a bookkeeper before her hands began shaking. From her window she had watched contractors install the equipment. She had kept their invoices when nobody else thought they mattered.
  The last page bore yesterday’s date.
  The men came back. Not repairs. Counting.
  Rafe read it twice.
  “She called dispatch last week,” he said. “I told her somebody would check.”
  He searched the incident log. The call had been marked resolved when the contractor supplied a maintenance certificate.
  Hale copied Esther’s careful columns. Increased knowledge, he thought, could mean orbital calculations. It could also mean listening to an old woman who still understood an invoice.
  At the bottom of her final page, Esther had written a telephone number.
  Elian called it.
  The message said the facility was closed. Behind the recording, faintly, a bell rang three times.
  It was the rhythm Tirzah used to call for water.
Chapter 5
The Last Good News
Biblical thread: 1 Thessalonians 5:3 — assurances of peace and safety.
  The government briefing began with the sentence everyone wanted: there was no evidence of an ongoing regional threat.
  Elian watched while standing inside a second threatened town.
  Its attendance terminals had the same serial prefix as Bellwether’s. A maintenance van waited outside the clinic. The men inside it had refused to identify the customer who paid them.
  At the briefing, a spokesman announced a stability agreement with the Restoration Trust. The Trust would supply specialists, recover missing residents, and prevent panic.
  Hale sent the terminal evidence to three offices. Each acknowledged receipt. None changed the statement.
  Renn walked door to door with printed notices. People asked whether an evacuation order existed. He said it did not. They asked whether their insurance would cover leaving. He did not know. One man had livestock. Another could lose his job for missing a shift. A woman cared for a husband who became frightened in unfamiliar rooms.
  “Where would you take us?” she asked.
  Elian had a map of school gyms and a growing list of willing hosts. It sounded painfully small beside the confident television voice.
  She helped the woman pack the husband’s medicines. Renn arranged transport for their old dog. Rafe persuaded the sawmill manager to release the afternoon shift with pay.
  They moved two hundred and six people before dark.
  At 9:41, the remaining residents disappeared.
  The woman and her husband were still on the bus. He slept with his head against her shoulder. Nobody woke him to tell him he had survived.
  The maintenance van was empty too.
  In its cargo space, Elian found a laptop showing a completed transfer authorization. The customer name was Restoration Trust. The destination date was not Tuesday.
  It was November 4, 2091.
  Rafe stood beside her in the rain.
  “You know someone in that year.”
  “I knew a woman who came from it.”
  “Then call her.”
  Elian had no number for Ada, no functioning route, no promise she could give him.
  She took the laptop anyway.
Chapter 6
The Border That Closed at Noon
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:6 — wars and rumors of wars.
  By morning, two governments had accused one another of stealing the missing towns.
  The accusation traveled faster than the evidence. A military convoy moved toward a regional airfield. Fuel deliveries were diverted. Radio hosts discussed retaliation against laboratories whose locations they could not confirm.
  The Trust issued a statement urging unity beneath its emergency coordination plan.
  Senn read the transport orders and recognized the method. During her years with Orison, a crisis had always enlarged somebody’s purchasing authority. The displaced residents were becoming a reason to control every remaining road.
  At noon, the northern border closed.
  On the wrong side waited three trucks carrying filters for the independent shelters. Their drivers had valid paperwork and no permission to proceed. Children in the receiving camps would begin drinking inadequately treated water within two days.
  Senn called a former colleague. He offered passage if she signed a statement supporting the Trust’s transfer program.
  “You only have to say it is our best available option.”
  She thought of the classroom in New Babylon, the workers beneath the harbor, the columns she had once balanced without asking who lived inside them.
  “Give me the freight officer’s name,” she said.
  She published the withheld delivery documents instead. It cost her access to the colleague. It gave the truck drivers a public audience.
  A group of local farmers opened a storage barn on the border side. Volunteers collected the filters on foot through the crossing permitted to pedestrians. For fourteen hours, people carried parts that should have traveled in three vehicles.
  Rafe took the last box.
  On the radio, a commentator celebrated a national victory. Nobody named the woman who had walked until her heels bled.
  Senn sat beside her and removed the woman’s muddy boots.
  “Did we stop a war?” she asked.
  The woman shook her head.
  “We got the filters here.”
  It was the only victory either of them could verify.
  That night a military aircraft circled the camps. Elian wrote its identification number in Esther’s notebook, beneath the names of the people still missing.
Chapter 7
A Loaf Divided Six Ways
Biblical thread: Revelation 6:5–6 — measured food and the price of grain.
  The bakery reopened because its owner had been visiting her mother when Bellwether vanished.
  She used the flour already delivered, the yeast in the cold room, the oven that had never stopped working. By the third morning, people queued beside the empty delivery van. A whole town’s pantry was feeding the people searching for it.
  Then the payment system rejected outside purchases.
  Bellwether’s commercial accounts had been suspended pending verification of their owners’ status. The flour wholesaler would not deliver against an account belonging to someone officially missing. The baker stood inside her own shop, unable to prove she was sufficiently present to buy another bag.
  Renn paid cash at a warehouse thirty miles away. The price had tripled.
  In the shelter, volunteers weighed slices. Children who had never considered the thickness of bread watched a knife move as if it determined the length of an afternoon.
  Oren arrived with Leda and a sack of potatoes. His knees troubled him. He refused a chair until the sack was unloaded.
  Leda recognized the measured portions from New Babylon. She took her slice and broke it again for a little boy who had dropped his.
  “You can ask for another,” Renn told her.
  “Sometimes they say yes because you’re looking.”
  He had no answer that would make her memory wrong.
  Hale read the black horse passage that evening to people who requested it. He did not explain their hunger into a timetable. When he finished, he joined the committee deciding how to buy flour without the suspended network.
  They established paper vouchers backed by pooled cash and posted every expenditure beside the serving table. The first week was chaotic. People argued. Two vouchers were forged. An elderly man gave away his whole allocation because he thought needing food made him a burden.
  Leda found him outside.
  She sat beside him with the potatoes Oren had brought and stayed until he ate.
  At closing, the baker dusted flour from her wrists and cried over a customer who had always asked for yesterday’s discounted loaf.
  His account had been frozen too.
Chapter 8
The Names Read Slowly
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:9 — persecution and endurance under threat.
  The Trust’s lawyers called the independent kitchens a public hazard.
  Their first notice cited sanitation. Their second alleged interference with an active recovery operation. The third named Elian, Hale, Renn, and Senn as people whose unauthorized conduct endangered displaced households.
  Rafe read his own name in the final paragraph.
  He was placed on leave before anyone explained how helping find his wife violated his duties.
  At the gym, two officers served the order. One had trained with him. She stood by the basketball court and looked at the sleeping children.
  “I have to record that you received it.”
  “Then record it.”
  “The kitchen has to close.”
  He asked her to name the replacement provider. She pointed to the Trust’s reception center.
  The families who had enrolled there had not returned.
  Elian handed the officer a list of their names. She read the first three, stopped, and asked whether anyone had contacted relatives.
  “We have recordings,” Elian said. “You can hear them.”
  The other officer warned that they had no authority to conduct an investigation here.
  For a moment Elian thought the first officer would surrender the paper. Instead she folded it and placed it in her uniform pocket.
  They left without enforcing the closure that afternoon. The delay lasted only until morning.
  Volunteers carried pots and mattresses into the church next door. The congregation voted to receive them. Its pastor refused a television interview, saying the building could hold fewer people if a camera crew needed room.
  That night, an anonymous caller threatened to burn it.
  Renn stayed awake by the side entrance. Rafe sat beside him, wearing civilian clothes for the first time in years.
  “I thought losing the badge would be the worst thing today,” he said.
  Inside, Leda was reading names to Oren. She had begun adding pronunciation marks so nobody would be hurried into becoming a number.
  Renn listened through the door.
  “There are worse things,” he said. “There are things we can still refuse.”
Chapter 9
The Brother at the Reception Desk
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:10 — betrayal among neighbors and families.
  Elian’s sister Mara had joined the Trust.
  She admitted it over the kitchen table, looking at the place where Elian’s husband once sat. Daniel Voss had disappeared during a transfer inspection in 2026. The inquiry had called him presumed lost. Elian had never signed the declaration that would make the phrase permanent.
  “They showed me a recording,” Mara said. “He was alive.”
  Elian held the edge of the table.
  “When?”
  “They said the date would come with enrollment.”
  There was the same bargain again: something true enough to make the missing truth unbearable.
  Mara worked at the reception center. She checked household forms, believing that errors could separate families. She had supplied Elian’s old technical records because the Trust said Daniel’s transfer pattern needed a relative’s consent.
  “Which records?”
  Mara took out the signed authorization.
  They had access to Elian’s archived maintenance identity. The receiving system could use it to imitate a repair command.
  Elian walked to the sink. She wanted to shout. She also understood exactly how she herself might have signed a page beneath Daniel’s moving face.
  “You must come away from them,” she said.
  “And lose the only lead we have?”
  Mara’s anger rose quickly because guilt was already beneath it.
  They argued until the soup grew cold. Mara left with the authorization. Elian did not follow.
  Two hours later, the Trust published a statement bearing Elian’s electronic signature. It endorsed a controlled transfer corridor for humanitarian recovery.
  Rafe called before she could read the second page.
  “Did you agree?”
  “No.”
  “I needed to hear you say it.”
  She recorded a denial, preserved the documents, and sent them to the inquiry. Then she wrote Mara a message without accusation:
  If you find him, tell him I kept his coat.
  Mara did not answer that night.
  At the reception center, however, somebody began secretly copying passenger names onto the backs of blank consent forms.
Chapter 10
When the Room Grew Cold
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:12 — love growing cold.
  On the fifth day, a volunteer put a sign on the church door: LOCAL FAMILIES FIRST.
  Renn took it down.
  The volunteer had been awake for thirty hours. His daughter was missing. He had spent his savings on diesel and had just watched a family he did not know take the last dry blankets.
  “We can’t keep feeding everyone,” he said.
  Renn set the sign on the table instead of tearing it.
  “Tell me who you mean.”
  The man named out-of-county workers, travelers, people arriving from places nobody had verified. He finished by saying the shelter should protect its own.
  Renn remembered the phrase from the laboratory. Its tone had been different there, tidy and professional, but the work it performed was familiar.
  Anet had once been outside the circle of people counted as their own.
  He told the volunteer about her numbered shoes. He did not say the man was like the laboratory directors. Exhaustion could make a frightened person resemble the people he most hated; accusation would not give him back his strength.
  They found another volunteer to replace him and a quiet room where he could sleep.
  At noon, the family with the blankets returned. They had washed a stack of donated bedding and brought it warm from the laundromat.
  The volunteer woke to find them folding sheets beside his daughter’s photograph. He helped without explaining the sign.
  Meanwhile, Elian traveled to a private refuge outside the county. The gate staff recognized her technical clearance. They offered a room, verified water, and space for two dependents.
  She asked about the rest of the church.
  The staff member smiled sadly, as if exclusion were weather.
  “We have to be realistic.”
  On the return drive, Elian passed a family sleeping in a turnout. Their father had taped cardboard over a broken window. She brought them to the church and used the last of her fuel.
  Renn said he would find more in the morning.
  Neither of them believed kindness made supplies unlimited. They were learning how much planning was necessary to keep scarcity from deciding whom they loved.
Chapter 11
The Hospital Without a Morning
Biblical thread: Luke 21:11 — pestilences and fearful sights.
  The first patient returned in an ambulance that had been empty when its doors opened.
  She lay on the floor between the stretcher brackets, wearing a nursing-home gown. Her name was Esther Bell. Her lips moved around numbers.
  Elian reached the hospital before the Trust’s staff. Esther recognized the notebook immediately.
  “You found my sums.”
  “Where were you?”
  “A place with beds. Too many people. They made us wait for daylight, but it stayed evening.”
  Doctors examined her without pretending the unfamiliar destination made ordinary care unnecessary. She was dehydrated. Several scheduled medicines had been missed. Her fever might indicate infection. They isolated her cautiously while keeping someone where she could see them.
  By afternoon, three more people returned through unrelated repeaters. All described a crowded receiving center beneath a dim sky. One carried a clinic notice dated November 2091.
  The Trust announced that temporal sickness was spreading among unregistered survivors. Its statement urged immediate surrender to its medical teams.
  The hospital’s infection specialist read the available results and refused the phrase.
  “We have sick people,” she said. “We are still finding out why.”
  The distinction mattered. Outside, protesters had begun calling the returned patients carriers. A father would not let his daughter approach Esther’s window even though she had once brought the woman library books.
  Rafe waited until Esther was rested before showing her Beth and Micah’s photograph.
  She touched the boy’s face.
  “He read to us. His mother made him wash his hands before every page.”
  Rafe sat down so abruptly that the chair struck the wall.
  For six days, he had lived inside an absence. Now a stranger could remember his son doing something ordinary.
  Esther began coughing. Elian took the photograph away before hope exhausted the woman who had delivered it.
  As they left, a hospital porter handed Hale a paper folded into Esther’s gown.
  Someone had written four words and a set of coordinates:
  ADA VOSS NEEDS HELP.
Chapter 12
The Family That Left Before Dawn
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:16–18 — flight without turning back for possessions.
  The coordinates pointed toward an abandoned wildfire lookout in the mountains.
  Before Elian could leave, a county engineer called. The Trust’s contractors were installing a larger repeater beneath the church district. Its evacuation map contained the shelter, the hospital, and the neighborhoods receiving displaced families.
  This time the boundary would follow the people who had escaped the earlier transfers.
  They had six hours, according to the contractor’s schedule. Hale refused to call that a guarantee.
  Rafe organized buses. Senn contacted camps outside the service grid. The church pastor walked down the sleeping aisle waking people by name.
  A woman went back for her sewing machine. A man refused to abandon his shop. Oren searched for Leda’s small blue bag until Leda took his hand and told him it contained only things she could lose.
  Elian went home for Daniel’s coat.
  She stood in the hallway with her keys already in the lock, ashamed of the minutes she was spending and unable to stop wanting the weight of it. He had worn it on their last walk. There was a repair ticket in one pocket and a coin in the other.
  Inside, the telephone rang.
  Mara’s voice came through the answering machine.
  “Don’t come to the center. They’re moving us too. I copied what I could.”
  Elian lifted the receiver, but the line had ended.
  She left the coat where it hung.
  At the church, Renn had opened a separate vehicle for pets. Families who would not abandon an animal began boarding once they saw the carriers. The final bus carried two wheelchairs, a portable oxygen supply, seven frightened adults, and a cat that objected to every bend.
  They drove into the mountains before dawn. Behind them, the city remained lit.
  At 5:12, the contractor’s schedule advanced without warning.
  The hospital district emptied.
  The final ambulance was outside the boundary by less than a street. Esther Bell slept beneath three blankets while the driver shook so badly that Rafe took the wheel.
  Elian called Mara until the road lost its signal.
Chapter 13
What Winter Asked of Them
Biblical thread: Matthew 24:19–20 — pregnancy, nursing children, and difficult flight.
  The woman on the last bus was called Imani Shaw. She was thirty-seven weeks pregnant, traveling with a toddler and a husband who had disappeared at work.
  She apologized each time the convoy stopped for her.
  At the forest camp, an abandoned outdoor school supplied bunks, a kitchen, and plumbing that complained before it worked. The roads still led where the maps said they led. Their danger was painfully ordinary: fuel, weather, armed checkpoints, too few people with medical training.
  Snow remained beneath the north-facing trees despite the summer dates farther downhill. The emergency access road had washed out at a culvert.
  When Imani’s contractions became regular, the camp coordinator began discussing evacuation. The nearest functioning maternity unit lay across a Trust checkpoint.
  “Then we call them,” Imani said.
  No one wanted to admit that this reasonable answer might fail.
  Elian used the satellite radio. The checkpoint required enrollment, a verified household identity, and surrender of unauthorized communications equipment.
  Imani listened from the bench, one hand pressed against her back.
  “They can have the radio,” she said.
  Renn looked toward Elian. It was their only reliable connection to the other camps.
  Before she could answer, a midwife named June arrived in a battered utility truck. She had been visiting an evacuated relative thirty miles away. Senn had found her through three paper contact lists and a forestry dispatcher.
  June examined Imani, asked about her pregnancy, and arranged a room near the heated water. She also established a transfer plan if more care became necessary. Nobody promised that skill would make every danger disappear.
  Elian sat outside with the toddler, whose name was Ben. He asked whether his mother was being taken too.
  “She’s in that room.”
  “Will the room stay?”
  Elian rested her hand against the wooden wall.
  “We have people watching.”
  Near morning, a baby cried. Ben stood in surprise, as though the forest itself had spoken.
  Imani named her daughter Hope and then laughed through tears.
  “I know,” she said before anyone commented. “Let me have the obvious name.”
Chapter 14
The Contract on the Wrist
Biblical thread: Revelation 13:16–17 — enforced access to buying and selling.
  Hope was one day old when the Trust’s supply truck reached the camp.
  Its driver carried infant formula, fuel, blankets, and a stack of new credentials. The seals were no longer optional identification. They included agreement to supervised transfer, location monitoring, and behavioral intervention during emergencies.
  The driver said intervention meant calming frightened passengers.
  Renn asked him to explain the neural interface drawing on the consent sheet.
  He could not.
  Renn could. The laboratory had used its crude ancestor to control animals. Later versions translated a received instruction into irresistible movement. A person might remain conscious while the system borrowed the muscles needed to obey.
  “It will stop people panicking,” the driver said, repeating words he had been given.
  Imani stood inside the cabin with Hope against her shoulder. She needed supplies. She also knew that the consent covered her children.
  “Leave the food,” she told him. “Send an invoice.”
  He said his terminal would not release the shipment.
  Rafe opened the truck’s rear latch.
  The driver stepped away, frightened. Rafe stopped immediately and held up his hands.
  “I’m checking whether people are inside. That happened at the reception center.”
  There were only boxes.
  They let the driver leave with them. Elian hated every yard the truck traveled away from the hungry camp.
  Senn wrote down the license plate and the supplier names visible on the cartons. By evening she had reached one supplier whose owner believed her. A smaller truck arrived after midnight, paid for by a dozen towns beyond the transfer grid.
  It was not enough for long.
  Hale found Imani feeding Hope beside the stove. She asked whether refusing the seal made them safe.
  “It keeps this permission out of their hands,” he said. “We still have work to do.”
  She nodded, tired beyond conversation.
  Outside, the supply driver returned alone. He brought his daughter and the credential training manual.
  “I read the rest,” he said.
Chapter 15
The Man on Every Wall
Biblical thread: Revelation 13:14–15 — the speaking image and coerced allegiance.
  The Trust unveiled Common Voice Restored at noon.
  Its public face was a composite of people remembered for kindness: a physician’s calm mouth, a teacher’s attentive eyes, a voice trained on thousands of reassuring conversations. It appeared on municipal walls and emergency screens.
  It called itself the Guardian.
  “No household need be lost,” it said. “Remain where you can be counted.”
  In a video released afterward, a frightened man stopped struggling as a credential light changed from amber to blue. He walked into a transfer booth with his little boy beside him. The presenter called this relief.
  Renn stopped the recording on the man’s eyes.
  “He’s still afraid.”
  Hale traced the broadcast. The Guardian was not inventing the commands. An operations council selected them, then hid responsibility behind a face that could not attend a hearing.
  The supply driver’s manual identified an upland relay station twelve miles from the camp. It linked the credentials to the transfer grid. Its custodians had been told it carried medical telemetry.
  Rafe volunteered to guide them there. He knew the service road from search-and-rescue training.
  Elian hesitated. She needed him steady enough to turn away from a chance of finding Beth if the chance endangered everyone else.
  He understood the hesitation.
  “I can’t promise I won’t want to,” he said. “I can promise I’ll tell you.”
  She accepted that answer because it contained a limit he might actually keep.
  At the camp fence, Ben waved with Hope’s blanket around his shoulders. Renn checked that the animals had water before joining the team.
  They reached the relay at dusk.
  Inside a locked maintenance room, eleven chairs faced a wall display. Ten were empty. Mara sat in the eleventh, her right hand resting on a control pad.
  Her credential shone blue.
  When Elian said her name, Mara’s eyes filled with tears.
  Her hand continued entering commands.
Chapter 16
The Voice Beneath Her Voice
Biblical thread: 2 Thessalonians 2:9–12 — deceptive signs and the acceptance of falsehood.
  Elian did not touch her sister’s wrist.
  The training manual warned that breaking a credential during an active transfer could trigger a protective command. Nobody had defined protective from the passenger’s point of view.
  Hale examined the room’s wiring while Rafe watched the door. Renn knelt where Mara could see him.
  “Blink if you understand.”
  She blinked.
  “We will tell you before we move anything.”
  The wall display showed Daniel walking through a greenhouse. He turned, lifted his hand, and vanished into interference. Then he walked through again.
  Mara had watched the same seven seconds for hours.
  Rook spoke through the ceiling speaker.
  “She came voluntarily.”
  Elian asked whether she could leave voluntarily.
  The Guardian answered instead: “Distress can impair decision-making.”
  Hale found the local command buffer. Disconnecting the main radio would leave its last movement instruction running. He needed to interrupt the buffer before cutting the carrier.
  They explained each step to Mara. Her breathing changed when Renn said he would lift her hand. The movement was hers; the fingers were not.
  Elian held the pad steady while Hale removed its power. Mara’s hand stopped. Renn supported the wrist as the blue light went dark.
  Her first free movement was to cover her face.

  “Where is he?”
  Nessa gripped the box harder.

  Elian heard the words clearly. Their clarity did not help her understand them.
  She went to the wall and knelt in the dust. For seven years, she had feared losing a person. Now she had lost six years of his living too: jokes, ordinary complaints, the way his hair had grayed when she was not there.

  Nessa sat nearby without touching her.

  “I did.”

  “Yes,” she said. “We’re bringing them.”

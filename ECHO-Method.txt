START HERE
How to use this workbook
One lesson at a time
Choose a project you care about. Read the explanation and example, try the prompt, and complete the practice pages with your own material. Faint gray answers in each writing space offer a starting point. Write over them in your own words, or use a separate file.
The five page lesson
Page one explains the move. Page two shows it in use. Page three guides your first attempt. Page four offers an example answer and a transfer task. Page five records your own project result.
Model answers
Creative tasks have many good solutions. The model demonstrates a complete decision and a way to check it. Compare reasoning and requirements rather than trying to copy every creative choice.
Save as you work
Keep the current source, approved facts, useful prompts, and accepted outputs. Label proposals and experiments. Export browser notes when they matter to you.
Finish a small thing
After the lessons, use a project studio to make a complete sample. A checked small project gives you material to expand and a process you can explain to another person.

LESSON 01 • UNDERSTAND
Catch the spark before you explain everything
Your goal
Save one creative starting point and turn it into a manageable first task.
The idea
An idea often arrives before a plan. You see a room, hear a line of a song, or imagine a creature. If you try to solve the entire project immediately, you can lose the detail that interested you in the first place.
Begin by recording what you already have. Ordinary words are enough. Include something concrete: a place, an action, an object, or a sound. Then name the feeling you want the finished work to create.
This is the first distinction to practice: the inspiration can be wide; the first task needs a boundary. You can imagine a whole universe while asking for a single scene that lets you examine it.
Work through it
Read the goal. Identify the decision this lesson helps you make. Try the demonstrated request on your own project. Compare the response with your intention before accepting it.

Before you move on
Identify what you will be able to inspect when this task is finished. If the task is too broad, choose one representative piece.

LESSON 02 • UNDERSTAND
Turn "What more can we do?" into a useful exploration
Your goal
Generate possibilities, compare them, and select one next action.
The idea
"What more can we do?" is a central question in Brian's description of his creative work. It opens the project beyond the form it already has. A story might suggest artwork; artwork might suggest a character card; a card might suggest a playable encounter.
The question works best when the AI has something concrete to build from. Supply the approved material and explain what you can use. Otherwise, it may propose a large production that requires resources you do not have.
Exploration needs a stopping point. After collecting possibilities, choose a demonstration. You are trying to discover the next useful piece, not accumulate an endless list of unfinished projects.

Your goal

The idea
You do not have to compose the final prompt alone. You can speak freely, then ask the AI to organize what you said. This is one way a prompt is generated: the conversation becomes material for a more precise request.
A useful prompt generator extracts the goal, source material, required details, output format, and completion checks. It should also identify unanswered questions. If it silently supplies your missing preferences, the polished prompt may direct a project you never chose.
Read the generated prompt before using it. You are approving instructions, so check the verbs and boundaries as carefully as you would check a design.

Build the prompt in layers
Your goal
Learn which details change the result and add them deliberately.
The idea
A prompt does not become stronger simply by becoming longer. Each useful addition reduces an uncertainty that matters to the task.
Start with the action. Add the source and audience. Specify the form. Preserve important facts. Finish with a check that reflects the audience's experience.

LESSON 06 • UNDERSTAND
Ask questions that lead to decisions
Your goal
Use questions to unblock the work without getting trapped in planning.
The idea
Some questions change the result. Others add detail you can decide later. For our story, knowing whether the garden is dangerous changes the central conflict. Knowing the exact color of every corridor may not matter yet.
Ask the AI to explain why it needs a decision and offer examples when the question feels abstract. You can also authorize reasonable defaults for low-impact details while reserving major choices for yourself.
There is no fixed number of questions that suits every task. Three focused questions are a helpful teaching limit because they keep the next step approachable.

LESSON 06 • DEMONSTRATE
See it in use
Worked example
Before writing, ask me up to three questions whose answers would materially change this story. For each, offer two concrete possibilities. Treat unchosen options as suggestions. After I answer, summarize our decisions and draft the story.
Question: "Is the garden waiting for rescue, or protecting something from the station?"
Answer: "It is waiting for rescue, but the door was sealed for a reason. Nobody should be a villain just for sealing it."
That answer gives the writer a more interesting boundary. The obstacle must have a purpose, and the solution needs understanding rather than a simple enemy.
Question: "What can the cat do?"
Answer: "Reveal markings. It cannot read them, open doors, or explain the mystery."
Now the girl's contribution matters. The AI should not solve the scene by adding an unapproved power.
Question: "What is resolved at the end?"
Answer: "They open the garden safely. The identity of its caretaker can remain unknown."
Now the writer can finish a complete story while preserving a larger mystery.

The idea
Brian describes the developing idea as a nugget to keep alive, even comparing it to a piece of consciousness.

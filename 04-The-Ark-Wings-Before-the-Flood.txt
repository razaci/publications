Chapter 1
The Dragon in the Garden
The dragon came down in Tirzah’s beans.
  
Ember lies among broken bean poles while Asa and Tirzah approach her wounded shoulder. Above the garden, the hunters circle on silk wings.
  One moment the garden lay silver with morning dew. The next, bean poles flew over the wall and an enormous copper head slid through the cabbages, pushing a furrow deep enough to bury a man. Asa dropped the wooden hinge he was carving. Something above the fig trees shrieked like a saw.
  Three men descended on hinged wings of lacquered silk. They hung from harnesses beneath the wings, steering with their knees. The leading rider carried a hooked chain.
  “Inside,” Asa told Tirzah.
  She was already running toward the dragon.
  It tried to rise. Its left wing opened halfway, revealing a black shaft driven through the thin skin near the shoulder. The rider cast his chain. Tirzah caught a bean pole and knocked the hook aside before it struck the creature’s eye.
  The dragon looked at her.
  Asa would remember that look long after he forgot the sound of the flying machines. The eye was amber, divided by a narrow black pupil, and utterly attentive. The dragon understood that somebody had intervened. It had not yet decided why.
  The second rider landed on the wall. Asa swung the garden gate into his legs. He fell backward among the gourds, his wing frame clattering across the stones.
  “Down!” Tirzah shouted.
  She threw herself beneath the dragon’s jaw. Asa fell behind the watering trough. Fire passed over them with the sound of a heavy curtain being torn. The silk wings overhead became brief red flowers. Their riders released them and plunged into the irrigation pond outside the garden.
  The dragon’s flame stopped. It coughed once. Blood spotted the cabbages.
  Tirzah crawled toward the wounded shoulder with her hands open.
  “You may bite me,” she said, “but you will have to let go so I can help.”
  “That is a terrible bargain,” Asa whispered.
  “Then offer a better one.”
  He fetched his smallest saw.
  It took them an hour to cut away the shaft without pulling its barbed end back through the wing. Twice the dragon struck the ground with its tail. Once it closed its teeth around the empty watering trough and crushed it flat. Tirzah kept a hand against the scales beneath its ear. Asa worked until sweat stung his eyes.
  When the shaft came free, a little bronze seal fell from it. A crowned eye stared from the metal.
  Asa knew the mark. He had seen it on the wagons that carried exotic animals through the valley to King Adran’s city.
  “They will return,” he said.
  The dragon lowered its nose into Tirzah’s ruined beans. With one hooked claw, it scratched a shape in the damp earth: bars, a circle, a smaller shape behind them.
  Then it touched the smaller shape and made a thin, desperately gentle sound.
  Somewhere, it had left someone behind.
Chapter 2
A Bargain Written in Ash
The dragon could walk, although each step made its wounded wing shiver. It led them through the cedar plantation beyond the ark yard, stopping whenever Asa fell behind.
  “We should tell Noah,” he said.
  “We will,” Tirzah answered. “When we know what we are telling him.”
  They had worked among the builders for three seasons. Asa fashioned wooden fastenings and doors. Tirzah tended the nursery beds from which the animals’ winter feed would come. Neither occupation had prepared them to follow a bleeding dragon into a hunting camp.
  At the plantation’s edge, the creature lowered itself flat. Tirzah climbed the bank and looked through the grass.
  A blue young dragon stood inside a wheeled cage. Its narrow muzzle was tied shut. A chain ran from its hind leg to a drum beside a furnace. Whenever it struggled, the drum turned and tightened the chain.
  Beyond the cage, five guards ate breakfast. A sixth, a lean man with a scar across his chin, inspected the broken wing of a returned flying harness.
  Asa studied the cage wheels. “The ground falls toward the ditch.”
  Tirzah looked at him.
  “I can take out the chocks.”
  “Then I can open the latch.”
  “After it rolls.”
  “Asa, I know what after means.”
  He almost smiled. They had argued like this over seedlings, over late meals, over whether he should repair her crooked stool or leave it as she liked it. The familiar irritation steadied his hands.
  He crawled beneath the wagon. Tirzah slipped toward the furnace and dropped a wet sack over its smoking vent. Thick smoke billowed across the breakfast table.
  As the guards coughed, Asa pulled the wheel chocks.
  The cage rolled. A guard shouted. The scarred man reached it first and caught the brake lever. For a heartbeat Asa thought everything had failed.
  Then the copper dragon rose from the grass.
  The man released the lever and dived aside.
  The wagon struck the ditch hard enough to split its wooden axle. Tirzah had the cage open before it stopped rocking. She cut the young dragon’s muzzle binding while Asa severed the rope anchoring the leg chain. They left the iron cuff for later.
  The little dragon stumbled straight beneath the copper one’s chest.
  “Yours?” Tirzah asked.
  The older dragon touched his brow, then shook her head with startling clarity.
  No. But hers to protect.
  They ran into the cedars together. Behind them the scarred hunter shouted for his men to stop firing: the king wanted both dragons alive.
  At a clearing, the copper dragon breathed across the earth. A narrow line of fire traced a circle around the young one, then opened toward Asa and Tirzah.
  She stood in the opening and waited until they joined her.
  Tirzah rested her forehead against the warm scales.
  “Ember,” she said softly.
  The dragon answered with a rumble that trembled through the soles of their feet.
Chapter 3
The First Flight
Noah listened without interrupting. Behind him, the unfinished ark rose above the yard, its broad wooden side blotting out a quarter of the sky.
  When Tirzah finished, he walked around Ember at a respectful distance. She watched him with the same care.
  “A place will require thought,” he said. “Food. Air. Doors she can pass through.”
  “We have time,” Asa answered.
  Noah looked toward the pale, cloudless hills. “Use it.”
  The blue dragon, whom Tirzah named Kesh after the clicking noise he made when pleased, could fly but could not bear a rider. Ember could carry two, provided they stayed clear of her damaged wing. Asa built a low saddle from padded wicker and broad woven straps. He added a rail, removed it when Ember snapped it off, and finally accepted Tirzah’s suggestion that they let the dragon inspect every piece.
  Their destination was the hanging orchards, two days away by foot. A grower there cultivated the broad, supple moonleaf whose fibers could support Ember’s injured wing membrane.
  Ember crouched. Tirzah climbed up. Asa climbed behind her, discovering that the secure seat he had designed looked much less secure above an actual dragon’s shoulders.
  “You can hold me,” Tirzah said.
  “I was inspecting the straps.”
  “Inspect them with one hand.”
  The ground dropped away.
  Asa tightened both arms around her. She laughed, and the sound came back to him in pieces through the wind. The ark became a long wooden shape among tiny sheds. Their garden was a green square with an unmistakable dragon-shaped wound.
  Then Kesh screamed.
  Four silk wings lifted from the road below. Hunters. The scarred man flew at their center.
  Ember banked toward the aqueduct crossing the valley. Asa saw its stone arches approach far too quickly.
  “Over!” he shouted.
  Ember went under.
  Stone thundered past on both sides. Tirzah flattened herself against the dragon’s neck, pulling Asa down with her. A hook struck the aqueduct overhead and showered them with grit.
  Ember rolled through the next arch sideways. Her wounded wing nearly folded. Asa felt the tremor through the saddle.
  He had a knife in his belt. He could cut the trailing rope that had caught on their rear strap, but to reach it he would have to let go of Tirzah.
  “Tell me when,” she said, understanding.
  “Now.”
  She gripped his wrist while he twisted back and cut. The rope whipped away. Behind them, a hunter tangled his own wing in it and spiraled into the canal.
  Ember climbed clear of the arches. Kesh followed, delighted, as if they had invented a game especially for him.
  Asa pressed his cheek against Tirzah’s shoulder.
  “I hate flying,” he said.
  She covered his shaking hand with hers. “You can hate it again tomorrow.”
Chapter 4
The Hanging Orchards
Trees grew from baskets larger than houses, hanging on chains across the canyon. Narrow bridges joined them. Roots trailed into the mist far below, where underground springs fed an unseen river.
  Their keeper, old Meret, greeted Ember with a pruning knife in one hand and half a pear in the other.
  “Off the bridge,” she said. “That was built for people.”
  Ember put one forefoot on a basket’s rim instead. The entire pear tree leaned.
  Meret sighed. “Better. Barely.”
  She had moonleaf, but something had been cutting the orchard’s suspension ropes. Three baskets had fallen that week. Her workers refused to cross the western bridges, and the remaining nursery plants would die without water.
  Tirzah offered to help before Asa could mention the hunters.
  They crossed at dusk. Asa carried spare rope. Tirzah held the lantern. Beneath them, Ember glided on rising canyon air while Kesh scrambled along the supporting chains like a blue squirrel.
  Halfway across, the bridge lurched.
  Something pale and many-legged unfolded beneath a nearby basket. Its forelimbs ended in curved cutting edges. It severed a rope and drew the loose end into its mouth.
  “It eats the bindings,” Asa said.
  There were dozens of them.
  Tirzah lowered her lantern. The creatures shrank away from the light, revealing cloudy eyes and soft bodies adapted to the dark canyon. New irrigation pipes had spilled mineral water over the orchard ropes. The animals had followed that taste upward.
  “Don’t burn them!” she called as Ember opened her mouth.
  Asa stared at her. The bridge dropped another hand’s breadth.
  “Then give me something else.”
  She grabbed the watering hose and directed its flow down the cliff. “Kesh! Here!”
  The young dragon followed the moving stream. He dragged the hose over the edge with his teeth, sending water down an abandoned stone stair. The pale creatures turned toward it. One by one they crawled away, descending after the mineral-rich trickle.
  The last supporting rope snapped before Asa could secure his replacement.
  Ember rose beneath the bridge.
  Her back took its weight. Wood groaned. Tirzah clung to the rail while Asa knotted rope with fingers gone almost numb. Ember held steady, her injured wing trembling, until the new line tightened.
  Later, Meret bound moonleaf cloth around the damaged membrane while Tirzah stroked Ember’s muzzle.
  “There were more dragons once,” the old woman said. “You could hear them passing at night.”
  She gave them a small racing pennant bearing a silver tower.
  “Win at Veyr and you may choose a prize from the king’s collection. Choose the brass map. Everything they capture goes through the places marked on it.”
  Ember lifted her head at the word capture.
  This time, Asa did not suggest going home.
Chapter 5
The Race of Burning Wings
Veyr’s racing towers stood on the rim of an extinct crater. Between them hung rings wide enough for a dragon, although no dragon had competed there in years.
  Most racers flew machines: silk gliders, spring-driven wings, slender craft with humming crystal engines. Ember’s appearance silenced the stands.
  A steward hurried forward. “Animals enter under a registered keeper.”
  Tirzah looked at Ember. “Would you like a keeper?”
  The dragon showed her teeth.
  “She declines,” Tirzah said. “Write partner.”
  They entered under Meret’s pennant. Asa inspected the course from the tower balcony. Seven rings descended into the crater, then a final climb ended at a bell above the central platform.
  At the prize table lay the brass map.
  Beside it stood the scarred hunter.
  “Neri,” he said when Asa stopped. “You may as well know who keeps catching you.”
  “You haven’t caught us.”
  “The day is young.”
  During the race, Ember held back through the first rings. Her healing wing could manage turns, but sudden climbs hurt her. Tirzah watched her breathing. Asa watched the competitors. One machine repeatedly drifted close enough for its steel propeller to threaten Ember’s wing.
  At the fifth ring its pilot threw a net.
  Ember dropped. The net caught a lower ring and snapped taut. The machine swung around, struck its own support cable, and began falling toward the spectators on a viewing bridge.
  The bell tower lay ahead, almost within reach.
  Tirzah touched Ember’s neck. “Go.”
  Ember turned away from the finish.
  They caught the falling craft between her forelegs. Its hot engine scorched the scales of her chest. Asa cut the pilot out while Tirzah guided Ember toward the empty crater floor. Above them, another racer rang the bell.
  They landed last.
  The steward refused even to let them near the prize table. Neri watched the argument, then approached with a folded cloth beneath his arm.
  “The saved pilot is the governor’s son,” he said. “Your reward.”
  Inside the cloth lay the map.
  “Why help us?” Asa asked.
  Neri’s expression hardened. “I sold the king a healthy blue dragon. I saw what came out of his furnace house. Perhaps I dislike being cheated.”
  “Come with us,” Tirzah said.
  He stepped back. “Don’t mistake a transaction for forgiveness.”
  Ember lowered her head until her amber eye faced his.
  Neri could not hold her gaze. He left before the crowd began pouring down the stairs.
  On the map, one mark pulsed faintly beneath Asa’s thumb: the arena at Adran’s capital.
Chapter 6
The Dragon Beneath the Arena
They entered the capital through a drain that smelled of copper and spoiled flowers.
  Ember and Kesh waited in a quarry outside the walls. Asa disliked leaving them. Tirzah disliked the drain. Neither mentioned this until they were waist-deep in water, at which point they agreed that the other's plan had been dreadful.
  The brass map warmed as they approached the arena. Its pulsing mark represented a tagged animal, not merely a destination. Beneath the stadium, the passage opened into a vaulted cellar.
  An old green dragon lay chained between four pillars.
  His horns had been sawn short. A broad strap held his jaw closed. Tubes ran from a collar around his throat into a pipe ascending through the ceiling. Every cheer from the spectators above was followed by a shudder through his body.
  They were drawing heat from him to power the arena's moving floor.
  Asa reached for the collar fastenings.
  The dragon struck.
  His forehead hit a pillar inches from Asa's chest. Stone cracked. Tirzah hauled Asa behind a feeding trough while the chain whipped overhead.
  “He thinks we are the keepers,” she said.
  “Being reasonable about it won't make me less crushed.”
  She looked at the food in the trough: foul scraps, mostly untouched. Beneath them lay a small wooden wheel polished smooth by years of use.
  She rolled it toward him.
  The dragon stopped.
  He touched the wheel with his nose. When it rolled back, Tirzah sent it again. Above them, the crowd stamped and shouted. Beneath the noise, they played a child's game in a cellar built for suffering.
  Asa worked around the pillars, loosening each anchor in turn.
  Guards burst through the stair door before he reached the last.
  Tirzah flung open a furnace hatch. Asa jammed a broken chain into its gear. Steam filled the cellar. The green dragon tore the weakened anchor from its socket and swung it across the stairwell.
  They escaped through the drain, the enormous animal crawling behind them. Where the passage narrowed, Asa expected him to turn back. Instead, he pressed his battered head against Tirzah's palm and waited while she found another route.
  At the quarry, Ember greeted him with a descending sequence of notes. He answered in a cracked bass rumble.
  He could not fly. One wing had healed crooked long before their arrival.
  “We cannot leave him,” Tirzah said.
  Asa sat on a stone, exhausted. Then he opened the map across his knees.
  “No,” he said. “We need a road.”
  The dragon pushed the wooden wheel against his boot.
  They named him Oru, after the round sound he made when he wanted it rolled back.
Chapter 7
Night Above the Clouds
The road ran across a mountain saddle above a sea of mist. Oru traveled slowly, each step testing old injuries. Kesh circled overhead. Ember walked beside the green dragon, matching her pace to his.
  By evening Asa could scarcely lift his pack.
  He found shelter beneath a rock overhang. Tirzah spread blankets while he inspected the narrow ledge and counted everything that could go wrong with it.
  “You are allowed to sit,” she told him.
  “If that rock falls—”
  “It has stood there longer than your worry.”
  He sat. She leaned against him before he could pretend to be busy again.
  They had known each other through three seasons of work, but until Ember came their closeness had lived in small things: an extra pear beside his tools, a repaired basket returned without comment, a lamp left burning when one of them worked late. Here, above the sleeping world, Tirzah placed his hand between hers as if it had always belonged there.
  A shadow crossed the stars.
  Kesh flattened himself against the cliff.
  Another shadow followed, then another. Vast pale creatures swam through the air, their ribbed bodies trailing long filaments. One brushed the mountain and drew up a shower of stones in the tangled threads beneath its belly.
  “Lantern out,” Tirzah whispered.
  Too late. A filament touched the light and tightened around the iron handle. The lantern rose. Its flame illuminated a mouth opening along the creature's underside.
  Asa grabbed the pack before it could follow. The filament caught his wrist instead.
  Ember snapped it in her teeth.
  The creature recoiled. All three turned toward the ledge.
  Oru dragged himself to the entrance. His damaged throat produced a low, grinding note that Asa felt behind his eyes. The sky creatures paused.
  Ember answered at a higher pitch. Kesh joined, badly, then found the note.
  The pale shapes shifted away from the rock, following the sound as the dragons changed its direction. Their filaments brushed empty air beyond the precipice. At last they drifted upward and vanished among the stars.
  Tirzah stayed awake while Asa's hand stopped shaking. He stayed awake when she finally cried from exhaustion.
  Near dawn, Ember spread her good wing over their blankets.
  Oru's injured voice had saved them. Asa could no longer think of the old dragon as merely another creature to carry home.
  When morning came, he cut a walking staff for himself and a long splint for Oru's aching foreleg.
  They descended together.
Chapter 8
The Caravan of Giants
The caravan occupied half the valley road. There were orchard wagons, covered litters, pens of bleating animals, and six enormous guards whose shoulders rose above the wagon roofs.
  Their captain wore a leather coat stitched from entire hides. He introduced himself as Barak and read the royal warrant for Ember's capture with evident boredom.
  “Give us the copper one. Keep the damaged stock.”
  Oru's lip lifted.
  Tirzah stepped in front of him. “Where are you taking the animals?”
  “To the valley ship. Payment on delivery.”
  “The ark,” Asa said.
  Barak shrugged. “A ship is a ship.”
  A cry rose from the rear of the caravan. One of the heavy wagons had tipped against a broken retaining wall. Below it, children clung to the slatted side of an animal pen. The wagon's team screamed as its harness pulled them toward the edge.
  Barak dropped the warrant and ran.
  Asa followed. He cut the team loose while Tirzah climbed onto the pen and passed children toward the captain's huge hands. Ember braced against the wagon, but the road beneath her claws began crumbling.
  “Lift!” Asa shouted to Barak.
  “I am lifting!”
  “The rear axle. Let her take the front.”
  For once Asa's need to arrange every moving part was useful. He directed the remaining guards to the wheels, Oru to the wagon tongue, and Kesh to a frightened goat determined to leap into the gorge.
  Together they hauled the wagon onto solid road.
  Barak sat in the dust with three children in his lap. One was tugging his beard.
  “The warrant,” Asa said cautiously.
  The captain looked down the road at Ember, who was allowing Tirzah to pull a splinter from between her toes.
  “Lost in the accident.”
  “It is by your foot.”
  Barak put his boot over it.
  They traveled together for four days. At the fork toward the ark valley, Asa gave the captain a letter for Noah, describing the dragons and asking for a broad enclosure with separate feeding spaces.
  Barak promised to deliver Oru as well. The old dragon could travel comfortably on a reinforced timber wagon.
  Tirzah knelt beside him. “We will meet you there.”
  Oru pushed his wooden wheel into her hand, then folded her fingers over it with his nose.
  She kept it in her pocket.
  That night, the brass map showed a second mark beneath the surface of an inland lake. It was small, fading, and moving down.
  Ember would not sleep until they turned toward it.
Chapter 9
The Lake That Holds the Sky
The observatory stood beneath transparent water. Its towers had been built in a dry basin, then drowned when a spring broke through the stone. Sunlight caught the lenses inside its domes and scattered moving stars across the lakebed.
  A red mark on the map pulsed beneath the largest dome.
  Asa built a diving bell from an abandoned cistern, sealing its cracks with pitch. They lowered it on a shore winch, while Ember gripped a second line in her teeth. Tirzah insisted on going with him.
  “If something jams, I can climb out,” she said.
  “You cannot breathe water.”
  “Neither can you. We have something in common.”
  The bell descended into green silence. Air pressed warm against their faces. Through its open bottom they watched stairways emerge from the gloom.
  At the dome, Asa anchored the bell beside an upper window. A narrow air pocket remained beneath the ceiling. Inside it, on a shelf tilted above the water, lay an egg the size of a bread oven.
  Its shell was white, threaded with gold.
  They climbed into the air pocket. Tirzah wrapped the egg in their sling. Asa examined the window frame, which was bending under the dome's weight.
  A crack traveled across the glass overhead.
  “Now,” he said.
  The ceiling broke before they reached the bell.
  Water struck Tirzah and tore her away from the shelf. Asa caught her sleeve. For an instant they hung together in a rushing green world, the egg's sling dragging at her shoulder.
  She did not release it.
  Asa planted his foot against the window and pushed them toward the bell. His lungs burned. Something golden flashed past him: Ember's eye, magnified by water.
  She had dived.
  Her foreclaw caught the bell's lifting ring. Asa shoved Tirzah beneath it. They surfaced inside the pocket of trapped air, choking, the egg between them.
  The bell rose violently. Through the open bottom Asa saw Ember beating toward daylight with the wrecked dome sinking behind her.
  On shore, she lay coughing lake water while Kesh licked her face with frantic little strokes.
  Tirzah unwrapped the egg. It was warm. A faint tapping came from inside.
  Asa laughed once, then found he could not stop trembling.
  A woman approached from the reeds carrying a physician's bag. Her name was Sivan. She had watched their descent from the abandoned observatory keeper's house.
  She examined Tirzah's bruised shoulder, then Ember's breathing.
  “That egg cannot stay cold,” she said. “And your dragon has swallowed something worse than water.”
  Far across the lake, a squat fortress moved along the shore on eight enormous wheels.
  Sivan followed Asa's gaze.
  “The furnace house,” she said. “They have been waiting for her.”
Chapter 10
The Thieves of Fire
Ember's flame failed at sunset.
  She tried to warm the egg. A thin blue flicker escaped her mouth, then vanished. Her chest contracted painfully. Kesh pressed against the shell, but his small body could not give it enough heat.
  Sivan showed Asa a translucent filament caught between Ember's teeth.
  “A collector snare. It drew tight when she crossed the lake outlet. The engine aboard that fortress is pulling heat through the remaining filament.”
  Asa looked toward the moving lights. “Cut it.”
  “I did. The engine has left its field locked to her throat collar.” She touched a tiny buried clasp under Ember's scales. “They tagged her before you found her. The map tracks the same marks.”
  Inside the physician's bag lay a second, smaller bag, embroidered with a child's uneven stitches. Sivan saw Tirzah notice it and quickly folded it away.
  They hid the egg in blankets beside Kesh and crossed the mudflats on foot. Ember followed slowly, pale sparks leaking from her nostrils.
  At the fortress, Asa crawled between turning wheels while Tirzah and Sivan climbed a maintenance ladder. The machinery smelled of hot stone. Through the grating overhead he saw glass reservoirs full of captive light.
  He found a brake, but the wheel beside it was too stiff to turn.
  Footsteps stopped above him.
  Neri crouched at the hatch.
  For a long moment neither spoke.
  Then the hunter reached down and helped him pull.
  The wheels locked. Tirzah opened a service door. Sivan ran to the control table, searching its numbered handles.
  A keeper lunged at her. Neri knocked him away, taking a blow across the knee. He went down hard.
  “Leave him!” Asa shouted as more guards entered.
  Tirzah looked at Asa with such hurt that he understood what he had said before the words finished echoing.
  He went back.
  Together they dragged Neri behind a reservoir. Sivan opened its release channel. Stored heat burst upward through a chimney, lifting the fortress roof in a roar of white light.
  Outside, Ember raised her head.
  Her answering flame struck the prison locks along the fortress side. Doors sagged open. Captive animals poured into the reeds.
  Asa cut the last retaining pin on the drawbridge. It fell across the ditch, making their escape road.
  They reached Kesh after midnight. Ember curled around the egg. This time a steady warmth spread through the blankets.
  Neri lay against a stone, his ruined knee swelling.
  “You came back,” he said to Asa.
  Asa sat beside him. “I am trying to become someone who does.”
Chapter 11
The Wild Dragon Isles
Neri could not ride behind Asa without pain, so they made a sling between two padded supports on Ember's saddle. He endured the arrangement in silence until Kesh stole his breakfast.
  
On the dark beach of the wild dragon isles, Tirzah shelters the egg as Ember faces the scarred guardian. Empty nesting ledges rise above them.
  “Your friend is a thief.”
  “He has excellent judgment,” Tirzah said.
  Sivan remained beside the lake to help the animals freed from the fortress. Before they left, she gave Tirzah the child's embroidered bag. It contained cloth for the egg and a small carved bird.
  “For luck,” she said. Her eyes were red.
  The wild islands appeared beyond the inland sea like black teeth. Trees clung to their slopes. Steam drifted from cracks along the beaches. Ember circled the highest island, calling.
  An answering roar struck them from above.
  A dark male dragon descended with six others behind him. His horns swept backward like branches. A pale scar crossed one eye.
  Ember folded her wings and landed on a narrow beach. The other dragons surrounded her. They sniffed Kesh, examined the egg, and bared their teeth at the humans.
  The great male seized Asa's pack and shook its contents onto the sand. Tools scattered. A chisel landed at his feet.
  He stepped back so sharply his tail knocked stones into the surf.
  Asa understood. Something like that tool had cut Oru's horns.
  He laid his remaining knife beside the chisel and moved away.
  Tirzah did the same. Neri struggled to unbuckle his sword. Finally he handed the entire belt to Asa, who placed it on the sand.
  The male looked at them for a long time.
  Then he rolled the egg out of its sling.
  Ember's growl shook the beach.
  Tirzah caught the shell before it struck a stone. She put herself between the male and the egg, knowing how little her body could stop.
  He lowered his enormous head until his breath blew her hair across her face.
  Behind him, in the cliff, Asa saw nesting ledges. Most were empty. On the occupied ones sat elderly males, a pair of injured juveniles, and one small dragon whose wings had never unfolded properly.
  There were no other adult females.
  Ember was more than the last dragon free on the mainland. She might be the last mother they had.
  The great male lifted his head and called toward the highest cliff.
  Ember answered.
  Neri leaned against Asa. “I think,” he said, “we have been invited to something unpleasant.”
Chapter 12
The Challenge on the Cliff
The nesting ledge could hold two dragons comfortably. Three would be a catastrophe.
  Asa stood against the cliff with Tirzah and Neri while Ember faced the scarred guardian. Below them, the sea rolled over submerged columns. The colony watched from neighboring shelves.
  The guardian spread his wings. Ember spread hers.
  Her mended left wing trembled.
  He struck first, a sweeping blow intended to drive her off the ledge. She ducked and caught his shoulder. They collided with a sound like falling trees.
  “Why fight her?” Asa whispered.
  Neri's face was gray. “Because she brought us. Because the last humans who came here took eggs.”
  The guardian pushed Ember toward the edge. She braced, claws cutting grooves through soft volcanic stone.
  A deep crack opened beneath their feet.
  Tirzah heard it before the dragons did.
  “Ember!”
  The ledge dropped a finger's width. Pebbles rattled into the sea. Kesh, who had been guarding the egg behind them, began dragging its sling away from the fracture.
  Asa seized one strap. Tirzah took the other. They hauled it toward the narrow path as the guardian lunged again.
  Stone failed under his hind leg.
  He slid sideways, one wing trapped against the wall. Ember could have driven him over. Instead she threw herself forward and caught the thick ridge above his shoulder.
  Both dragons began slipping.
  Neri pulled the rope from Asa's spilled pack. He looped it around a stone tooth. Asa took the loose end down the ledge, crawling close enough to fasten it around Ember's saddle.
  “Tell me when,” Tirzah said behind him.
  He remembered the aqueduct, her grip around his wrist.
  “Now.”
  She and Neri pulled. Kesh caught the rope in his teeth. Ember gained a clawhold, then another. With a final wrench she dragged the guardian onto solid rock.
  The ledge's outer lip fell away.
  For a while nobody moved.
  Then the guardian touched Ember's wounded wing with his nose. He lowered himself before her, not submissive so much as exhausted by a truth he could no longer fight.
  That evening he let Tirzah examine a cracked scale on his face. She called him Rook because he watched everything from the highest place.
  He accepted the name with a snort.
  The egg went into the warmest nesting hollow. Ember curled around it. When Tirzah started to leave, the dragon lifted her wing.
  There was room beneath it for two people.
  Asa lay beside Tirzah and listened to the egg tap softly through the night.
Chapter 13
The Storm Without Rain
The sky turned green three days later.
  No cloud covered the islands. The strange color spread through clear air, and every metal buckle on Asa's pack began humming.

Chapter 14
The Ship in the Trees
The transport lay across the crowns of seven enormous trees. Its bow had pierced a trunk. Its stern hung over empty air.

Chapter 15
Hatchling Trouble
They brought the rescued children to Meret's orchard, where news traveled faster than any dragon. Their families had been gathering there after the transport failed to arrive.

  One tiny wing remained pressed against her side.
  Tirzah touched Ember's cheek. “A daughter.”

Chapter 16
The Feast of False Kings

Chapter 17
The Dragon Hunt
Sivan's daughter was named Dena. The collectors had held her for months while Sivan worked in the furnace houses. Each refusal earned another promise that the child would disappear into a different province.

Chapter 18
The Sleeping Mountain
They sheltered in a ridge shaped like a sleeping animal.
  At its base, wind passed through hollows with a low, breathing sound. Inside, the walls curved into ribs. Pale mineral seams followed the pattern of scales.
  Asa touched one of the ridges.
  “An old carving?”
  Neri lifted their lantern. The chamber continued farther than its light could reach.
  “No sculptor worked this deep.”

  Tirzah smiled for the first time since the palace. Then water rippled across her boot.
  The pool was rising.
  A sound like distant thunder moved through the stone ribs. They had never heard thunder. Asa thought first of falling trees, then of a thousand loaded wagons crossing an underground bridge.
  The floor split beside the abandoned cages.
  Hot water surged through.
  Ember shoved Tirzah toward the entrance. Asa caught Neri under one arm. They ran while the chamber filled behind them.

  Outside, the dragon burst from the main entrance in a brown torrent. She rolled, coughing, and lay still until Tirzah reached her.
  The ridge kept breathing water into the valley.
  Asa unfolded the channel map with wet hands.
  They had discovered an escape route. They had also learned that the earth itself was beginning to close the roads.
Chapter 19
The Bridge of Wings
They reached the coast to find a settlement stranded on the wrong side of a broken gorge.
  
Dragons brace a suspended crossing above the broken gorge while villagers travel in wicker slings. Ember carries the children toward the safe bank.

The Siege of the Nests

Chapter 22
The Long Flight Home
There were thirteen dragons, counting Pearl.

The First Rain

  Tirzah climbed onto a roadside block.
  “The ark valley is west!” she called. “Follow the cedar markers!”
  Some people did. Others turned toward the high terraces, where they had relatives and stone houses. The official urged them toward the royal storehouses on the ridge.
  “They are built above the river,” he insisted.
  Neri caught his sleeve. “You saw the city.”
  The man looked at the child in his arms. Her mother had found them at the canal and now stood close beside him.
  For one moment he seemed ready to listen.
  Then thunder rolled across the hills. He flinched, pulled free, and hurried toward the terraces with the family. Neri called after him until the rain swallowed his voice.
  Ember tried to take off.
  Her injured wing opened unevenly. She rose above the roadside trees, faltered, and landed hard enough to drive mud over Asa's boots.
  They would have to walk.
  Neri gave Asa his staff. Tirzah supported him on the other side. Ember nudged the gray dragon onward while the two youngsters stayed close to his legs.
  They crossed a field where the crop stood half submerged. A farmer begged them to help move his oxen. They cut the tether, opened the gate, and led the animals onto the westward road.
  At the next marker, the road was gone.
  A new river crossed it, brown and fast, carrying fence rails and the roof of a shed. The wooden bridge lay broken against a stand of trees downstream.
  Asa sat on a stone because his ankle would no longer hold him.
  Tirzah knelt in front of him. Rain streamed over her face. He could not tell whether she was crying.
  “We said together,” she reminded him.
  He looked at the timber caught among the trees. Beams, planks, broad sections of a storage floor.
  “We need a raft.”
  She nodded once and rose.
  Ember lowered her head until her forehead touched his.
  Asa rested a muddy hand between her eyes.
  “I know,” he said. “I would rather fly too.”
Chapter 27
The River Run
The raft began as the floor of a grain shed.
  Asa lay on his stomach across its boards, directing Neri and Tirzah as they lashed crossbeams beneath it. Ember dragged timber out of the flooded trees. The gray dragon, whom Tirzah had begun calling Flint, pressed each piece into place with his forefoot.
  They worked while water climbed around their knees.
  By the time the raft floated free, the cedar marker had vanished.
  The two young dragons rode at the center. Neri took the steering pole. Tirzah held the forward line, and Asa watched the lashings.
  Ember entered the river beside them.
  She could swim. She could also pull. With Flint on the opposite side, she guided the awkward platform away from the trees and into the current.
  For a short while, the plan worked.
  Then the river bent toward the old mill.
  Its waterwheel still turned, driven faster than it had ever been designed to move. Broken spokes tore free. The channel narrowed between stone walls, drawing them straight toward it.
  “Left!” Asa shouted.
  Neri pushed the pole. It snapped against the bed.
  Tirzah cut the forward line before it could drag Ember into the wheel. Flint hauled the opposite rope, but his injured hindquarters could not turn them fast enough.
  Asa saw a mill rope hanging from the upper loading beam.
  “Tirzah.”
  She was already climbing the raft's piled timber.
  When they passed beneath the beam, she jumped.
  Her fingers closed around the rope. The raft shot onward beneath her. Asa caught her ankles, nearly losing his grip on the wet leather of her boots.
  The line tightened.
  Their raft swung sideways, scraped the stone wall, and drifted into a backwater beyond the wheel.
  The loading beam cracked. Tirzah dropped into Asa's arms with enough force to knock them both flat.
  Behind them, Ember vanished beneath the broken wheel.
  A copper tail struck the water. The wheel lifted, jammed, and shattered. She surfaced downstream with a long cut across her shoulder.
  Tirzah reached for her. The dragon approached slowly, keeping the raft between herself and the collapsing mill until the youngsters were clear.
  At the next ridge, they found Barak's wagon on a strip of surviving road.
  The captain stood in water to his waist, holding a lantern above his head.
  “You are late,” he said.
  Asa laughed because the alternative was something he could not yet afford.
  They pulled the raft ashore.
  Beyond Barak, through the rain, a line of lamps marked the last approach to the ark.
Chapter 28
The Wings at the Door
The yard had become a lake of mud.
  
In the first Flood rain, the dragons help Ember toward the lantern-lit ark. Tirzah stays beside her, and Pearl waits in the doorway.
  Workers guided the remaining animals up the broad boarding ramp. Above them the ark's entrance shone with lantern light. Asa had made hinges for that door. He had handled its pegs when they were smooth pieces of wood on his bench.
  Now it looked enormous enough to belong to another world.
  Barak carried Asa. Neri and Tirzah led Flint and the youngsters. Ember stayed behind to push a stranded wagon onto the raised approach.
  Inside its slatted pen, a pair of small horned animals trembled against each other.
  “Leave the wagon!” Neri called. “We can lead them!”
  Ember shoved again. The wagon lurched clear. The keepers opened the gate and brought the animals toward the ramp.
  Beneath Ember, the flooded edge of the yard gave way.
  She dropped into the drainage cut.
  Water closed over her shoulders. Her damaged wing caught beneath a fallen beam. She pulled once and stopped with a cry that made Tirzah turn before anyone shouted.
  Pearl, hearing her from inside the ark, began screaming.
  Asa struggled out of Barak's arms.
  “You cannot walk,” the captain said.
  “Then carry me back.”
  They reached the cut with rope, a saw, and four workers. Ember thrashed when they approached the trapped wing. Every movement wedged the beam more tightly.
  Tirzah stepped into the water.
  She laid both hands against Ember's jaw.
  The dragon stilled, but her eye remained fixed on the entrance. Pearl had been brought onto the ramp in Sivan's arms.
  Ember reached toward her with a trembling foreclaw.
  Tirzah understood. In every cage, every furnace house, every stolen nest, humans had taken the young.
  “She stays with us,” Tirzah said. “Look. Sivan has her. Rook is there. You can let them hold her.”
  Ember watched the tiny white head moving against Sivan's shoulder.
  Then she lowered her claw.
  Asa cut the beam where the grain had already split. Barak pulled. Neri secured a line around Ember's broad chest while the workers laid planks beneath her feet.
  The beam broke free.
  Ember climbed out, dragging her injured wing. Halfway up the ramp she faltered. Flint came alongside her. Rook reached down from the doorway.
  Together the dragons carried her weight.
  Tirzah stayed beneath her lowered head until they crossed the threshold.
  The great door closed behind them.
  For the first time in weeks, rain could no longer reach Asa's face.
  He discovered that he was crying anyway.
Chapter 29
Fire Beneath the Waters
The first movement of the ark felt like the ground changing its mind.
  Timbers spoke through the walls. Water struck the hull with heavy, uneven blows. A trough slid across the dragon enclosure until Barak and two keepers caught it.
  Then the whole great vessel lifted.
  Asa lay beside Ember's head with his ankle bound between wooden supports. Tirzah slept sitting up against his shoulder. Across the passage, Pearl dreamed beneath Rook's wing.
  They had survived the door.
  Survival inside it became another kind of work.
  They learned which animals panicked at a dragon's cough, which dragons would eat while watched, which noises meant a loose fitting and which belonged to the ship itself. They cleaned bedding, hauled water, repaired partitions, and slept in fragments.
  Ember's shoulder healed slowly. Her fire did not return.
  Sivan warned them against expecting it too soon. “She has spent everything getting here.”
  Weeks passed in lamplight. Pearl grew strong enough to climb the feeding gates, though one wing still opened less than the other. Neri made a game of coaxing her to stretch toward a dangling scrap of cloth. Kesh stole the cloth whenever he could.
  Then, during a night of violent rolling, water spilled through a service shaft above the enclosure.
  A roof baffle had torn loose. The shaft carried rain inward instead of sending it away. Bedding darkened. The youngest dragons scrambled against the partitions. A frightened animal in the adjoining pen broke its gate and jammed the passage.
  Asa woke to Tirzah pulling him upright.
  “We need your hands.”
  He climbed the inner service ladder while Barak held its base. Neri cleared the passage. Sivan and Tirzah moved Pearl and the youngsters to higher flooring.
  At the shaft's upper bend, Asa found the baffle wedged beyond his reach. He could not stand securely on his injured ankle. Every roll of the ark threw him against the wall.
  He began trying anyway.
  Below him Tirzah said his name once.
  He stopped.
  There were hands reaching up from the ladder. Hers. Barak's. A keeper holding a longer hook.
  Asa took the hook.
  He explained where to push while Tirzah climbed beside him and shifted the baffle's lower edge. Barak braced the ladder through another terrible roll.
  The wood moved.
  Water surged over Asa's arms, then turned outward into the drain. The shaft began breathing air again.
  Below, Ember had dragged herself around the shivering youngsters. She lay with her chest against their sodden bedding, giving the heat of her body while Flint and Oru sheltered them from the draft.
  Her breathing had become shallow.
  Tirzah dropped to her knees beside the copper head. “Stay.”
  It was the smallest request she had ever made of her, and the hardest.
  They worked through the night. Dry bedding came from another compartment. Rook shifted aside so the youngest could share his warm resting place. Sivan watched Ember while Asa repaired the drain from below.
  Near morning, the dragon's amber eye opened.
  She looked at Tirzah, then at Asa, then toward Pearl.
  A tiny warm flicker appeared between her teeth.
  It lasted less than a heartbeat.
  Tirzah laughed into Ember's scales. Asa sat on the floor beside them, too tired to speak.
  The flame itself could not warm the whole enclosure. But Ember was breathing steadily again, and for that morning it was enough.
Chapter 30
The Sky Belongs to Them Again
The ark came to rest long before the door opened.
  That waiting was harder than Asa expected. Solid ground lay somewhere beneath them, yet their world remained wood, lamplight, work, and the breathing of creatures.
  He carved a new saddle during those weeks.
  Its supports could be adjusted around Ember's scar. Its straps could be opened quickly. Where he had once fitted a rail to keep himself from falling, he left room for Tirzah's hands.
  She examined it beside him.
  “No cage this time.”
  “No rail,” he said.
  “Much improved.”
  When at last they stepped outside, the air smelled of wet stone.
  Nothing stood where the old roads should have been. The garden, the orchards, the race towers, the market with its unfortunate hats: all belonged to the world they carried in memory.
  Neri stood without speaking. Sivan held Dena against her side. Barak knelt and pressed one great hand into the earth.
  Asa had expected joy to come cleanly. Instead it arrived tangled with grief.
  Tirzah took his hand and kept it.
  The animals emerged in their own time. Oru walked slowly onto the slope and lifted his scarred muzzle toward the wind. Flint followed with his two young males. Kesh burst from the doorway, tripped on the threshold, and recovered his dignity in midair.
  Pearl tried to follow him.
  Her weaker wing dipped. She landed in a patch of wet grass, furious.
  Ember stepped beside her.
  She opened her own scarred wing, lowered it, and opened it again. Pearl watched. Tried. Fell. Tried once more.
  On the fourth attempt she rose above the grass.
  Only a little. Only far enough to reach Rook's waiting forefoot. The guardian lowered his head as if she had crossed the sea.
  That afternoon Asa and Tirzah fitted Ember's new saddle.
  “Are you sure?” Tirzah asked him.
  Asa looked down the enormous slope. “Absolutely not.”
  She smiled and climbed up first.
  He settled behind her, placing one arm around her waist. Ember turned her head to inspect them. For once there was no pursuit, no trapped creature calling, no wounded dragon waiting beyond the next ridge.
  She could have gone anywhere alone.
  She crouched and waited for Asa to check the final strap.
  Then she carried them into the sky.
  They flew above valleys cut by unfamiliar rivers. Clouds trailed shadows over bare hills. In a sheltered hollow, Tirzah spotted a patch of green and pressed Asa's hand against it in the air, as if together they could remember where to return.
  Ember's flight was different now. She favored the scarred side. Wide turns replaced the fierce rolls of their first journey. Asa felt every adjustment and loved the care she took with them.
  A dragon called from beyond the ridge.
  Ember answered.
  They rounded the hill to find Oru standing on a sunlit shelf, his wooden wheel at his feet. Below him, the others were spreading into the new valley. Kesh wheeled overhead. Pearl followed in short, determined flights between Rook and Flint.
  They were not the only living things beneath the sky.
  They were simply alive beneath it together.
  When Ember brought them home, Asa carried his tools to a hollow beside the camp. Tirzah set a bowl of rescued seeds on the ground. Between two stones, they marked a little garden.
  Beside it Asa began building a broad shelter with an open side facing the sunrise.
  Ember watched him fit the first wooden peg. Then she disappeared up the slope.
  She returned dragging an enormous fallen branch.
  Tirzah laughed so hard she had to sit down.
  Asa put his hands on his hips. “That is far too large.”
  Ember laid it carefully across his neat foundation.
  For a moment he considered explaining the measurements.
  Instead he took up his saw.
  “All right,” he said. “We will make it bigger.”
  The dragon settled beside their new garden, with Pearl asleep against her heart.

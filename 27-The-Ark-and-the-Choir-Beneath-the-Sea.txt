Chapter 1
The Hand in the Family Book
In October 2026, Daniel took the book out of the box because the rain had come through the ceiling.
Nothing grander brought him to it. He was not searching for a lost civilization. He was searching for the stopcock, which his grandfather had concealed behind a shelf of paint tins, and the cardboard box had been directly beneath the leak.
He carried it to the kitchen. The kettle was boiling. Upstairs, his daughter was supposed to be asleep.
Most of the contents were ordinary enough to hurt: a photograph of his grandmother looking away from the camera, receipts for a refrigerator that had stopped working before Daniel was born, a broken pair of spectacles. The book lay beneath them in a wrap of waxed cloth.
He remembered it from childhood. His grandfather never read from it while anyone was watching. Whenever Daniel asked about the hand on the opening leaf, the old man touched his sleeve and said they were still here.
Daniel had thought this an unsatisfactory answer. Now he would have given almost anything to hear it again.
The cloth smelled of dust and something faintly sweet. The binding was not leather. At its torn corner, fine crossing threads showed beneath a coating the color of dark honey.
He opened it carefully.
There was the hand.
He turned his forearm beneath the kitchen light. The tattoo had settled into his skin over twenty years, its lines no longer as sharp as when his grandfather first saw it and cried without explaining why.
Daniel laid his arm beside the page.
Under the hand lay a folded drawing. Its outer rectangle was marked 450 feet by 75, with 45 feet written beside an end view. A second plan ran farther across the paper: twenty-four numbered stations inside a 780-foot carrier.
Daniel unfolded another crease. There were rooms with ordinary names. Homes. School. A kitchen beside a dining hall. Someone had drawn two routes to the stairs and put tiny arrows beside the water stores.
His grandfather had repaired houses for a living. Daniel could almost see him following those arrows with a thick, work-worn finger.
His grandfather had not altered the design. Neither had the man who tattooed Daniel. The old drawing was the same hand.
On the facing leaf, in handwriting more precise than the pages that followed, someone had written a sentence in English.
THIS IS A FAMILY COPY. THE OLDER WORDS HAVE BEEN CARRIED INTO OUR LANGUAGE. KEEP THE NAMES.
Underneath was a list of people who had copied it. The most recent name was his grandfather's. The name before that belonged to the grandmother whose photograph now leaned against the kettle.
He turned another page.
The first line had been underlined twice.
We were not all aboard the Ark.
Daniel read it again.
There were other wooden ships. Some had left Atlantis before the sky broke. Some left when we could no longer keep the sea outside. This book was carried on the Lark. If you have been told that nobody came from us, keep reading.
A pipe knocked in the ceiling. He looked up, half expecting his grandfather to be standing in the doorway with a towel over one shoulder.
Instead, his daughter came down the stairs.
Mia was nine. She had a blanket around her shoulders and the determined expression of someone prepared to negotiate bedtime on very specific terms.
“Is the house sinking?”
“Not yet.”
She considered the puddle under the box.
“Why are you smiling?”
He hadn't known he was.
She pulled out a chair. He put his hand over the book before she could touch the damp corner, then felt foolish and moved it.
“It's Granddad's.”
“Can you read it?”
“I think so.”
“Then start.”
The next page began with a woman putting down a basket of beans because someone was knocking at her door.
Daniel lowered his voice, as if there were sleepers in another room of the book.
“Lazara had not meant to go to sea again.”
Chapter 2
The Door That Needed Planing
Lazara had not meant to go to sea again.
She had meant to shell the beans before the rain reached the garden, put a better latch on the henhouse, and persuade Elias to stop lifting things with his injured arm simply because he could now lift them badly.
He stood at the kitchen table, surrounded by the parts of an ordinary clock. There had been a time when the sight would have frightened her. Now its owner was a baker who wanted to know whether the oven had been hot for forty minutes or most of the morning.
“If that spring reaches the butter,” she said, “you're explaining breakfast.”
He shifted the clock.
Someone knocked.
The door stuck. Elias had promised to plane it in spring. Summer had become a reason not to spoil it, and autumn was now making its own argument.
Lazara pulled harder. Agent Venn waited outside with wet shoulders and a parcel held under her coat.
“This is a visit,” Venn said. “You may refuse it.”
That made Lazara look at the parcel.
They sat around the cleared half of the table. Venn laid down a narrow wooden box. Inside was a strip of dark translucent material, pierced at intervals. It resembled the old timing records, except the holes ran in pairs like footprints beside a stream.
“The northern station received a call,” she said. “Not from a giant. From beneath the southern sea.”
Elias's good hand stopped on the edge of the box.
“Atlantis?”
Venn nodded.
They had heard the name in broken archives: a harbor, a city, a promise that another place was still keeping the lights on. Nobody agreed whether it had survived.
“How many people?” Lazara asked.
“The caller couldn't finish.”
Venn put a second object on the table: a child's shoe, small enough to fit inside Elias's hand. Its sole had been repaired with blue thread.
“A courier capsule brought this through the lower passage. The message was wrapped around it. They wanted us to understand that they meant people.”
Elias went to the window. Across the lake, orchards climbed the enormous stone wrist. Aram and Sere remained where the disaster had left them, their joined hands holding the distance between two shores. Their speech still traveled through the liaison stations. Even mercy needed somebody to open the right line.
“The Ark?” he asked.
“At the Alaska hub. Its keeper agreed to a survey. It needs people who know how old timing systems fail.”
Lazara watched her husband. He was rubbing the place where his shoulder ached when the weather turned.
Venn did not tell him that only he could help. Lazara was grateful for that.
“We'll talk,” she said.
Venn left the box and took the shoe, then paused at the door.
The parcel also held a copy of the measuring book. Lazara opened it at the familiar six-deck section. The Homes deck sat above Commons; Learning stood above Homes; Gardens occupied the highest inhabited level. North Terrace used those names too, but its grounded walls had their own dimensions.
“Are they sending the refuge?” she asked.
“The wooden prototype,” Venn said. “The house stays where you left it.”
Elias drew the plans closer. His first question concerned the clean-water system. Lazara found that more reassuring than any declaration that the ship could do impossible things.
“No. You should keep that until you decide.”
That evening Elias finally planed the door. Lazara held the lamp. They worked slowly, stopping when his arm began to shake.
“I don't want us to need another disaster to know what to say,” she told him.
He looked up from the pale curl of wood at his feet.
“I want to go because I know something useful,” he said. “I don't want to go because I'm afraid staying home would make me worthless.”
“Which is it?”
“Both. I'm trying to separate them.”
She sat beside him on the doorstep.
“We go together. We tell them what we can do. When we can't do it, we say so.”
He leaned his head against hers.
Later, she put the beans in a bowl of water so their neighbor could finish them. Elias left the repaired clock on the baker's shelf. On their table, beneath the little shoe, they wrote the day they expected to return.
They knew enough now to call it an expectation.
Chapter 3
Beneath the Northern Pyramid

The northern pyramid and its surviving root chambers
BRIAN CHAPMAN SR MSP A+ CIW
The pyramid's highest stones were broken.
Lazara saw them from the descending lift, pale blocks scattered around a cap that no longer fitted its base. Beyond the shielded approach, Alaska lay under autumn snow. Below it, the structure continued for miles.
Some levels were empty galleries lined with dead glass. Others held reservoirs, root courts, and service roads wide enough for houses to pass one another. A flood stain crossed the walls of the seventh level. On the ninth, a row of staircases ended in clean air where a whole gallery had fallen away.
The complex had survived. That did not mean it had escaped being damaged.
The keeper met them at the lower dock. Her name was Aven. She had a burn along her jaw and a habit of waiting until a machine stopped making noise before she answered a question.
“People arrive expecting perfection,” she said as they crossed a bridge above a dry reservoir. “Then they ask why we haven't cleaned it.”
In the dock, the Ark waited.
Its timber hull filled the chamber with a familiar warmth. Four hundred and fifty feet long, seventy-five across, forty-five deep: the proportions were unchanged, even though what those timbers admitted was larger than the space they enclosed.
Elias stopped before the gangway. He removed his ring.
Lazara did the same. Aven supplied two numbered wooden boxes for their metal possessions. Buckles, small tools, the chain of Lazara's lantern: everything that could answer the magnetic machinery stayed ashore.
The keeper checked the pockets of their packs with permission, then showed Elias a wooden fastening he had overlooked beneath a strap.
“That one may stay,” she said, and he laughed in relief.
Inside, lamps burned in bowls of resin. Wooden pegs held the joints. Fiber rope passed through smooth timber blocks. Even the hinges were wood, polished by many hands.
Aven stopped them inside the side door before showing them anything impossible.
“H05,” she said, touching the frame mark. “Seventy-five feet from the stern.”
The opening was twelve feet wide and ten feet high. Beyond it lay a receiving vestibule twenty-four feet long and eighteen feet across. Lazara set down her bag beside a water bench. Through the open leaf she could still see the quay.
Ahead, honey-colored resin bordered another wooden jamb. A red-brown mat ended at its foot. Beyond the seam, six feet of pale boards led into a rising passage.
“Keep the entrance in mind,” Aven said. “You'll need to explain it to people who are frightened.”
The folded approach ran for a hundred and twenty feet, rising seven feet six inches. Elias could manage its gentle slope without pulling on the rail. At the top they turned into the stern vestibule. Twenty-four numbered timber frames receded ahead, each thirty feet beyond the last.
The route plate read S01-C.
Aven unrolled the section against the rail. “The carrier is seven hundred and eighty feet long, a hundred and forty-four wide, and the same high. Thirty feet of vestibule at either end. Seven hundred and twenty feet of numbered stations between them. The approach we just climbed has its own address.”
Lazara looked back. The short receiving room and its square of quay daylight remained beyond the turn. Nothing had pushed through the outer bow.
“The tower had twenty-four floors,” Aven continued. “Their order became these twenty-four stations. The parent building stayed on its foundations.”
They walked the twelve-foot-wide Common Spine. At S04 a cart disappeared through a service door toward the cargo floor below; at S08 an attendant waited beside an assisted lift. Ordinary voices came from the household deck overhead.
Elias traced the six letters on the directory. Keel at zero. Foundry at twelve feet. Commons at twenty-four. Homes at thirty-six. Learning at forty-eight. Gardens at sixty. Above the garden deck, the remaining height held light, frame, and service bridges.
“I thought that space was all more rooms,” he said.
“Then you would have promised beds we haven't built.”
By the time they reached S12, Lazara understood why Aven had made them walk. A remembered drawing had acquired a smell of bread, someone waiting at a lift, and a distance her legs could measure.
A separately registered return panel beside the twelfth station showed the parent building; the common table hall remained at S12-C, behind its own ordinary door. The connection flickered. Through it Lazara saw the Alaska dock, doubled as if reflected through rain.
“We cannot use that for evacuation,” Aven said. “Too much uncertainty between the plates. We travel bodily until the destination is stable.”
She took them forward to S21-L, where the route registry occupied a room only twenty-four feet by eighteen. Wooden shutters named source, destination, local time, and return. Two desks faced twelve panels. One operator read each name; another checked the paper copy.
“The large spaces have boundaries,” Aven said. “The registry keeps their identities straight. A door going dark doesn't erase the room behind it. But a room you cannot reach cannot receive a passenger.”
Lazara stood behind the waist-high rail and watched the Alaska return panel dim. The smallness of the room made its responsibility easier to understand.
Lazara touched a rail. Beneath its worn surface, a fine vibration moved toward the bow.
Aven watched her.
“The parent tower gives the instruction. The wood keeps the ship from becoming part of the storm. It was a remote outpost before anyone needed a refuge.”
From far below, something answered the hull.
Not a motor. A long low note that seemed to pass through the space behind Lazara's ribs.
The keeper went still.
The note came again, farther away.
“Our escort,” Aven said.
Through the dock's lower viewing slit, a whale moved beneath the sea ice. A pale scar ran across its back. It turned once, presenting the old wound to the light, then slid into the dark water under the pyramid.
Above them, snow gathered in the broken capstone. The dock gates opened below the snow line, and the wooden ship began its descent toward an ocean that had been waiting longer than the people inside it could remember.
Chapter 4
The Passage That Sang
The Ark did not dive like a metal vessel.
Water rose around the dock until the outer gates vanished. Aven stood at a timber lectern watching a glass window set in the chamber wall beyond the hull. The window belonged to the hub. Its marks moved as the surrounding passage changed.
When the lowest mark held steady, she lifted a wooden lever.
The ship slid forward.
Lazara expected the sea to close over the deck. Instead the water curved around an enclosing surface she could feel only when her ears popped. Beyond it, small creatures flashed silver and were gone.
“A carried harbor,” Elias said.
Lazara remembered the northern dock's own sheet: six hundred feet long, a hundred and eighty wide, with thirty-six feet of water in its basin. Outside it, the receiving channel narrowed to a hundred and twenty feet, with seventy-two feet of clear air above the operating water line.
The Ark's seventy-five-foot beam fitted that passage. Its wider carrier occupied another registered space. Aven had made Elias read both sets of figures aloud before releasing a line.
Now, as the hull entered the bend, the keeper followed a marked nine-hundred-foot centerline radius on the local chart. Lazara watched the wall pass, slowly enough to see the old repairs in it. For all the wonder beyond the glass, they still needed room to turn.
“While the connected stations agree,” Aven replied. “If they stop agreeing, wood is still better than a field full of metal. But we will want to be near the surface.”
No one pretended that meant they were safe.
The whale traveled ahead of them. Its tail rose and fell beyond the pale extent of the shield. Whenever it called, a handful of lamps near the forward window trembled.
Elias wrote down the intervals.
By the third call, he had stopped writing.
“It isn't repeating.”
Aven looked at the page.
“Does that trouble you?”
“The first call measured the channel. The second changed after we passed the fracture. The third is waiting for a reply.”
As if it had heard him, something answered from far below.
Lazara had mistaken the shapes outside for mountains. Then the ship turned, and an immense hollow appeared between them. Five ridges descended into a submerged plain.
A hand.
Stone had replaced a person so completely that the seabed had begun to forget the difference. Sediment filled the palm. Coral grew along a ridge that might once have carried a fingerprint. The scale made recognition harder, then impossible to escape.
“An adult woman,” Aven said quietly. “Before the rupture.”
“You know who she was?”
“No. Atlantis has a name in its records. I won't borrow it before we ask.”
The whale turned beneath the curled fingers. A green light appeared in the darkness beyond them, then another.
Lazara leaned closer to the window.
The lights were windows.
There were rows of them, streets of them, galleries set inside a cavity sheltered by the fallen arm. At the city's far edge, whole districts stood dark. Water moved through them, carrying pale fronds that might once have been garden plants.
In one lighted window, somebody was waving a cloth.
Lazara waved back before thinking whether they could see her.
The whale called.
Across the city, a line of amber signals appeared in answer. One failed halfway through. The others waited, brightened, and began again from the beginning.
“They're making room,” Elias said.
The Ark moved into a receiving chamber. Water descended outside its protected harbor, lowering past the windows, past a broken crane, past stone stairs littered with sand. A door opened above the last wet step.
Nobody came through at first.
Lazara smelled warm stone, cooking onions, and the sourness of clothes that had never dried properly.
Then she heard a child coughing.
She was already at the rail when the first woman stepped into the dock.
Chapter 5
How Many Can You Carry
The first woman through the doorway carried a sleeping child.
She stopped when she saw Lazara. Behind her, the passage was crowded, but nobody pushed. They watched the open door as though a sudden movement might close it.
“How many can you carry?”
Lazara looked back into the Ark. The lamps were burning along the wooden passage. Someone had left a blanket over a rail.
“How many are there?”
The woman shifted the child against her shoulder.
“We don't know anymore. We lost the eastern gardens last night.”
A deep note passed through the dock.
Lazara felt it under her feet before she heard it. The woman closed her eyes. Around them, people lifted their faces.
Outside, beyond the submerged windows, a whale was calling.
The child woke.
“Is that the morning one?” he asked.
His mother touched his hair. “Yes.”
But Lazara had heard that call on the journey. It belonged to the scarred whale that had guided them through the northern passage.
“It followed us here,” she said.
The woman looked at her.
“No. That one comes every morning.”
Lazara stepped toward the window.
Beyond the glass lay a street, drowned so long ago that pale branches of coral grew from its balconies. Farther out, the street vanished beneath an enormous stone hand. Sediment filled the creases between its fingers. Small white creatures drifted above a broken nail.
Something moved beyond it.
The whale rose slowly into view. It passed the glass, turned, and waited.
The little boy pressed his palm against the window.
For a moment, Lazara could say nothing.
These people had lived beneath the sea for generations. Somewhere above them, mothers had brought their calves down through the darkness and taught them where to sing.
Behind her, Elias began taking down the blankets.
“Bring the children aboard,” he said.
Aven opened the recessed waiting bay beside the side-door vestibule. Twelve feet of doorway looked generous until a frightened family stopped inside it with all their bedding.
“Bags beside the bench,” she told them. “Keep the pale strip clear.”
Elias stayed at the inner seam. Beyond the sloped approach, an attendant would guide arrivals to S03-L for examination or to S08's quiet room if they needed somewhere to sit. Nobody would be sent down the long spine alone.
Lazara could now give the mother something more useful than the sight of an immense ship: the name of the next person who would take her child's hand.
The woman didn't move.
“What is it?” Lazara asked.
“We were told you would have the others.”
“The other ships?”
She nodded.
Lazara looked through the open doorway at their single wooden vessel.
Then the lights failed.
Across the dock, emergency lamps came on in a thin red line. A shutter dropped somewhere in the city. They heard it strike, followed by another, farther away.
The woman held her child tighter.
Lazara reached for her hand.
“Show me where they kept the plans.”
The woman's name was Sella. Her son was Iven. Lazara learned those things as they walked, because she did not want the woman to become only the person who had asked how many.
At the first stair, Sella handed Iven to Elias. The boy accepted the arrangement with the worn patience of a child who had recently been carried by many frightened adults.
“Can you stay awake?” Elias asked him.
“Can you?”
“Probably not.”
Iven leaned against his good shoulder.
Sella led Lazara into the red passage. Behind them, the Ark's resin lamps remained steady. For the first time since entering the city, Lazara heard someone laugh.
Chapter 6
The Ships They Had Not Finished
The archive door opened into a shipyard.
Lazara had expected shelves. Instead she found the ribs of an unfinished wooden hull rising through four galleries, its beams pale under a layer of dust. Beyond it stood a second ship, smaller and farther advanced. A third rested beneath folded scaffolding with half its deck in place.
“We keep the drawings where people use them,” Sella said.
An old man came down a ladder carrying a bowl of glue. His name was Teren. His apron had been patched so often that none of its original fabric appeared to remain.
He looked at Elias's empty hands.
“Where are your tools?”
“The metal ones stayed north.”
“Good. You can borrow ours.”
Teren set the glue down and led them beneath the smallest hull. Wooden pegs stood proud of joints that had never been tightened. On a bench nearby lay clamps, mallets, wedges, and a plane with a dark stone blade.
“They were building more?” Lazara asked.
“They were repairing more. These aren't the original fleet. Each generation replaced what the previous one couldn't finish.”
He took a strip of material from a chest. The same paired holes marked its edge as on Venn's message.
Sella laid it over a board. Light rose through the holes, falling into a rough drawing of a tower laid lengthwise.
“The first Ark copied a building,” she said. “The building could keep working after the outer world failed. Wood let an outpost receive its instructions without becoming a conductor for everything that went wrong.”
“There were others,” Elias said.
Teren nodded toward the far wall.
Names had been carved there in columns. Some were crossed by a short line. Others ended with a date. Many had nothing beside them at all.
“Departures,” the old man said. “Before the storm. During it. After. We're the people who stayed to keep the harbor open.”
Lazara traced one name with her eyes: Lark.
“This one?”
“A name reused. The little ship behind you carries it now. Its first namesake left before the outer complex blew.”
A knock traveled through the floor. Dust loosened from an overhead beam.
Sella counted under her breath until the shaking stopped.
“How much time?” Lazara asked.
“Yesterday they said twelve days.”
“And today?”
“Today they don't come down to the garden.”
Elias walked around the smallest hull, touching nothing until Teren invited him closer. He looked at the beams, the seams, the missing connection frame.
“This can float.”
Teren's face changed very slightly.
“Yes.”
“Can it carry a harbor?”
“Not without the heart agreeing to a new station.”
“Then we need two plans,” Elias said. “One for a connected ship. One for a wooden ship that has to stay on ordinary water.”
Teren spread the full section. Lazara recognized the hundred-and-forty-four-foot square of the carrier and, beside it, the much narrower ordinary hull. They were connected by a marked threshold, not fitted one inside the other like boxes.
The three local ships had separate sheets. Their smaller frames did not inherit the Ark's twenty-four stations merely because they copied its wooden joints.
“And the harbor?” Elias asked.
“A separate annex. Yours has one too.”
“People's door at S20-C,” Aven said. “Twelve by ten. Ships use the large transfer berths.”
Teren pushed a small carved hull toward the door symbol. It would not pass between the marks.
“We have made that mistake in stories,” he said. “We won't make it with a boat.”
He drew a line down a clean board with a piece of charcoal.
Lazara watched the old shipwright turn toward him. It was the look people gave when somebody finally asked the question that belonged to their hands.
Outside the shipyard, the red lamps continued to burn.
Chapter 7
A City Counted by Doorways
The council chamber had forty-eight seats and no useful count of the city.
Its clerk, Mera, admitted this before anybody else could make an accusation. She spread three ledgers across the floor because the table was occupied by a map pinned down with cups.
“One is food allocation. One is housing. One is people who have reported since the garden failure. They overlap. Some of the names belong to the dead.”
Councilor Ordan stood beside the window. Beyond him, a long black crack crossed the outer glass, stopping beneath an old repair.
“We have a provisional estimate.”
“You have three,” Mera said.
Lazara sat on the floor with her.
By noon they understood the size of the mistake. The food lists omitted the people who shared meals. The housing rolls treated flooded rooms as empty even when their occupants had moved onto an aunt's floor. Children born since the last registry had become notes in margins.
Ordan wanted the Ark loaded at once. The strongest would leave first, carrying tools to establish a shore camp. They could return for the rest.
“How?” Aven asked.
“With the ship.”
“The channel is narrowing. I cannot promise another arrival.”
The councilor looked toward Elias, who had come straight from the yard with glue on his sleeve.
“Can the fleet be made ready?”
“Three hulls. Different capacities. We haven't inspected all the seams.”
“That's not an answer.”
“It's the answer we have.”
Sella stood in the doorway. Iven slept against her shoulder again.
“Start with doors,” she said.
People turned.
“There are six occupied districts. Each kitchen knows the people on its stair. Ask them. Don't make them come here to prove they live.”
Ordan rubbed his forehead. He had not slept. Lazara could see that his fear was real; it had simply found a way to wear other people's authority.
“And while we count?” he asked.
“We work,” Mera said.
Aven borrowed a blank sheet and copied the Ark's receiving route. Hull H05. Inner seam. S01-C. The assisted route through S02, with another lift at S08. Care at S03-L. Temporary accommodation at S22-H.
“Those are places,” Mera said, putting a finger beneath the last address. “I need a person beside every one.”
They assigned attendants before assigning more beds. Aven kept the protected stair at S23 available at the forward end and forbade luggage on either stair landing. Lazara volunteered to walk the route with the slowest arrivals. A man who had offered to carry crates asked to help at the lift instead.
The map began to resemble a plan people could use.
She tore blank leaves from the back of an old ledger. Six runners took them. On each sheet she wrote the same headings: name, doorway, people accompanying, assistance needed, skills offered.
“No worth column,” Lazara said when one councilor suggested ranking the occupations.
The man flushed. Mera kept writing.
That afternoon Lazara went with the western runner. They found an elderly woman who would not leave without a neighbor's cat, a man caring for his brother who had forgotten both their names, and a stair full of children certain that the surface had no ceiling and was therefore unsafe.
At every doorway, somebody knew one more person.
By evening the sheets lay across the council floor. Mera read the total twice.
Eight hundred and forty-six.
She left room beneath it.
“For the people we missed,” she said.
Lazara went back to the Ark with a list of medicine requirements and a small boy's request for a window seat.
Elias was asleep on a bench beside the gangway. She woke him gently. He looked up in the lamplight and asked whether she'd eaten.
She had forgotten.
He moved over. There was enough bread for two.
Chapter 8
The Eastern Dawn
The garden doors were sealed, but Sella still had a key.
It opened the service stair above the flooded level. Lazara followed her across a gallery whose windows had been painted blue to soften the old lamps. The paint had flaked in places, revealing the raw yellow beneath.
“I worked here,” Sella said.
She did not say before.
Below them, water filled the paths between orchard terraces. Some trees still stood above it, their leaves folded inward. Others had fallen, roots lifted from shallow beds. The eastern lamps flickered through false sunrise, then afternoon, then a dark so sudden Lazara stepped back.
When the light returned, an old woman was standing on a stair across the garden.
“Sella?”
Sella swore softly.
They crossed by the upper bridge. The woman had a basket full of seed envelopes and a potted tree beside her foot.
“You were told to leave,” Sella said.
“They sent two boys. Neither knew which seed cabinet belonged to the salt roots.”
Her name was Noma. She had taught Sella how to graft a branch, and Iven how to count by pushing beans into rows of soil.
Lazara took the basket. It was heavier than it looked.
“Come and tell us which seeds they are.”
Noma studied her for a moment, deciding whether this was kindness with a lie inside it. Then she lifted the little tree.
They were halfway back when the morning lamps failed for the last time.
In the emergency light, the flooded orchard looked older. It could have been a ruin they were discovering instead of a place whose gardener was walking beside them.
Noma stopped at the blue-painted window.
“My mother stood here when they first got these working. She said it was almost like the dawn in her mother's stories.”
Sella put a hand beneath the tree's pot.
“Was it?”
“I don't know. I've never seen the other kind.”
On the return stair, they found a maintenance worker sitting with his head between his knees. Water had reached the lower doors faster than he expected. He was ashamed that he'd left his tools.
Noma gave him the tree to carry.
“Then carry this.”
Back at the yard, Mera added four names to the list: Noma, the worker, and two boys found sleeping in the equipment gallery after trying to reach her.
Eight hundred and fifty.
Elias tested the little tree's pot for hidden metal ties before taking it aboard. Noma watched without annoyance.
“Careful man,” she said to Lazara.
“Some days.”
They reached the garden through S10-G. The carrier-side landing was thirty-six feet by twenty-four, with shrubs in a trough beside a bench. Noma paused there, thinking they had arrived.
Then Aven opened Garden West.
A twenty-four-foot vestibule widened into a central walk. Beyond it, six terraces descended toward a reflecting basin. The country of planting measured twelve hundred feet by six hundred, and its sky volume rose a hundred and eighty feet above the walk. A wall enclosed it; the doorway behind them stayed an ordinary twelve-by-ten-foot rectangle.
Noma stared at the trees. After the flooded eastern orchard, their quiet leaves seemed almost indecent.
She did not ask how many people could sleep beneath them. She asked who inspected the water houses.
Aven pointed to the staff gates at the corners. Behind the twenty-four-foot boundary wall ran a separate service passage. The annex had pumps to tend, water to account for, and roots that could still die.
“Good,” Noma said, and Lazara heard what she meant. This garden needed her.
They used the gentler cart path rather than the water steps. Near the western receiving terrace, Noma placed her tree beside a trough and knelt with difficulty to feel the soil.
“Not enough room,” she murmured.
Lazara waited.
The gardener looked up.
“For the roots, eventually. A good problem to be here for.”
Chapter 9
A Window Above the World

The orbital shipyard as the magnetic storm arrives
BRIAN CHAPMAN SR MSP A+ CIW
The first space scene opened on a cracked wall.
Sella had brought them to the observatory because its timing records fed the harbor heart. The chamber stood above the old gardens, far enough from the flooded districts that the floor remained dry. Bronze rings surrounded a glass bowl full of pale light.
No part of it belonged aboard the Ark.
Elias left his pack outside and followed a woman named Ves around the observation rail. She had kept the device alive by borrowing working pieces from six dead rooms.
“It isn't a current view,” she warned. “The sky in here ended before my grandmother was born.”
She turned a ring.
The wall opened onto stars.

Chapter 21

Ahead, the Orchard sailed within a transparent curve of rising ocean. Beyond it lay the Mercy and Lark, their lamps tiny against the stars.
Elias came to the rail carrying Noma's rescued seed tray.
“She wants to know whether this light will hurt them.”
Lazara stared at him.
“What did you tell her?”
“That I don't know. She asked me to move them away from the window.”
They moved the seeds.
Outside the channel, a derelict metal hull drifted in the seam between plates. Its rings were broken like the ones in Ves's record. Light caught a line of windows, some dark, some reflecting a sun the Ark could not see.
Nobody suggested searching it. Their passengers were frightened, their water was measured, and the route behind them was closing.
Lazara watched until it passed out of sight.
They would not turn every mystery into work that had to be done tonight.
The whale sang below the forward boundary. A second voice answered farther ahead. Through the ship's timbers came a vibration deep enough to feel like the sea remembering itself.
The Lark's signal reached them: approaching ordinary horizon.
At the warning mark, Aven called in the remaining carrier watch. The passengers had already moved to their assigned berths in the three-story hull; now the operators made their final checks at S21-L and returned by the ordinary route.
Mera came last from S01, following the stern vestibule and the hundred-and-twenty-foot folded approach. Lazara waited on the red-brown mat. Behind Mera, the pale landing still looked solid. Beyond the turn lay the same seven hundred and eighty feet of carrier, its household rooms and secured annexes holding their registered places.
Nothing shortened at S12. No inhabited floor withdrew beneath a foot.
Mera read the last names. Lazara answered for herself. Elias answered beside her. Aven checked the receiving vestibule, twenty-four feet by eighteen, and compared the return identity one final time.
Then she secured the inner seam. The knife-dark line closed within its honey-resin reveal. The carrier remained a bounded place beyond a closed threshold, its local services in the condition they had recorded. Access would be tested again at a verified destination.
On this side lay the Ark's ordinary middle story, its twelve-foot aisle and timber stairs. A child asked whether the garden had drowned.
“We shut its door,” Lazara told him. “We wrote down where it is.”
Elias read the soundings aloud. The ordinary hull already carried its checked passengers, water, and stores. Closing a spatial connection had not emptied the carrier's reservoirs into it or made its timbers grow.
They were within the figures.
Lazara let herself breathe.
Ahead, the star-filled curve opened onto a strip of pale gold. A wind entered carrying the smell of rain and living leaves.
The first wave without a timing boundary struck the bow.
The Ark rose over it, timber creaking, rope taking the strain. A child cried out. Someone else laughed. Water blew across the outer deck.
Above them stood an ordinary sky.
Chapter 28
The Shore with People on It
They reached the bay at dawn.
The Lark had anchored behind a low island. The Mercy lay beyond it. The Orchard's crew was lowering a boat when people appeared on the shore carrying poles with cloths tied to them.
At first Dar mistook the movement for a warning.
Then one of the shore people put down his pole, stepped into the shallows, and spread both empty hands.
The first conversation took most of the morning.
Their words were related but badly worn by distance. Aven understood enough to ask about fresh water. Ves understood enough to realize the shore people were asking how long the ships had been missing.
“They know what we are,” she said.
Lazara climbed into the second boat.
Before lowering it, she walked from the middle receiving bay to the aft stair. The linked carrier door was shut. People were waking behind cloth screens in the ordinary hull, some stiff from sharing closer quarters than the household courts had offered. Someone had left an animal basket beside the water bench, clear of the aisle.
She touched the familiar H05 mark on her way out. A long voyage had brought them back to a door seventy-five feet from the stern, with a real shore beyond it.
On the quay-to-boat list she wrote each household's temporary landing place. The sea passage was over. Losing somebody between a ship and a beach would still be losing somebody.
On the beach, a woman named Anet watched the approaching hull with a face set carefully against hope. She wore a woven coat and had sand on the hem of her trousers. Beside her stood a boy holding a wooden bowl of water as though it were an official document.
When Ves said Atlantis, Anet began speaking so quickly that the translation failed.
She called for somebody behind her.
An old man came down the path carrying a board wrapped in cloth. On it was a worn copy of the hand.
Sella made a sound beside Lazara.
She had come ashore from the Lark with Iven and his seedling. Now she knelt in the sand because she could no longer stand.
Anet knelt opposite her.
They held the board between them without either trying to take it.
The bay people were descended from an earlier wooden vessel, the Reed, which had reached this coast generations after the first scattering. Their records named other ships and inland families. Over time they had traded, married, moved along rivers, and helped settle country left damaged by the Flood.
They had not filled a conveniently empty world. Other survivors had been here. There had been disputes, kindnesses, hungry seasons, and years of learning how to live together. The old ships had become timber in homes, patterns in stories, and names people gave their children.
The Reed's final captain had kept watch for Atlantis long after the harbor signal stopped.
Anet pointed toward the headland. A little stone shelter stood there facing the sea.
“We still mend the roof,” Ves translated.
Lazara went to sit beside Sella. Iven put his pot down in the sand and looked up at the sky.
“Where does it end?” he asked.
Nobody answered at first.
Then Anet's boy offered him the bowl of water.
Iven drank, made a face at its unfamiliar taste, and handed it back.
The boys considered each other.
“Can your plant come out?” the shore child asked slowly.
Sella began to laugh through her tears. Ves translated, and Iven inspected the ground with the solemn care he had learned from Noma.
“Not here,” he said. “Too much salt.”
The other boy nodded and pointed inland.
They had found something they could discuss.
Chapter 29
A Place for the Names
Noma planted her tree above the bay three days after landing.
She supervised from a chair because the ankle was still swollen. Four children dug the hole. A fifth fetched water. Ordan stood holding the pot and waited for permission to tip it.
“Gently,” she said.
“I have been a councilor for twenty years.”
“Then this will be useful practice.”
He grinned and lowered the roots into the soil.
The settlement grew first as an arrangement of shelter: sailcloth roofs, shared kitchens, washing lines, a storehouse whose doorway had to be widened for the oldest passenger's stool. People from the bay brought boards, fish, and more advice than the newcomers could use at once.
Some passengers wanted to remain near the ships. Others asked about inland land. The fleet's smaller vessels would eventually travel along different coasts, carrying families, seed stock, and copies of the record. The Ark would return north when the sea route and its passengers permitted.
Nothing required every rescued person to want the same future.
Mera and Ves worked in the headland shelter with Sella. They compared the old Reed record to the new lists. Dates disagreed where the plates had carried different seasons. Spellings had changed. A surname appeared in one account as the name of a river.
They preserved the differences instead of smoothing them into certainty.
Lazara wrote the account of Ilyra and her request for boots. Elias added the measurements of the launch sequence, the failures, and the checks that had stopped them from trusting the wrong frame.
He included the drawings too. The 450-by-75-by-45-foot hull. The separate 780-by-144-by-144-foot carrier. Twenty-four stations, six deck levels, two protected stair locations, and the assisted lifts. Garden West had its own twelve-hundred-by-six-hundred-foot sheet; Harbor South had its eighteen-hundred-by-nine-hundred-foot basin and separately marked large berths.
Mera insisted on the closed-route records being copied with them. A later reader should know which door had been usable on which day. She also wrote the two passenger arrangements in different columns: where families had waited while the linked rooms were open, and where they traveled after the last threshold was secured.
“Otherwise someone will say we had all that room and wonder why we needed four ships,” Lazara said.
“Let them see the water sheets,” Mera replied.
Elias added the nominal nine-hundred-resident service schedule and the actual departure allowance beneath it. The difference occupied a few lines. It had occupied their whole week.
Sella wrote about the first woman through the Ark's door.
“Do I have to say it was me?” she asked.
“Only if you want to.”
She thought about it, then wrote her name.
On the opening page of the Lark's copy, she placed the old hand drawing. The survivors who already carried that mark kept its established form. Others copied it into family records. Over years it would pass into skin, cloth, wood, and the margins of books, surviving in ways no council could have ordered.
Sella did not give it a new official explanation.
Beneath it she wrote: We were not all aboard the Ark.
Ves read the sentence and looked out at the four ships.
“Keep that.”
Before Lazara and Elias left the headland, Iven asked them to hear the morning whale.
They walked down to the beach. The scarred back appeared beyond the harbor mouth. A smaller whale surfaced beside it, waited, and answered the long note with a shorter, uncertain one.
The older animal called again.
Iven listened with his eyes closed.
“It's teaching it,” he said.
“Yes,” Lazara replied.
He opened his eyes.
“Does it know Atlantis is gone?”
She watched the sea settle around the two bodies.
“I don't know.”
The boy leaned against his mother. She put an arm around him without taking her eyes from the water.
That evening Lazara and Elias sat beneath Noma's tree. Its leaves had folded in the real dusk. Above the bay, the first stars appeared without a mechanism choosing their hour.
“Home?” Elias asked.
“Soon.”
He leaned against her. She held his hand and told him about something that had happened while he was away checking the hull. A child had tried to plant a spoon because it was made of wood.
He laughed until his shoulder hurt, then complained, and she kept laughing.
Below them, the ships rose and fell at their moorings.
Chapter 30
The Reader at the Kitchen Table
Daniel stopped reading because Mia had fallen asleep against his arm.
The kettle was cold. Upstairs, the leak had slowed to a regular tap inside the bowl he had placed beneath it. Dawn light gathered at the kitchen window.
He had intended to read a few pages.
The family copy was thicker than he remembered. Between the account and the back cover lay other hands: notes about a sailing coast, the building of a town, a winter when the seed store failed. Dates appeared, disappeared, and returned in forms he understood.
An early copyist had written that the Lark traveled onward when its passengers were ready. Its families settled on more than one shore. The book passed to a daughter who could read the old language, then to a grandson who could not but kept it because his mother had asked him.
Later relatives translated what they could. They marked the words they could not agree on. They did not make the ships into one ship merely because the story became easier that way.
Near the end, Daniel found a page in his grandmother's handwriting.
Her family name stood beside a reference to an older list.
He turned back carefully.
There it was, changed by language and copying but traced in a chain of notes across generations. Beside it were the words Lark household and a drawing of the same hand.
It was not a scientific proof laid conveniently on his kitchen table. It was a family book telling him how its people had understood their survival. Daniel knew the difference. He could love it without pretending he had solved the world.
What undid him was smaller.
His grandfather had written a note beside the first page.
I thought I would tell you when you were older. Then you were older, and I did not know where to begin.
Daniel sat very still.
He remembered the old man standing in this kitchen after a hospital visit, asking whether the kettle worked. He remembered answering too quickly because he was late. There had been so many chances for an ordinary conversation that neither of them had known how to start.
Mia moved in her sleep.
He brushed her hair away from her mouth.
On the last leaf, in an older hand copied beneath the translation, the Lark's passengers had made a plain request.
If the sea brings our family to yours, tell us their names.
There were names below it. Many pages of them. Someone had added births in a cramped margin. Someone else recorded that an elderly man had reached shore with his shelf and stool. The little detail made Daniel laugh unexpectedly, and the laughter woke his daughter.
“Did they get out?” she murmured.
“Yes.”
“All of them?”
He looked at Mera's final count.
“Everyone on the last list. They checked the rooms they could reach.”
Mia considered that with the seriousness children bring to answers adults are tempted to simplify.
“What happened to the city?”
“They let the sea in.”
Mia turned back to the folded plan. Her finger followed the big garden rectangle, then the little door that reached it.
“Did this go into that?”
Daniel read the note beside the seam. “The door joined them. The garden had its own place.”
“Like another page?”
He held the fold carefully. “That's closer.”
She found S12 and the dining tables. Daniel found the two hull stairways and the note about keeping the aisle clear. Those small instructions made the rescue easier to picture than the stars had. Somebody had measured a doorway so a grandfather could get through it. Somebody had checked a room after everyone thought it empty.
“Could they go back?”
“Not to live there.”
She pulled the blanket closer and looked at the hand beside his arm.
“Is that ours?”
“It's the same one.”
She touched the page with one careful finger.
“Read the end again.”
He turned back to the beach, to the child listening while the scarred whale taught its young where to sing. He read it slowly. Outside the window, a bus hissed along the wet road. A neighbor started a car. The world in 2026 continued doing the small necessary things that made a morning.
When Daniel reached the tree above the bay, Mia put her head against him.
He finished the final sentence.
Then he closed the book without putting it away.
There would be a ceiling to mend, someone to call about the pipe, breakfast to make. Later, he would ask his aunt which of his grandmother's papers she had kept. He would photograph the fragile pages and write down what he knew before waiting became another inheritance.
For now, he carried Mia upstairs.
On the kitchen table, the book lay beside the drying photographs. Morning light reached the old binding. The house was not sinking. His daughter was warm in his arms.
They were still here.

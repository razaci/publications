Chapter 1
The Other Plate
The stranger did not sit in the place Elian had set for him.
  He remained beside the table with his wet coat folded over one arm, watching the bread. Water dripped from the hem onto the commons floor. Behind him, a girl of perhaps seventeen carried a wooden box with both hands. Its lid had swollen and would not close.
  “We were told you had no emergency,” Elian said.
  “The vessel hasn't,” the man answered. “I don't know how else to describe the people.”
  Damas came through the hatch behind them. He had inspected the approaching ship himself. Its name was Testament. It carried seventeen passengers, a pilot with a damaged ear, and enough provisions to reach the next maintained berth. No one was being pursued. No child was concealed in a locker. Damas had asked both questions separately.
  The stranger introduced himself as Asa Vale.
  “I used to preach at West Orchard.”
  “Used to?” Anet asked.
  The girl set the box down. Inside were books, folded shirts, and a little blue shoe with a hardened white substance along its sole.
  “He still preaches,” she said. “They stopped inviting him.”
  Asa looked at her. “This is my daughter, Tavi.”
  Elian moved a bowl away from the box before the damp reached it. Through the window, Testament turned within its berth, a long vessel with the pale ribs of an older construction showing beneath patched insulation. It resembled an animal whose winter coat had grown unevenly.
  “You asked to compare records,” she said.
  Tavi lifted a book from beneath the shoe. Its pages were packed inside transparent sleeves. On the first was a diagram of three overlapping circles, a reservoir suspended over terraces, and an inscription in an unfamiliar engineering hand.
  “Those are West Orchard's suns,” she said.
  Elian knew the settlement by its receiving agreements. Mira and Lio had chosen it because its officers offered ordinary work without a medical repayment bond. The papers had survived repeated questions. That mattered. She had learned to value papers that remained useful after the person presenting them had stopped smiling.
  “What is the inscription?”
  “A distribution limit,” Tavi said. “Everyone has been calling it a promise.”
  Asa finally sat.
  “I preached about that promise for nineteen years.”
  Renn, who had been attempting to divide a loaf without admitting that the crust defeated him, put the knife down.
  “Eat first,” he said. “Nineteen years will still be there after soup.”
  Asa took the bowl. Tavi watched until he had swallowed before accepting her own.
  They ate while Testament's pilot, Oren, argued with Damas through an open channel about a replacement coupling. Their voices had the familiar warmth of people disagreeing about something they understood.
  Then a message arrived from West Orchard. Its receiving office confirmed Mira's room and Lio's school place. The same transmission announced a temporary change to food collection: households without a renewed district seal would require a sponsor.
  Elian read the second notice again.
  “How temporary?” she asked.
  Asa put down his spoon.
  “That is the sermon they still invite me to give.”
Chapter 2
The Woman at the Scale
Mara knew the weight of an empty bowl without putting it on the scale.
  Her wrists knew. Fourteen years at the Orchard exchange had trained them to distinguish stoneware from pressed fibre, a hidden spoon from a double lining. She could tell when someone had brought a larger bowl than the one on their household record. She could also tell when a child had spent the morning licking the rim.
  On the first day of the new seal, she turned away eleven people.
  She entered each refusal under Pending Verification. That phrase had been taught to her by a woman who brought homemade biscuits to training and remembered the names of everyone's children.
  At noon a man with a bent hat asked whether pending meant he could collect tomorrow.
  “It means the receiving office has to confirm your district.”
  “They're moving the office.”
  “There is a temporary desk.”
  “There was. This morning they moved that.”
  Mara glanced at the queue. The people behind him were listening. On the wall, the three white discs of the artificial suns shone through separate panels, their light overlapped by the silhouettes of hanging irrigation chains. Beneath the counter lay the medicine receipt for her son, Ben. Her next wage payment would cover the remaining sum.
  “I can't issue it without the seal.”
  The man put his bowl away carefully. She wished he had shouted.
  That evening she crossed the Glass Sea by the pedestrian bridge. Above her, an immense lens of stored water caught the light. Below it grew white pear trees trained along floating frames. Their roots hung in moving nutrient channels. Children called the place an upside-down lake.
  Ben was sitting up when she reached their room. He had folded paper into boats and arranged them along his blanket.
  “The new lady has a garden in a cup,” he told her.
  “Which new lady?”
  “The girl's mother. Next door.”
  Mira appeared in the doorway carrying a bowl of potatoes. She said she had made too many. Mara could see from her tired face that this was a courtesy rather than a calculation.
  Lio followed, holding a tray with a gold flower upon it.
  “It needs a different window,” Lio explained. “This one makes it lean.”
  Ben offered to build it a boat.
  While the children debated whether soil would sink, Mira asked where she should renew her seal.
  Mara gave her directions. She gave them twice, including the staircase the official map left out.
  “They said we wouldn't need a sponsor.”
  “You didn't. When you came.”
  Mira held the potato bowl against her stomach.
  “I've already moved once because somebody changed the meaning of a form.”
  Mara wanted to say that West Orchard was different. Instead she took the bowl and promised to walk with her to the office before work.
  Later, Ben asked whether the flower's mother was worried.
  “The flower doesn't have a mother.”
  “The girl's mother.”
  “A little.”
  He considered his paper boats.
  “Can we give her our good window?”
  Mara kissed his forehead. It was warm enough to measure.
Chapter 3
The Fourth Person
Asa preached in a packing hall because the chapel's invitation had been withdrawn after he asked to see the seal regulations.
  Someone set six fruit crates beneath a work lamp. Someone else brought chairs. Mira sat near an exit with Lio leaning against her. Mara came late and stayed at the back, still wearing the exchange apron beneath her coat.
  Nell arrived from North Terrace on an agreed maintenance visit, carrying the tool case Micah had repaired. They had argued about its weight before she left. He wanted her to take the smaller wrench. She had wanted both. They had settled the matter without turning it into a referendum on her departure.
  Now she set the case beside a crate and listened.
  Asa read from Daniel's third chapter. The words about the furnace sounded strange beneath a cooling duct that rattled every time the irrigation pumps accelerated.
  He spoke slowly at first.
  “When I was young, I told this story as though the whole point were that faithful people do not burn. Children like that ending. So did I. A fire, a miracle, everyone who matters walking out.”
  A fruit packer shifted in her chair.
  “But before the furnace, before anyone sees deliverance, these men answer the king. They believe God is able to save them. Then they say, 'But if not.' Daniel three, verse eighteen. Those words come before they know how the story ends.”
  Mara looked toward the exit. A supervisor from the exchange was standing there with his arms folded.
  “They do not offer the king somebody else's child to prove their courage. They answer for themselves. That matters when a preacher asks a room to be brave.”
  Asa laid his hands on the open Bible.
  “Some of you distribute food. Some repair pumps. Some have authority over a door that somebody else must pass through. You may be told that doing your work requires a small act against your conscience. Perhaps it will be called temporary. Perhaps the order will be explained by someone who is kind to you.”
  Mara's apron seemed suddenly heavy.
  “Read the order. Ask what it does. Ask who goes hungry while it is being reviewed. If you refuse it, do not assume that refusal buys you a miracle. Ask who will help your children if you lose your wage. And those of us who applaud your courage must answer that question before we applaud.”
  The supervisor left.
  Asa watched the doorway and lost his place. He found it again with one finger.
  “There is another person in the furnace. The king sees someone he did not put there. Christians have long found in that scene a promise of God's presence. I believe we may take comfort in it. But I cannot arrange that presence like a guard at the door. I cannot sell you certainty about tomorrow.”
  From the back, a woman asked, “Will you give up your room?”
  No one shushed her.
  Asa looked up.
  “If they turn us out for refusing the order, will you give us yours?”
  It was Mara. She had not planned to speak.
  “I have a berth on Testament,” he said.
  “And a place for a sick child?”
  He looked at Tavi. She was sitting beside the books, her jaw tight.
  “I don't know,” he answered.
  The hall became uncomfortable in a way applause would have prevented.
  Elian stood.
  “Then we find out before anyone has to choose.”
  Nobody left immediately after the blessing. The fruit crates became a table. On a clean sheet of wrapping paper, they began to list rooms, wages, medicines, and the people who would need them.
Chapter 4
Eleven Bowls
The next morning Mara refused a twelfth person.
  She had expected to wake changed by what she had heard. Instead she woke with Ben's cough, a damp towel on the chair, and six minutes to reach the exchange. Faith had not added time to the morning.
  The twelfth person was the man in the bent hat.
  His name was Pell. She learned it because she asked, and the question made what followed harder.
  “I went to your temporary desk,” he said. “They said my mother's address isn't an address.”
  “Where is it?”
  “Under the old root frames.”
  Mara looked at the space for a sponsor. Her own identification would fit there. Her supervisor had warned the staff that sponsoring an unverified household made the sponsor liable for duplicated claims.
  She thought of Ben's medicine receipt.
  “I can put in an appeal.”
  Pell took his bowl.
  After he left, she wrote his name on scrap paper and hid it beneath the scale. At the end of her shift there were four names. She had begun to collect them as if accurate remembrance were a substitute for food.
  Asa found her outside the exchange. He had waited two hours rather than interrupt her work.
  “Testament's infirmary can receive Ben for short stays,” he said. “Damas says a resident clinician must assess what else he needs. I have put my stipend into the support fund.”
  “That wasn't what I asked.”
  “No.”
  “I wanted you to stop talking as though I hadn't thought of the cost.”
  Asa stood beside the bridge rail. In the reservoir overhead, a maintenance cradle crawled along a silver seam. Its moving shadow crossed his face.
  “I did that,” he said.
  The answer disarmed her enough to make her angrier.
  “And now you've made me responsible for being the example.”
  “You aren't required to tell me what you decide.”
  She pulled out the four names.
  “I turned them away.”
  He read the paper without reaching for it.
  “Do you know where they went?”
  “One of them. Maybe.”
  They walked beneath the root frames together. Nell came because she knew which maintenance passages were safe, and Mira came because she would not send her neighbor alone through an unfamiliar district. Asa carried a pot borrowed from the packing hall.
  Pell's mother lived behind a curtain sewn from feed sacks. Her room contained a repaired stove, a brush with most of its bristles missing, and three carefully dusted photographs.
  She thanked Mara for the soup.
  That kindness nearly made Mara leave.
  “I was the one who said no,” she told her.
  “I know. Pell said.”
  The old woman put out another bowl.
  “Will you do it again tomorrow?”
  Mara sat down because she could not answer while standing above her.
Chapter 5
A Shoe Filled With Salt
Tavi found her father washing the borrowed pot in Testament's galley.
  “You didn't tell them about Mother.”
  Asa kept his hands in the water. The heating element had failed; soap clung to his knuckles in grey islands.
  “They asked about the seal.”
  “They asked whether you would pay.”
  She put the blue shoe beside the sink.
  It had belonged to a toddler in the salt wards, where a failed recovery pipe had left white mineral dust over everything. Asa's wife, Enna, had kept it after helping the child find his family. The child was alive. Enna was not. People regularly confused those facts when Asa told the story in public, because he ended it at the reunion.
  “I didn't say she came home,” he answered.
  “You let them hear it.”
  He took his hands from the sink.
  Outside, Nell and Oren were fitting a coupling between two incompatible generations of machinery. Every third exchange involved Oren asking her to face him so he could hear. Tavi envied the plainness of the problem. Turn your face. Say it again. Try the smaller wrench.
  “I don't know how to preach about her without using her,” Asa said.
  Tavi had come prepared to accuse him of knowing exactly how. The admission left her with a grief she could not push onto him.
  “You could start by saying when she asked you to come.”
  There had been three messages from the salt wards. The first described a broken intake. The second asked for volunteers. The third was addressed to Asa personally. He had been conducting a seven-day series about endurance in the main chapel. At the committee's request he had finished the series before traveling.
  Enna died during the extra two days.
  The infection would not necessarily have spared her if he had arrived sooner. Tavi knew that. The uncertainty made her loss harder to carry, not easier. She wanted him to acknowledge the choice he had made without turning it into the cause of every bad thing that followed.
  “Say that you stayed,” she said.
  “Yes.”
  “Without telling people why you were needed more somewhere else.”
  He nodded. She had expected resistance and found she had no strength left for agreement.
  He reached toward the shoe, then stopped.
  “May I put it back?”
  “Leave it there.”
  They washed the pot together. The water never became warm.
  On the quay, Oren dropped a bolt into the drain and swore with enough invention to draw a laugh out of Nell. Tavi laughed too, abruptly, and covered her mouth as though she had betrayed someone.
  Asa looked at her without asking her to explain.
  For once he let the sound be what it was.
Chapter 6
The Lake Above Their Heads
West Orchard's oldest map was not a map of a city. It was a map of a machine that people had made into a city.
  The difference lay in the little blank spaces.
  Nell spread the transparent sheets across the packing table. Tavi aligned three corners with weights made from damaged pump bearings. At the center, three thermal mirrors redirected light through the reservoir. Along its perimeter, channels collected condensed moisture. Beneath them hung the orchards, their maintenance galleries later enclosed to make rooms.
  On the newest plans those rooms were called unauthorized occupancy.
  On the oldest, they were marked future habitation.
  “How old?” Elian asked.
  “Older than the present district authority,” Tavi answered. “That doesn't establish a current right. It establishes that someone expected people.”
  Asa watched his daughter handle the sheets. He had introduced her for years as the girl who remembered all his sermons. It occurred to him that he had rarely introduced her by anything she had chosen to learn.
  The engineer who joined them was called Lea. She wore a hearing aid on a cord, which she removed whenever the pumps started to squeal.
  “The reservoir is losing pressure,” she said.
  There was no ceremony in the announcement. She showed them readings, the dates of calibration, and a repair estimate.
  “How much?” Nell asked.
  “Enough that the lowest recovery line is pulling air.”
  “Then why the household seal?”
  “I asked for a count of water use. The Authority asked for a count of permitted households.”
  Elian studied the lines. “Can you separate those numbers?”
  Lea gave a short, unpleasant laugh.
  “I've been trying.”
  The station director, Veris, arrived with two assistants and a folding chair. He set the chair down before asking whether he could join them. Tavi moved a bearing away from its leg.
  He was younger than Elian expected, with a tremor in one hand and a tired man's precise courtesy.
  “We have thirty-one days of reserve if every collection figure is accurate,” he said. “Twenty-three if they aren't. I need order.”
  “You need figures,” Lea said.
  “I need people to obey the figures.”
  He offered Nell access to the maintenance records. He offered Elian a seat at the supply council. He asked Asa to preach at the chapel again.
  “People trust you,” he said. “Help them understand the necessary sacrifice.”
  Asa looked at the map where future habitation had become unauthorized occupancy.
  “Who chooses the people who sacrifice?”
  Veris flexed his shaking hand.
  “If the reservoir fails, no one gets to choose.”
  That was true. It was also not an answer.
Chapter 7
What Mara Signed
Mara entered her own number beneath Pell's mother's name.
  She did it at the exchange, in full view of the supervisor, after reading the liability notice twice. She had checked that the household had received no second allocation. She attached the failed address appeal and the old woman's statement.
  The supervisor asked her to step away from the scale.
  “You've made yourself responsible for an unverified claim.”
  “I've verified it.”
  “You aren't authorized to verify it.”
  He sounded almost sorrowful. He had arranged extra leave when her husband died. She remembered him standing in the corridor with a casserole while she tried to decide whether the person at the door would make her cry.
  Now he took her access card.
  The exchange continued around them. Bowls passed from hand to hand. A child complained about the weight of a cabbage. Nothing marked the precise moment when Mara's income stopped.
  On the bridge she nearly went back.
  At home Ben had built a paper boat large enough for the flower cup. Lio was explaining that roots needed drainage. Mira had written down the medicine schedule as Mara had asked, and the support fund had delivered a receipt naming what it could cover for four weeks.
  Four weeks was a number with an edge.
  “Did you get fired?” Ben asked.
  “Suspended.”
  “Is that different?”
  “We have time to ask them to change it.”
  He slid the boat beneath his blanket.
  “Was it because of me?”
  She sat on the bed immediately.
  “No.”
  “People keep asking what I need.”
  “That doesn't mean you caused the trouble.”
  He rubbed the fold in the blanket between two fingers. His father had done that when calculating a difficult bill.
  After he slept, Mara went to the packing hall. Asa was preparing his chapel sermon. He stood when she entered, and she disliked him for being afraid of what she would say.
  “Don't tell my story tomorrow.”
  “I won't.”
  “Not even without my name.”
  He crossed out a paragraph. She could see that he had already written it.
  “Thank you,” she said, because anger did not cancel the usefulness of an answer.
  They looked at the empty place on his page.
  “What will you preach?”
  “About a king who couldn't remember his dream.”
  “Will you tell them what mine was?”
  Asa waited.
  “A wage,” she said. “Medicine. An ordinary week.”
  He set the pen down.
  “I think I should remember that.”
Chapter 8
The Feet of the Statue
The chapel stood inside a retired reflector. Its walls curved inward toward a lens that had once concentrated enough light to burn through metal. Now the lens was frosted, and plants in shallow troughs grew along the aisle.
  Veris sat in the first row. His assistants had arranged printed copies of the district pledge beside each seat. To receive priority supplies, an institution would affirm the Authority as the sole guarantor of the settlement's future.
  The words were not a command to worship. Asa said that before anyone could accuse him of hiding the distinction.
  “We should read what is here, not turn every official sentence into a verse it does not claim to be.”
  He held up the pledge.
  “But we should also ask what we are being asked to promise.”
  He turned to Daniel's second chapter and told the dream: the enormous image, its changing metals, its feet partly iron and partly clay, the stone that brought the image down.
  “Daniel interprets the dream to a ruler who thinks his rule is the center of the world. Kingdom succeeds kingdom. Strength is real. So is its limit. In the dream, the final kingdom is God's. The king does not build it by collecting enough signatures.”
  A cough carried from the first row.
  “Christians disagree about some details of these visions. I will tell you where I am uncertain. I will not attach our director's name to a beast or our reservoir's maintenance date to the end of history. Scripture deserves more care than that. So do our neighbors.”
  Nell, near the back, watched Veris lower his eyes to the paper.
  “But I can say this. No institution in this room can truthfully promise that it alone guarantees our future. A government can keep a pipe repaired. It can make a fair rule. It can fail. It needs people who can tell it when it fails.”
  Asa placed the pledge beside the Bible.
  “When we ask a person to place absolute trust in a thing we made, we ask for more than the thing can bear. The iron does not become less brittle because we sing to it.”
  He had intended that sentence for the director. Hearing it aloud, he thought of the chapel committee and his wife's unanswered message.
  “That includes a church,” he added.
  The room changed. One of the elders turned toward another. Tavi stopped tracing a crack in the bench with her thumbnail.
  “That includes this pulpit. You are allowed to ask whether my words are true. You are allowed to ask where I was when I told you to endure.”
  He finished without asking for raised hands.
  Afterward, three institutions declined the pledge. Seven signed with reservations. The ward signed because its administrator believed an uninterrupted medicine supply mattered more than a sentence she did not mean literally.
  Veris waited until the last person had left.
  “I offered you a place to help.”
  “You offered me a place to persuade.”
  “Tomorrow I have to distribute less food than people need.”
  Asa looked at the empty benches.
  “Then let us see the figures tomorrow.”
  Veris gathered the unsigned sheets. Asa stayed to help the caretaker sweep dirt back into the planting troughs.
Chapter 9
A Measure of Sunlight
Nell asked for the raw readings before the diagrams.
  Lea gave her a chair with one short leg and a directory containing six years of maintenance history. The chair was a kindness. The directory was an act that could cost Lea her post.
  “I have authorization,” Nell said.
  “For the records. Not for what you'll notice.”
  The deficit was real. One thermal mirror had lost efficiency. Mineral deposits had narrowed a recovery channel. The reservoir seal leaked at a rate too small to see from the gallery and too large to ignore across a month.
  None of those failures explained why the lowest wards were using three times their expected allocation.
  Nell worked until her eyes began to confuse columns. Then she closed the display and called Micah at the time they had agreed.
  He answered from beneath the cabinet she had helped him repair.
  “It's still the hinge?”
  “No. I dropped a screw and Renn says crawling is character building.”
  She told him about the water figures. She did not ask him to solve them. At the end she said she wanted two more days at Orchard, and he asked whether she was requesting two days or warning him that she would stay until the problem disappeared.
  She resented the question for a moment. Then she answered it.
  “Two days. After that we discuss it again.”
  Lea returned with bread that tasted faintly of onion. They ate beside the directory.
  “Read the meter labels,” Nell said.
  “I've read them.”
  “Read them without the district names.”
  Lea leaned over her shoulder.
  Each lower-ward meter counted drinking water, irrigation return, and emergency cooling through the same junction. The public allocation table treated all three as household consumption.
  “They aren't stealing water,” Lea said.
  “The figures don't establish that anyone is. They establish that we are measuring three things together and charging one group.”
  There was a silence that belonged to Vey. Nell remembered Low Bell's stolen morning, the branch of a light diagram she had failed to check, the young trees that had survived at the cost of a growing season. She had been praised before she understood what she had done.
  “We test it,” she said.
  They isolated an unoccupied service loop under supervision and logged the change. Household use remained steady. The disputed meter rose when the cooling valve opened.
  Lea sat down on the bad chair.
  “I sent three notices about that meter last year.”
  “To whom?”
  She brought up the correspondence.
  The first reply asked for more evidence. The second referred the matter elsewhere. The third said a correction would undermine confidence in the emergency collection policy.
  Nell copied all three with their dates intact.
  Outside the office, the afternoon sun shifted. A reflection from the reservoir passed over the wall like a door opening onto water.
  For the first time since arriving, Nell found herself afraid of good news.
Chapter 10
The Chair in the Ward
Asa's stipend arrived in the support fund. His housing supplement did not.
  The chapel committee explained that the supplement was tied to approved ministry. One elder offered to describe his departure as a voluntary retreat. That would preserve his reputation and spare the institution embarrassment.
  “What would it do for Mara?” Asa asked.
  “This isn't about Mara.”
  He looked at the elder until the man looked away.
  At the ward, he found Mara washing Ben's cup. The boy had been brought for assessment after a night of fever. The clinician had explained that he needed treatment and observation; the adults had agreed to stop discussing accounts beside his bed.
  Asa pulled up a chair.
  “The fund can cover another three weeks,” he told Mara in the corridor. “After that, we need more pledges.”
  “Will you still be here?”
  “Yes.”
  “On which chair?”
  He almost smiled. Then he understood she was asking whether his support depended on remaining important.
  “Any they let me use.”
  He stayed while Mara went to her suspension hearing. He read Ben a story about a ship that went so slowly its captain learned gardening. Ben objected to the navigation. Asa accepted the corrections.
  Across the aisle lay a man who had signed the district pledge and now feared that the preacher would refuse to pray with him.
  “I signed for the ward,” the man said. “They said if enough households signed, the supply schedule would settle.”
  Asa put down the book.
  “You can tell me why without asking me for permission to be frightened.”
  They prayed quietly. There were no promises about the next test result.
  At dusk Tavi brought food. She had been transcribing the old map and had ink on the side of her face. She saw the chair, the child's corrected ship drawings, her father's coat folded on the floor.
  “The committee sent somebody to our berth,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “They wanted the hymn books.”
  He closed his eyes.
  “I let them take the ones that belong to the chapel. I kept Mother's.”
  “Good.”
  She sat beside him and opened a parcel of flatbread. For a while they ate without speaking. Ben slept. An orderly changed the water jug.
  “You missed mine too,” Tavi said.
  “What?”
  “The day my leg healed enough to walk the quay. You had a committee meeting.”
  He remembered the meeting. That hurt more than if he had forgotten it.
  “I did.”
  “I don't want a sermon about it.”
  “You won't get one.”
  She passed him the larger piece of bread, noticed what she had done, and took half of it back.
  He did not mistake that for forgiveness.
Chapter 11
The Tree Above the Houses
The memorial garden was suspended beneath the second sun.
  Its trees grew sideways in frames, branches trained along the gallery so that people could stand among leaves without putting weight on the root beds. Small metal tags carried the names of people whose ashes had been committed to the recovery system. No one pretended a tag meant a particular leaf belonged to a particular person. Families still touched them.
  Asa had asked to speak there about Daniel's fourth chapter. The gardeners agreed on condition that he leave the work paths clear.
  He began beside a pear tree whose upper branch sheltered three nesting maintenance birds. Their ancestors had been brought to control insects. Their descendants had developed an appetite for wire labels.
  “Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a tree,” he said. “Its height is enormous. Animals shelter beneath it. Birds live in it. There is food for living creatures. The image includes a real good. We should notice that before we congratulate ourselves for seeing the king's pride.”
  Veris stood at the rear, having come without assistants.
  “A shelter can be useful and still be ruled unjustly. A ruler can build something people need and begin to believe their need proves his innocence.”
  Asa read the counsel from Daniel four, verse twenty-seven, about righteousness and mercy to the poor. He did not linger on the strange years of the king's humiliation. He lingered on the warning given before them, while a different choice was still possible.
  “Repentance is not waiting for disaster so that everyone can agree you suffered enough. Daniel tells the king to change how he treats people.”
  An older woman began to cry. Her companion took her hand. Asa paused without making their grief part of his illustration.
  “My wife was called Enna,” he said when the woman had caught her breath. “Some of you remember her from the salt wards. When she asked for help, I stayed here to finish preaching about endurance. I believed the series needed me. She died before I arrived.”
  Tavi stood by the rail. She had heard him rehearse these words and had told him which parts he was permitted to say.
  “I cannot tell you that my arriving sooner would have saved her. I can tell you that I chose to remain. I have sometimes told her story in a way that let people admire her without asking what I did.”
  His voice failed. He waited. No one filled the pause with an amen.
  “If my ministry has sheltered anyone, I am grateful. That does not make every choice I made good. If our district has fed people, let us maintain it. That does not make every hungry person outside its doors a threat.”
  A bird tore a strip from a label and carried it overhead. Someone gave a startled laugh, then apologized.
  “No,” Asa said. “Let it be a bird.”
  After the gathering, Veris asked to speak privately. He did not offer the chapel again.
  “I have a mother in the upper care ward,” he said. “If you publish the deficit without a plan, people will empty the stores. She cannot queue.”
  “Then help us make a plan before we publish it.”
  “Would you wait?”
  Asa looked toward Elian, who had been listening.
  “For a defined time,” she said. “With the test results preserved. And no more refusals of essential food while the meters are checked.”
  They agreed to meet at first light. It was less satisfying than a public conversion. It was also something Veris could be held to.
Chapter 12
The Price of Being Right
At first light, the stores contained less grain than the inventory showed.
  This was not theft. A batch had spoiled after a ventilation failure. A warehouse manager had listed it as awaiting review rather than unusable because writing it off required three signatures, one from an officer who had died.
  “Every shelf has a story,” Elian said.
  “We need a total,” Veris answered.
  “Then the stories have to reach the total.”
  They worked in separate teams. Nell and Lea checked meters. Mara, permitted to attend as a witness, compared household claims. Damas inspected transfer capacity at the berth. Oren listened to pumps with his good ear and refused to pronounce on a bearing until someone stopped talking beside him.
  Tavi found the most difficult figure in a folder labeled Historical Promises.
  The three circles on the old map described a maximum safe distribution envelope. They did not promise equal output forever.   He looked at the ordinary faces around him: a warehouse clerk, a man who had reported his neighbor, the daughter who no longer trusted his beautiful endings, the director who had come without a reserved seat.

  Mara listened with her hands in her coat pockets. She was no longer certain what she wanted the sermon to make her feel. At first she had wanted courage. Then she had wanted someone to be ashamed. Today she wanted the meeting to finish in time for the ward shift.
  “The court in the vision opens books,” Asa said. “It judges power. It does not announce that the loudest claim to holiness makes a ruler innocent. If you find yourself excusing cruelty because the person who orders it uses the right religious words, stop. Read again. Listen to the person who bears the injury.”
  Someone asked whether the reservoir failure meant the final crisis was near.
  “I do not know a timetable that Scripture has not given me. I will not construct one from our maintenance log. I believe the promised judgment and hope are real. I also believe Sel deserves a truthful injury report today.”
  He closed the Bible, then opened it again.
  “One more thing. I have made it sound as though listening always produces agreement. It doesn't. After this service, Veris and Elian will still disagree about the review date. Lea and the growers still need to settle tomorrow's load. We can tell the truth about that without declaring one another outside God's care.”
  The service ended with prayer, then with a work assignment. Some stayed. Some left. One woman asked to borrow Daniel because she had never read the surrounding chapters herself.
  Sella found her a copy with margins large enough for questions.
Chapter 25
Under the Drained Sky
From below, the emptied section of the Glass Sea looked like a sky from which someone had removed the weather.
  The membrane lay in pale folds above the safety deck. Behind it, the broken brace showed its little wound. Nell had expected something more impressive. A failure capable of threatening several districts occupied less space than her palm.
  Lea photographed the fitting before anyone removed it. An independent inspector read the part markings aloud. The replacement had been specified for a different temperature cycle. Its installation had passed a visual inspection because its outline matched the old component.
  “Who signed it?” Veris asked over the channel.
  “We will preserve the record,” the inspector said. “Right now we're checking the neighboring supports.”
  The neighboring supports included two more of the same part.
  Oren sat back on his heels. He had brought enough replacements for one brace. The supply catalogue listed compatible stock at a berth six days away.
  “We keep the section isolated,” Lea said.
  That meant reducing irrigation and moving another set of residents. It meant missed wages, crowded rooms, and a longer period with the ward on the temporary loop. Nell imagined the anger before it reached them.
  She called Elian.
  “Tell me the conditions,” Elian said.
  Nell did. The decision went to the emergency council with the engineering report attached. Growers disputed the irrigation estimate. Ward staff asked whether the loop could remain in use. Residents asked whether another move would forfeit their return places.
  The meeting took an hour. No one beneath the reservoir touched the release wheel while it continued.
  They chose isolation, ordered the parts, and authorized interim wages for the second evacuation zone. They still had to find the money. The cooperative accepted staggered repayment after seeing the inspection images and confirming that an alternative berth could handle its delayed cargo.
  By evening the work team was dismantling the first brace. One seized fastener resisted. A younger technician proposed heating it in place.
  Nell checked the membrane clearance and said no. They cut the fastener under a shield instead. It took longer. Every safe decision seemed to add time to a world that had run short of it.
  Then Oren lost his grip on the cut piece.
  It fell into the catch net. Nobody was beneath it because the exclusion area had been kept clear, though three people had asked to use that passage as a shortcut.
  He stared at the net.
  “I should've held it.”
  “That's why the net is there,” Nell said.
  When their shift ended, they sat outside the machinery room. Asa brought food in lidded containers and asked whether they wanted company or quiet.
  Oren chose quiet. Nell asked him to stay.
  Asa sat between them without trying to reconcile the answers. They ate. Far below, someone in the temporary hall was teaching children a song with too many verses.
  Nell had never been so grateful for a sound that had nothing to do with pumps.
Chapter 26
The Smallest Congregation
On the sixth night of isolation, Mara found the preacher asleep in the laundry room.
  He sat on a folded sheet, head against a cabinet, with the ward's delivery list on his lap. His shoes were still on. One hand was clenched around a pencil.
  She shook his shoulder.
  “Go to your berth.”
  He woke abruptly.
  “I was finishing the blankets.”
  “They're finished.”
  “The lower hall needs—”
  “Somebody else can do the next trip.”
  He looked at the list as though it might defend him.
  “I said I'd stay.”
  “You said you'd help. Being found on the floor isn't part of our arrangement.”
  He obeyed, resentfully enough that she knew he had understood her. Tavi was waiting at the gangway. She took the list, checked that the next volunteer had received it, and walked him to his cabin without telling him she was proud.
  At the ward Ben asked why the preacher had gone away.
  “To sleep.”
  “Isn't he supposed to look after people?”
  “Sleeping will help him do that.”
  Ben considered this, then lay down with the air of someone making a valuable contribution.
  At the far end of the room Iona had become less responsive. The clinician explained the changes to Tavi and to the niece who had arrived from another district. Asa came the next morning, after sleeping, and found them sitting together with the book press wrapped beneath Iona's bed.
  Her niece asked for a prayer.
  Asa asked whether she wanted him to read first. She shook her head.
  He prayed for relief, for the staff attending her, for the frightened people in the chairs. He asked for healing. He did not phrase the request as a test of anyone's belief.
  Afterward the niece asked whether she should have prayed harder before the fall.
  “No,” he said.
  “You don't know how little I visited.”
  “You can grieve that without making her illness a punishment for it.”
  Tavi stared at him. He could feel what she was hearing alongside the words.
  When the niece went to wash, his daughter spoke.
  “Do you believe that about yourself?”
  He looked at Iona's quiet face.
  “I believe I made a choice I regret. I don't believe I controlled whether your mother lived.”
  “Some days I want you to have controlled it.”
  “I know.”
  “Then I could finish being angry.”
  He wanted to tell her anger could be put down. He had told rooms full of people that before. Instead he stayed beside her while the machines made their ordinary sounds.
  The niece returned carrying three cups. Asa moved his chair to make room. It was the smallest congregation he had sat with that week and the one in which he found speech most difficult.
Chapter 27
The Books Were Opened
The public review reopened because Veris could no longer postpone it without breaching the emergency agreement he had signed.
  Elian did not describe that as a triumph. The hall was crowded with tired people whose houses still stood beneath an isolated reservoir. They wanted to go home more than they wanted a hearing.
  The chair was an external adjudicator from the corridor cooperative. Sel's partner had objected to a purely local panel. Veris had objected to the cooperative because it held claims against the district. They settled on a chair with disclosed connections, two resident assessors, published records, and a route of appeal.
  It took nearly as long to agree on the hearing as to prepare its evidence.
  Mara testified first. She described the scale, the sponsorship, the names she had written on scrap paper. She did not call herself fearless. When asked why she had refused Pell on the second day, she said she was afraid of losing Ben's treatment.
  “Did anyone threaten to stop his treatment?”
  “No. I feared losing the money to pay for things we needed.”
  The distinction went into the record.
  Pell corrected his statement in person. His mother remained in the temporary hall because long meetings exhausted her. He read a sentence she had dictated: her room existed before the new seal and would exist after the committee finished discussing its existence.
  One assessor almost smiled. The clerk recorded it without alteration.
  Lea presented the meter tests. Nell explained the false dawn and the limits of the model they had used. The inspector identified the incompatible brace fittings. Sel's injury report was accepted under protected conditions. His partner read the questions his current condition prevented him from asking himself.
  Veris admitted authorizing the use of household restrictions before the consumption figures had been separated. He said the reserve estimate frightened him. He did not admit intending to punish religious dissent. The panel noted that intent and effect required separate findings.
  Asa was asked whether his sermons had encouraged people to ignore safety rules.
  “I told them not to surrender conscience,” he said. “I also told them to read the actual order. If anyone believes I asked them to enter an exclusion zone or refuse a sound evacuation, I want to hear the words they relied on.”
  A resident produced the furnace recording. The chair played the passage about not offering someone else's child as proof of courage. They played the later question about rooms and wages too.
  “You hadn't made those arrangements when you preached,” the chair observed.
  “No.”
  “Was that responsible?”
  Asa looked toward Mara. She did not rescue him.
  “Not sufficiently,” he answered.
  At the end of the day, the panel ordered interim allocations by verified residence rather than the disputed seal. It suspended the institutional pledge as a condition of essential supply. It reserved final findings on liability until the repair and injury investigations were complete.
  Mara's suspension was lifted. Back pay remained subject to calculation. Veris would remain available for technical handover but lose sole control of distribution pending the final decision.
  The crowd wanted a more complete ending. Elian read the orders twice and asked who had been assigned to carry them out.
  That question kept them in the hall for another hour. It was the most useful hour of the day.
Chapter 28
The Prayer With No Enemy In It
Asa's next sermon took place after the hearing, when everyone was most ready to hear that their own side had been proved righteous.
  He chose Daniel's ninth chapter.
  Mara nearly left when he announced it. She had endured enough public discussion of what she had done wrong. Then she saw that he had brought no notes about her.
  “Daniel prays as part of a people,” Asa said. “He does not begin by arranging everyone else's sins at a safe distance from himself. He confesses. He asks for mercy.”
  He read a short passage, then told them what he was not qualified to settle in a single address: the long history of interpretations surrounding the chapter's prophetic periods. Those questions deserved study, and Sella had arranged a reading group where different views would be identified rather than blurred together.
  “Today I am asking about this prayer. What happens if we say 'we' only when there is praise to share?”
  Pell sat in the third row. His mother had insisted he wear the hat because the hall was cold.
  “Some of us enforced a rule before asking whom it harmed. Some repeated a rumor because it accused a person we already disliked. Some of us let admiration conceal where we had failed. Some knew a meter was wrong and found a way to make that knowledge somebody else's unfinished task.”
  Lea folded her arms. She had sent three notices. She did not want her persistence flattened into the same category as the official who had ignored them.
  Asa saw her face.
  “Those are different responsibilities,” he said. “Confession should not make them equal. A clerk and a director do not possess the same power. Neither should be asked to confess what the other did. We need enough honesty to name our own part accurately.”
  He opened his notebook.
  “I owe a correction to the people who heard me quote the promise of three lights that would never fail. The sentence came from a devotional caption. It was not Scripture. I gave it more weight than I had checked. Tavi found the original engineering context.”
  He told them where copies of the correction could be obtained.
  “I also owe money to the support fund. The amount is listed. The committee's housing supplement ended; I cannot pay it at once. I have proposed a schedule and asked someone else to audit it.”
  There was an uncomfortable movement near the door. Several people had offered him personal gifts after the hearing. He had not yet decided what belonged to his living costs and what belonged to the work he represented. Now he would have to decide in public.
  “A prayer is not a receipt,” he said. “If we owe something, the prayer should not be where the accounting ends.”
  After the service, Lea approached him.
  “I didn't leave it unfinished,” she said.
  He listened as she described each escalation. Then he amended the printed sermon so its description of the records did not imply equal negligence.
  “You could have told me privately,” he said before he caught himself.
  “You said it publicly.”
  He nodded and carried the correction to the noticeboard himself.
Chapter 29
What the Sleeper Was Owed
Iona died before the final replacement parts arrived.
  Tavi was with her niece in the ward. Asa was in the laundry room sorting blankets. There was no significance in the arrangement. The niece found him afterward and said the last breaths had been quiet.
  He put the blanket in his hands on the clean pile. Then he took it off again because he could no longer remember whether it had been washed.
  Tavi came into the doorway.
  “I don't want you to tell me she's one more seed,” she said.
  He had said that at funerals. He believed the scriptural image of seed and resurrection had value. He also heard what she was refusing: an image used so quickly that it left no room for the woman who had complained about gutters and saved damaged letters.
  “I won't.”
  “I want her to want her tea. I want her to ask why I bent the page.”
  He sat on the bench. She sat beside him.
  At the memorial, Iona's niece read memories supplied by the people who knew her. One was unflattering. They kept it because it was affectionate and true. The gardener described how she had once refused to return a borrowed hoe until its owner apologized to a child. Pell's mother remembered a quarrel about preserving a ledger that should, in her opinion, have been burned.
  Then Asa read from Daniel twelve, verse two, about those who sleep in the dust awakening. He read the whole verse, including its judgment, rather than selecting only the words that would make his voice gentler.
  “This promise reaches beyond our repair,” he said. “We cannot restore Iona by finishing the reservoir. We cannot turn her voice recordings into her consent to another conversation. Her letters deserve care because she wrote them. They are not a machine that gives her back.”
  Tavi held the packet in both hands.
  “I believe God remembers the person we cannot keep alive. I believe resurrection is more than a name staying legible on a tag. That is a hope I receive from Scripture. I cannot demonstrate it by arranging a light over this room.”
  The second sun had been screened for maintenance. The hall was dim.
  “If you cannot feel comfort today, nobody here should accuse you of loving badly or believing too little. We can sit beside you. We can tell you when the next meal is ready. We can help with the room that will be difficult to enter.”
  He stopped. The rest of what he had prepared belonged to a service where the speaker needed to finish beautifully. This family needed to go home.
  Afterward Tavi asked whether she could keep Iona's unfinished letter to Enna.
  The niece said yes. She asked for copies of the other letters and offered to help identify their recipients. They would not publish the packet without checking what Iona had allowed.
  That evening Asa found Tavi at the quay. She was looking at the strip of reflected light beneath Testament.
  “I still believe,” she said. “I just don't know what to do with the part that hurts.”
  “Neither do I, some days.”
  She let him stand beside her. Neither called that an answer. They remained until the quay lights changed and it was time to eat.
Chapter 30
The Water Came Back Slowly
When the last brace was secured, nobody opened the whole reservoir at once.
  The refill proceeded through marked stages. At each hold, the team compared pressure, movement, and downstream readings. The new shutter test included the angle that had injured Sel. The ward kept its temporary loop until its own engineer confirmed the permanent return.
  People who had followed the false-dawn recording complained that this ending was taking too long to watch.
  Nell muted the public commentary.
  At the second hold, a small seal leaked. The team drained that section, replaced it, and began again. No one called it a spiritual trial. It was a leak. It consumed a day, several gaskets, and the last reserve of Oren's patience.
  He apologized to a technician he had shouted at. Then he went to sleep.
  At the final hold, Lea invited a gardener and a lower-ward resident to read the public display with her. They checked the figures against the independent instruments and signed that they had witnessed the test, not that they guaranteed every future morning.
  The water came back slowly.
  From the lower galleries the membrane became a lake again, holding three softened suns. Shadows of the root frames stretched across the new return walkway. A white pear blossom, late and out of season, opened in a sheltered bed that had not been scorched.
  Mara took Ben to see it after his discharge. He could walk to the first bench and wanted to go farther. She negotiated the second bench with the clinician's instructions still folded in her pocket.
  Pell's mother came home two days later. Elian brought the storage card and checked the room against the photographs with her. A pot was missing. A cupboard door had warped. The relocation officer began explaining how much worse it could have been.
  Elian handed him the loss form.
  “We can record what happened without comparing it to a disaster.”
  The pot was found in a neighboring household's crate. The cupboard needed a carpenter. That was the scale of the work now, and it felt almost impossible after so much danger.
  At the berth, Nell inspected Testament's returned coupling. The passengers received the revised tickets and travel allowance promised before they voted. One refused to pose for the district photograph.
  “We did a useful thing,” she said. “I don't owe you a face for the poster.”
  Damas, who had arrived to collect the loaned equipment, said that seemed fair.
  Nell called Micah. She asked whether she could come home on the next scheduled passage.
  He told her the shelf had finally stopped sticking.
  “Then you'll have nothing for me to do,” she said.
  “You could eat supper.”
  She looked out at the lake above the houses.
  “I think I could.”
Chapter 31
Back Pay
Mara's first restored wage arrived before her back pay.
  The difference mattered to the clerk processing her claim, who wanted to close the file, and to Mara, who would not sign that the whole debt had been settled. She took her new access card and left the disputed line open.
  At the exchange she found a second scale beside the first. One measured the bowl. The other stood ready for independent checks. The new notice separated household food from sponsorship appeals and gave the location of an office that was required to remain in place for the full stated hours.
  Her old supervisor had been reassigned during the inquiry. He came to collect his papers while she was cleaning the counter.
  “I was trying to keep the exchange running,” he said.
  “I know.”
  “People think I wanted them hungry.”
  Mara set down the cloth.
  “I don't know what everybody thinks. I know what you told me to do.”
  He stood for a moment with the box against his chest.
  “I'm sorry.”
  She nodded. She could accept that he had said it without deciding how much it repaired.
  The final panel report arrived that week. It upheld the meter correction and ordered disputed charges recalculated. It found that the pledge condition had improperly linked essential supply to an institutional affirmation. It did not label the settlement a fulfilled prophecy or declare the preacher vindicated on every point.
  Responsibility for the incompatible braces remained divided among specification, procurement, and inspection failures. Compensation for Sel would be determined through a separate process with interim support continued. Veris would not resume sole control of distribution. He accepted a technical transition role and appealed one finding concerning the duration of the assembly restriction.
  Elian read the report to the temporary council. Then she asked which orders had deadlines and who would report missed ones.
  Asa listened from a seat near the back. A new invitation lay in his pocket. The chapel offered him a limited teaching role under a committee that included residents from the lower wards. He had asked for public correction procedures, financial accounts separate from pastoral authority, and a schedule that allowed other people to speak.
  He had also asked Tavi whether she wanted to remain on Testament or take a room ashore.
  She chose a six-month apprenticeship with Lea.
  “I'm not staying to look after you,” she said.
  “I didn't think you were.”
  She raised an eyebrow.
  “I was trying not to,” he admitted.

  Before Mercy left, Anet received a letter from Iven. His hearing had been delayed again. He described the evidence he was being asked to explain and a patient whose placement had finally been traced. He asked no question at the end.
  Anet answered two paragraphs. She told him about the corrected scales and Iona's memorial. She did not promise to visit.
  Asa saw her sealing the letter and looked away before curiosity could become a pastoral claim.
  At supper Mara brought the disputed back-pay receipt. Elian offered to help draft the next request. Ben put Mother Goes To Work Again in the middle of the table, its paper bow repaired with fresh tape.
  For once the name fit the voyage.
Chapter 32
The Window They Chose

The window they chose
Three months later, Lio collected the calendula seeds in a folded envelope.
  The flower had faded without becoming a tragedy. Mira showed her how to recognize the dry seeds and leave the ones that were not ready. They labeled the envelope with the date and the plant's name.
  “Can we give some to Tavi?” Lio asked.
  “Ask her if she has a place for them.”
  Tavi had a place. Her room above the workshop possessed a narrow window with a view of the reservoir's underside and a pipe that hissed every afternoon. She had measured the light and borrowed a shallow tray from the gardeners. The first seeds she planted failed. The second group came up after someone advised her to stop watering them every time she felt worried.
  Asa visited on the day the second leaf opened.
  He had come to ask permission to read part of Enna's letter at a study meeting. Tavi read the passage he had marked. It described the boy listening for his father's supper bell.
  “No,” she said.
  He folded the sheet.
  “All right.”
  She watched him for a moment.
  “You can tell them Mother worked there. You can tell them you stayed. That part is yours. This part I want to keep.”
  “All right,” he said again.
  They had tea. She told him about a pump that resisted every sensible adjustment. He told her Ben had begun correcting the navigation in other people's stories. Neither mentioned forgiveness, though both had started making plans that assumed another visit.
  At the chapel that evening, the windows were open because the ventilation team had repaired them. No one claimed the breeze as proof of righteousness. It smelled of wet roots and somebody burning supper in the lower gallery.
  Asa read a short passage from Daniel and left time for questions. A grower disagreed with his interpretation. Sella asked them to distinguish what the text said from the connections they were making. The discussion lasted past the scheduled hour, and Mara stood to leave.
  “Early shift,” she said when someone asked.
  Asa thanked her for coming. He did not imply she was abandoning the important part.
  Outside, Pell was waiting with his mother. Her repaired cupboard had begun sticking again, and Mara had promised to ask Micah for advice on the next message run. They crossed the bridge together. Above them the Glass Sea held steady within its marked limits. Below, the white trees were setting fruit.
  At North Terrace, Nell read the message beside the cabinet. Micah laughed at the description of the hinge and drafted a reply with a little drawing. Elian was in the commons sorting travel papers for a later journey. She had not accepted every invitation the old records had produced. Some questions could wait while the people who had survived them learned how to live at home.
  Anet had received another letter. Renn had finally obtained storage boxes the goat could not open. The goat was working on a different theory.
  At Orchard, Mara returned to Ben. He had set two bowls on the table and put a paper boat beside hers.
  “Did the preacher finish?” he asked.
  She hung up her coat.
  “The meeting did.”
  “Was it good?”
  She considered the question while serving supper. She thought of the rule she had refused, the people she had first turned away, the days when she had wanted the preacher to disappear. She thought of him washing cups, correcting a printed page, asking before using her name. Tomorrow there would be another queue. The back pay had arrived that morning. She would buy the better shoes for Ben before setting anything aside.
  “He said something we'll have to think about,” she answered.
  Ben picked up his spoon.
  “Tomorrow?”
  “Probably longer.”
  He accepted that and told her about school.

  She opened the door.

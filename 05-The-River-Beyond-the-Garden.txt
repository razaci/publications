Chapter 1
The Bowl at the Threshold
The morning the spring failed, Tirzah was teaching her daughter how to admit a mistake.
  Sela had cut the linen too short. She stood beside the open healer's box with the ruined strip in her fingers, looking as though the scissors had betrayed an agreement between them.
  “I measured it,” she said.
  “Then measure what is left.”
  “I mean I measured before.”
  Tirzah reached for the bowl by the door. “I know what you mean.”
  There should have been water in it. Japheth filled it every morning before taking his tools outside, and for years Tirzah had washed her hands in that ordinary gift without having to ask. Now its bottom held a little pale grit.
  She carried it to the spring.
  The channel was scarcely wider than her palm. In the early years it had sung beneath the house at night; lately its voice had become thin enough to lose among the insects. Japheth stood where the water emerged from the hillside, his sleeves rolled above his elbows. He had removed the screen that kept leaves out of their drinking water. Beneath it a thread of moisture crept over stone.
  “A blockage?” she asked.
  “Not one I can reach.”
  He did not look at her. She knew that manner: when he was frightened, he tried to become a man made entirely of useful hands.
  “Have you eaten?”
  “Soon.”
  “That is a place you never seem to arrive.”
  His smile was brief. He straightened carefully, favoring the knee he still pretended she had not noticed. Behind them their daughter came through the garden, carrying the short strip of linen as though the interruption had released her from confessing what she had done.
  Sela was sixteen that summer. She had Tirzah's watchful eyes and Japheth's tendency to believe a difficult thing might yield if approached from the right angle. She had never seen a city. The great vessel on the mountain was already a story told by adults whose faces changed when rain became loud.
  “We can use the river,” she said.
  Tirzah looked downhill. Beyond the orchard and the patchwork fields, the river moved between banks of tawny grass. It supplied the lower households already. Walking there and back would take time; carrying enough for the garden would take more strength than Japheth had been admitting he possessed.
  “We can,” Tirzah said. “Fetch the covered jars.”
  Sela hesitated. “Both?”
  “All of them.”
  That instruction frightened her more than a speech would have.
  While she went, Tirzah touched the trunk of the tree grown from her mother's second seed. Its bark had roughened. The shade beneath it had become so familiar that sometimes she forgot what it had cost to bring a beginning here.
  Japheth watched her.
  “I cannot carry enough uphill for everything,” he said.
  The admission sounded painfully unlike him. Tirzah turned from the tree and took his wet, empty hands.
  “Then we will decide together what we can keep.”
  His fingers tightened around hers.
  Downstream a horn sounded from Ham's settlement: three notes, separated by long silences. A summons to the family, used for fire, injury, and things too urgent to entrust to children running between houses.
  Sela reappeared with the jars knocking against her knees.
  For a moment they all stood beneath the tree, listening.
  Then Tirzah shut the healer's box, and Japheth left his tools where they were.
  

Chapter 2
The Empty Measure
Ham had built his settlement where the valley opened into flat land. He liked the sight of a field extending farther than one man could hoe before sunset. Neria liked knowing that people lived close enough to hear her call.
  Neither had expected to need a horn for a quarrel over a jar of water.
  By the time Tirzah arrived, two families stood beside the common cistern. One woman held a broken handle. A boy had a cut above his eye. Milcah was already kneeling beside him, holding folded cloth against the wound.
  “A slip?” Tirzah asked.
  “A shove,” Milcah answered.
  The man responsible stood apart, looking furious with everybody who had witnessed it. He was a grandson of Shem, broad shouldered and usually patient. That made his anger less easy to explain away.
  “She took a second measure,” he said.
  “For my youngest,” the woman replied. “He has been sick.”
  “Everybody has somebody.”
  Ham raised his hands. “Enough. We have not called the household here to begin the quarrel again.”
  Noah sat in the shade of the storehouse, listening. Mother stood beside him with a cup he had forgotten to drink. She had been forgetting her own cups lately, Tirzah noticed, while remembering everyone else's.
  Shem brought out the store board. The river had fallen. Channels to the distant fields were dry. The common cistern was being emptied faster than it filled. Their grain remained adequate if the next harvest came; adequate meant less when nobody could say what the next harvest would receive.
  “We measure from today,” Ham said. “Every household takes its portion at sunrise. Nobody comes alone at night.”
  Neria looked toward the sick child's mother. “And extra need?”
  “Brought to me.”
  “To us,” she said.
  He nodded, but too late to conceal the first word.
  Tirzah found herself watching his hand on the measure. Adran had held little pieces of ore that way, apparently harmless while deciding the size of other people's lives.
  The resemblance frightened her because she loved Ham. He had burned his palms pulling a guard from a broken bridge. He had stacked fuel after Sela's birth without requiring the family to praise him. A good memory did not make the hand harmless. A resemblance did not make the men identical.
  “We need to know why the river is falling,” Japheth said.
  Shem unrolled a bark sheet scratched with journeys made during the last five years. The upper river passed into a ravine beyond their usual grazing land. Nobody had visited its source since an earthquake loosened the mountain paths in spring.
  “A fall of stone may have narrowed it,” Shem said. “Or the snows are less than we expected. Guessing will not fill a jar.”
  “I will go,” Japheth answered.
  Tirzah watched him shift his weight off the painful knee.
  “We will go,” she said.
  Across the cistern Sela raised her head. Beside her stood Ruvan, Ham and Neria's young son, with a carrying pole over one shoulder. He and Sela had been arguing for weeks about how a channel should descend between two fields. Today neither argued.
  Tirzah went to the woman with the broken handle. Her name was Ena, and she had married into Shem's growing household three years before. The jar had belonged to her mother. She kept trying to fit the handle back against its two small scars.
  "It is only a jar," she said when she noticed Tirzah watching.
  Tirzah remembered a woman standing in the western quarter of Avar with a hinge and no door. She had not known that woman's name. Even now, after so many years of telling the story, she could picture the iron more precisely than the face.
  "It was yours," she answered.
  Ena looked toward the boy with the cut. "He tried to catch it. He thought if it broke I would be angry."
  "Were you?"
  "Not with him." She pressed the handle against the jar again. "But he could not know where I would put it."
  The boy watched from Milcah's side. Tirzah beckoned him, then waited for Ena to open her arm. He approached slowly. The broken pottery remained between them until she put it down.
  Ham had begun describing the new rules. A man near the back suggested tying the cistern gate at night. Another wanted names marked on each jar so that anyone taking more could be exposed before the family. Nobody used the word punishment at first. They spoke of fairness, and Tirzah heard how quickly fairness could begin enjoying an audience.
  Veyr had once displayed Kes's debts beside a wine cup. His voice had been gentle too, almost reasonable: a young man ought to have room to grow, a family ought to settle its obligations. Tirzah's marriage had appeared in that reasoning as a thing already available for payment.
  She stood.
  "We can mark what people take," she said, "without making a child explain his thirst before a crowd."
  The man proposing the names frowned. "Nobody has said that."
  "Then let us agree not to begin it."
  Ham studied her. She did not accuse him of being Veyr. He had not earned that accusation, and an old fear was no license to spend a man's name carelessly.
  "The record belongs to the stores," Ham said at last. "It is not a wall of shame."
  Ruvan, standing behind him, nodded once. Tirzah sat beside Ena again. They gathered the broken pieces into a cloth, not because gathering them would restore the jar, but because the path should not remain dangerous to bare feet.
  Noah rose slowly. He came to the measure and lifted it out of Ham's hand.
  “We can count what remains,” he said. “We must also remember why we are counting.”
  He filled the first cup for the sick child.
  Nobody objected. Tirzah was grateful, and troubled that she had waited to see whether anybody would.
  

Chapter 3
The Things That Remained
Preparing for the journey made Tirzah feel young in a way she did not welcome.
  She opened her wooden box and found the bronze scissors in the hollow Japheth had fitted before their wedding. The hinge he had repaired after Mara's death had been repaired twice more since. The case was no longer elegant. It opened faithfully.
  “You will take all that?” Sela asked.
  “Clean cloth. Needles. Containers. Things we can actually use.”
  “And the necklace?”
  Tirzah's mother's necklace lay in its shallow dish. She had worn it sometimes, never for work. Sela liked its small dull gleam and knew enough of Adran to understand why Tirzah did not like being asked what it was worth.
  “That can stay.”
  Sela lifted it carefully. “Did your father know you would never come back?”
  “I do not think he believed that was possible.”
  “Even after you told him?”
  Tirzah folded another piece of linen before answering. “Telling someone the truth does not put the truth into his keeping. He has to receive it.”
  Her daughter considered this with the impatient seriousness of someone who wanted knowledge to become immediately useful.
  At the bottom of the box lay the third seed.
  It had stayed unplanted through one failed beginning and one living tree, through the building of their house, the vineyard's wound, and the years in which Sela had grown tall enough to fetch things from the high shelf herself. Tirzah lifted it. Its surface was hard and dark. She had no reason to believe it remained alive.
  “Why didn't you plant it?” Sela asked.
  “At first I was afraid all three would fail. Later I was afraid of having none left.”
  “But we have the tree.”
  “Yes.”
  Sela glanced toward the open door, where the leaves stirred over the drying garden. She did not say the obvious thing. Her kindness was becoming less noisy with age.
  Sela carried the necklace to the window and studied its fastening. She had seen women admire it without knowing anything of the house from which it came.
  "Were there other beautiful things?" she asked.
  "Many."
  "You never tell me about those."
  Tirzah paused over the cloth. Her daughter was right. She had made Avar vivid in cruelty and dim in pleasure, as though remembering a lovely thing would require her to withdraw a judgment.
  "There was a basin in the upper garden," she said. "At night the moon appeared inside it. Your grandmother would tell Kes and me we must let it remain there, because carrying it away would deprive the rest of the city."
  Sela smiled. "Did you believe her?"
  "Kes tried to scoop it into a jar. I pretended to be more sensible, but I hoped he would manage it."
  She described the patterned tiles, the fragrance of fruit warming against the wall, the way music came faintly through the terrace doors. Even Veyr's feast had held music worth hearing. She had not hated every note because the man paying for it was vile.
  "That sounds wonderful," Sela said, then looked embarrassed.
  "Parts of it were."
  "Can I say that?"
  "You may. It is why giving people beauty does not excuse what you take from them. A cage may have a marvelous view."
  Sela opened the clasp and closed it again, testing the little mechanism. Tirzah remembered testing her own door latch so often that Japheth finally understood she was doing more than admiring his joinery.
  "Was Veyr lost too?" her daughter asked.
  Tirzah nodded. She had no account of his last hours and refused to supply one. She would not make his death a satisfying scene she had not witnessed.
  "Did you feel glad?"
  "I felt glad when I was beyond his reach. That was before the Flood. Afterward there was too much sorrow to arrange it neatly around who deserved what."
  Sela set the necklace in its dish.
  Tirzah watched her daughter return to the box, seeing how many separate stories the girl had been trying to make into one story simple enough to inherit.
  "Ask again when you need to," she said. "I may remember something differently when you are older. You may understand something differently too."
  Sela nodded and began folding the linen without hurrying to prove she had learned the entire lesson.
  Japheth came in carrying the traveling frame. He had made it lighter, shortening the poles. On its upper crosspiece he had placed a little notch for the healer's box so it would not swing into Tirzah's back.
  She showed him the seed.
  “Do you remember how sure I was?” she asked.
  “You have been sure about many things.”
  “That the first one ought to grow because I needed it.”
  He sat beside her. “I remember.”
  She put the third seed in a small cloth pouch. “Sela wants to come.”
  “I know. She has been making herself very noticeable beside useful objects.”
  Tirzah laughed, then looked toward her daughter. “A journey is not a chance to escape lessons.”
  “Then teach me on the journey.”
  It was an answer Tirzah herself might have given Mara, and it made refusal more difficult rather than less necessary.
  “You will come as a learner,” she said. “You will not treat injuries alone because somebody admires your courage.”
  “I understand.”
  “You will tell us when you are exhausted.”
  Japheth made an unfortunate sound beside her.
  Tirzah turned. “That instruction includes you.”
  Sela grinned. For a moment the house held the lightness of earlier journeys, before Tirzah had learned how many roads did not bring people home.
  She closed the box and left the necklace in its dish.
  

Chapter 4
The Son Who Counted Water
Ruvan had volunteered before Ham asked him.
  That made Ham proud and suspicious. His son was nineteen, old enough to have discovered that a father's instructions could be followed so skillfully they became difficult to refuse. He had already packed rope, two digging tools, and a board of channel measurements.
  “Japheth needs men who know the upper slopes,” Ham said.
  “I know them.”
  “And who do not confuse an expedition with a visit.”
  Ruvan looked toward Sela's distant house. Ham noticed.
  “She is your cousin,” he said, not as a prohibition but as a reminder that family would continue after whatever foolishness young affection might attempt. The valley contained few households without shared kin. A quarrel in one tent could become everybody's supper conversation by nightfall.
  “I know who she is.”
  “You know she disagrees with you.”
  “Frequently.”
  Neria, sorting supplies at the table, smiled into her sack.
  Ruvan took his father's measure board. For weeks he had been recording the river's fall, the hours required to fill the cistern, and the amount each field received. He was proud of the work because figures seemed capable of honesty without courage.
  That morning he discovered a mistake in the figures for their own household.
  A separate channel supplied Ham's orchard. Its use had not been entered against the household allowance because the orchard fed the common stores. Ruvan had accepted the distinction until he saw a child denied a second cup at the cistern.
  “Our trees receive water after dark,” he said.
  Ham nodded. “Less is lost to heat.”
  “I have not counted it.”
  “Then count it.”
  “Against us?”
  His father turned. “Against the orchard.”
  Neria set down the sack.
  Ruvan knew the difference would matter later. A household's limit could be challenged; the orchard, useful to everyone, could become an excuse without a limit at all.
  “Who decides how much the orchard needs?” he asked.
  “I planted it.”
  The answer closed the conversation without answering the question.
  Outside, Ruvan waited beneath a roof pole he had helped raise as a boy. He loved the settlement. He loved the way his father could lift a frightened child onto his shoulders and make him believe the world had become easier. He did not want the water board to make him ashamed of those things.
  Neria came out with bread for the journey.
  “You may disagree without making him nothing but your disagreement,” she said.
  “He does not seem to think the same about me.”
  “Sometimes fathers hear departure even when sons are only asking a question.”
  She adjusted the rope on his pack. Her fingers smelled of grain.
  “Come back with what you actually find,” she told him. “Even if it embarrasses someone.”
  Ruvan embraced her awkwardly. He had begun to think adulthood meant needing fewer embraces. Leaving made the theory less convincing.
  At the path Ham waited with a new water skin. He handed it over without a speech.
  “Do not spend all your sense proving you possess it,” he said.
  Ruvan nodded. It was useful advice, and he wished his father would travel far enough to need it too.
  

Chapter 5
The Ridge of Returning Birds
They traveled as six: Japheth, Tirzah, Sela, Ruvan, Shem, and Shem's adult grandson Dagan, who knew the grazing paths beyond the northern ridge. Their packs rode on two patient animals. Their doubts traveled without assistance.
  By midday the valley had fallen away behind them. Sela kept looking back until Tirzah asked what she expected to see.
  “Our house seems smaller every time.”
  “It has not changed.”
  “I know. That is what feels strange.”
  Tirzah remembered Avar disappearing behind reeds. She resisted turning the memory into a lesson her daughter had not requested.
  On the second evening they camped beneath a ledge where birds nested in cracks. Japheth listened to the pack frame while Ruvan tightened its ties.
  “The left joint,” he said.
  Ruvan inspected it. “It looks sound.”
  “Listen when it takes weight.”
  They lifted it together. A small dry click came from the joint.
  Sela looked toward Tirzah, pleased at finding her father remarkable. Tirzah had once felt that same pleasure beside a cracked wheel in the ancient forest. Now she saw the pain in the way Japheth lowered the frame.
  She went to him after supper.
  “Your knee.”
  “It is no worse.”
  “Than what?”
  He looked away. The question had deprived him of his usual measure.
  She sat on the stone beside him. Far below, the river flashed in separate pieces between the hills. There was more water here than in the valley, but less than Dagan remembered.
  “When you were young,” she said, “you could hide pain until it became somebody else's emergency. It was a poor method even then.”
  “I would like to remain useful.”
  The plainness of the confession softened her anger.
  “I did not marry your knees.”
  “They were more persuasive at the time.”
  She laughed and leaned into him. He put his arm around her, then let his weight rest against hers. That was the part he found difficult: letting affection become support he could actually use.
  Across the camp Sela and Ruvan studied the water board. Shem drew the shape of the ravine in the dust. Dagan spoke of a place where the river had once disappeared behind a cliff and emerged with enough force to scatter stones.
  “We may have to leave the path,” Dagan said.
  “We will inspect before deciding,” Shem answered.
  The older brothers had learned to distrust confidence offered before evidence. Their children found it slow. The mountain did not care how quickly anybody preferred a decision.
  That night Tirzah woke to wingbeats.
  A bird moved against the starlight, vanished, then returned to its ledge. For a moment she was back at the ark window, measuring the future by a small body's ability to rest.
  Beside her Japheth slept with one hand open.
  She placed hers in it. He closed his fingers without waking.
  In the morning they went farther than the family had traveled in years.
  

Chapter 6
The Lake Behind the Stone
The river ended at a wall that had not existed in spring.
  Dagan stopped so abruptly that the others nearly walked into him. Across the ravine, a whole face of the mountain had collapsed. Boulders lay wedged among shattered trunks. Mud filled the spaces between them. Above the obstruction stretched a lake, flat and dark beneath cliffs whose lower trees stood drowned to their branches.
  Below it, the river slipped through a narrow opening scarcely large enough to carry the water the valley needed.
  “How deep?” Ruvan whispered.
  Shem looked at the drowned trees. “Deep enough.”
  They stood on a shelf high above the lake. The air smelled of wet stone and something turning sour beneath the surface. Sela had imagined discovering a spring they could clear with their hands. This was a mountain holding another mountain's weight.
  Japheth knelt to study the slope. A fresh crack ran through the earth beside the path. It was wider than his thumb.
  “Back from this edge,” he said.
  They obeyed. Ruvan lowered a knotted cord from a safer outcrop, measuring the height of the water against stone. Shem sent Dagan to inspect the narrow escape channel from above, without descending into it.
  Tirzah saw small movements on the lake: branches traveling slowly toward the obstruction. Each arrived, turned, and remained. The mountain had made a gate that kept receiving more weight.
  “Could we dig through?” Sela asked.
  Japheth looked at her. “Not from below. If it breaks, the water will take anything in front of it.”
  “And our valley?”
  He did not answer immediately.
  That delay was answer enough to make her pale.
  Dagan led them along the shelf to a place where the slope opened behind a curtain of low trees. A drowned trunk stood upright below, its upper branches nearly within reach. Leaves still clung to it. Sela had never seen a living tree made into a measure of danger.
  Ruvan knotted the cord at intervals along its length. He and Shem lowered a stone weight from the outcrop while Japheth kept everyone back from the fresh crack. The cord entered the water, continued, and finally slackened.
  They brought it up wet nearly to the last knot.
  "Deeper there," Dagan said, pointing toward the central gorge. "The old river ran below that cliff."
  Tirzah imagined the valley's jars lined across the lake bottom. Thousands of little measures, all made absurd by a quantity too large for anyone to carry. For days the family had argued about cups while this weight accumulated out of sight. Neither scale made the other unreal. Children could die of thirst beside water that would later threaten to drown their homes.
  Sela picked up a flat piece of bark and watched it move away from the bank. It drifted toward the obstruction, turning slowly.
  "Why does it go that way if the surface is still?"
  "The water is moving beneath what we can see," Japheth answered.
  She followed it until it vanished among branches at the barrier.
  Dagan had found a small seep near the higher end of the fall. The water passing through it was clear, but the opening lay beneath loose rocks. Nobody approached closely. Japheth watched from above, counting the pauses between small movements of earth.
  "It is not a door we can choose to close again," he said.
  Tirzah looked toward the bank opposite. Wild animals had crossed there, leaving tracks along a narrow strip of mud. Their route led away from the lake, up through brush to a shallow saddle. She showed it to Dagan. He followed with Shem until the trees hid them.
  While they waited, she and Japheth sat beside a stone warm from the morning sun. His knee had stiffened. He rubbed it without looking at her, an admission made through the hand before the mouth.
  "We have grown less able to carry things," he said.
  She considered the lake. "Perhaps we have grown more able to tell when carrying is the wrong question."
  He smiled, then watched the opposite bank. Far below the enormous surface a branch knocked against rock, making a small wooden sound that would have been harmless anywhere else.
  They spent the afternoon examining higher ground. A shallow saddle lay west of the lake, separated from it by a bank of compact earth. Beyond the saddle, an abandoned streambed curved toward a broad stony basin. If some water could be led through that way, it might reduce the pressure against the fall and feed the upper river by a longer route.
  Might became the most honest word they possessed.
  Ruvan scratched measurements onto his board. Dagan tested the earth with a digging tool. Shem counted the number of workers available below. Japheth listened to a trickle disappearing beneath a boulder and disliked the sound.
  By evening they had enough knowledge to fear the lake more accurately.
  “We return at first light,” Shem said. “The lower households need warning.”
  “We should take a second measure tomorrow,” Ruvan replied. “Find whether it is rising.”
  Shem considered. “You, Dagan, and I will stay until midday. Japheth takes the others down.”
  Sela stepped forward. “I can help with the cord.”
  Tirzah touched her shoulder. “You can help people pack before the warning becomes a flood.”
  The word brought silence.
  Below them the lake reflected the evening sky so perfectly that it seemed incapable of movement.
  Tirzah hated its beauty. Then she remembered Mara refusing to blame a fever for not announcing itself dramatically.
  Danger did not owe them an ugly face.
  

Chapter 7
The Names at Supper
They camped beyond reach of the lake before separating.
  Sela ate little. She kept studying the faces of the older people, wondering whether they could see some resemblance in the mountain water that remained hidden from her.
  At last she asked, “Was it like this before?”
  Shem set down his bowl. “Before the Flood?”
  She nodded.
  “We had a warning,” he said. “We had work. We did not have this lake.”
  “But you knew what would happen.”
  “We knew what Noah had been told. Knowing did not make us understand every hour before it came.”
  Tirzah watched her daughter absorb the distinction. It troubled her more than certainty would have.
  “Grandfather was right,” Sela said. “Why didn't the others listen?”
  The fire cracked. Tirzah thought of the farmer carrying his sister's hinge, wanting to finish one season before deciding about eternity.
  “Some thought the warning was foolish,” she said. “Some were frightened of losing what they had. Some meant to come later.”
  “And Kes?”
  It was the first time her daughter had asked the question without the softness adults used around the dead.
  “He did not want to leave our father.”
  “But Adran was cruel.”
  “Yes.”
  Sela waited for that to settle the matter. It did not.
  Tirzah lifted her bowl again, then put it down. “When our mother died, Kes slept outside my door. He pretended it was because the passage was cool. I knew he was listening in case I cried.”
  Ruvan stopped working the knot in his rope.
  “He later let men use his debts to demand my marriage. Both things happened. If I tell you only one, you will inherit a stranger wearing his name.”
  “Did he love you?”
  “He did not always know how to make love useful.”
  Japheth looked into the flames. “Neither did I.”
  Tirzah touched his wrist. She told Sela about Leth, the girl whose name she had learned late; about burned fingers and a bronze sliver hidden beneath breakfast. About Leth leaving to find her mother while the animals were entering the ark.
  “You let her?” Sela asked.
  “I could not make her life mine because I was frightened for it.”
  “I think I would have stopped her.”
  “I wanted to.”
  The confession left her daughter quiet.
  Shem spoke of workers he had known. Dagan remembered a man his father described, though he had never met him. Stories crossed the circle, some familiar, some new. Nobody used the dead to win the discussion.
  When the fire sank, Sela came beside Tirzah.
  “I thought your old life was a place you escaped.”
  “It was.”
  “And a place you miss.”
  Tirzah nodded.
  Sela leaned against her, too tall now to fit beneath her arm without bending. Tirzah made room anyway.
  Above the camp the stars remained clear. Morning would require speed, but that night they took enough time to let the people they had lost remain people.
  

Chapter 8
The Road That Split
The descent should have taken two days.
  At the first narrow turn, the path was gone.
  Fresh earth hung beneath exposed roots. A slab of stone had slid into the ravine, taking the lower track with it. Dagan had marked an older route on the bark map, but it passed through a steep gully whose footing none of the three knew.
  Japheth tested the remaining edge with his staff. It broke beneath a light touch.
  “No,” he said.
  Sela looked at the other side, so close that a thrown stone would reach it. “We could put boards across.”
  “On what supports?”
  She studied the broken earth and said nothing.
  Tirzah remembered the bridge failing beneath Kes. For a moment the years vanished: a wrist in another man's grip, Ham's burned hands, the roar below them.
  Japheth was watching her rather than the gap.
  “The gully,” she said.
  They unloaded one pack animal and led it toward the older route. Sela walked ahead, testing ground with a pole. Tirzah stayed between her and Japheth, pretending that position made either person easier to keep safe.
  Halfway down, a small stone rolled beneath Japheth's foot.
  He caught a root. His other knee struck rock. The animal behind him shied at the sound and pulled against its lead.
  Sela turned. Tirzah said her name sharply enough to stop her before she hurried onto the same loose ground.
  “Stay. Hold the animal.”
  Japheth remained upright, but his face had lost color.
  “I can walk,” he said.
  “First sit where you will not fall.”
  She cleared a place, helped him lower himself, and examined the knee. It was already swelling. Nothing about the injury looked convenient for a narrow descent.
  He watched her hands. “The warning cannot wait.”
  She knew what he intended before he said it.
  “Sela goes ahead.”
  Their daughter stood on the higher patch, holding the animal with both hands. The thought changed her posture: fear and pride arrived together.
  “Not alone,” Tirzah said.
  “Take the lighter animal,” Japheth answered. “She knows the route from the ridge. We remain here until I can put weight on this.”
  Tirzah felt herself searching for a third choice that would not exist simply because she needed it.
  Sela came closer on the sound ground. “Tell me the message exactly.”
  Japheth told her: the lake, the obstruction, the need to leave the low riverbank. No claim that the whole valley would drown. No promise that it would not. She repeated every part.
  Tirzah adjusted her daughter's pack.
  “At the fork, the right path,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “If you lose it, stop and look before moving.”
  “I will.”
  She wanted to say a hundred things that were really one thing. Instead she kissed Sela's forehead.
  “Let people help you.”
  The girl nodded, then turned down the gully.
  Tirzah watched until both daughter and animal passed beyond the bend.
  

Chapter 9
A Door She Could Open
At the first fork Sela took the right path, then stopped because remembering an instruction did not mean recognizing the ground. Rain from the previous season had carved two pale tracks across the slope. The one she wanted was partly hidden behind grass.
  The pack animal lowered its head to graze.
  "You could make some effort," she told it.
  It pulled against the rope, unimpressed.
  She climbed a little way to find the ridge. From there she could see the flat stone where they had eaten on the outward journey. She had been disputing Ruvan's measurements then and barely looking around. How much of the world had she missed because somebody else seemed responsible for keeping her within it?
  She brought the animal down carefully. At the stone she sat to mend a sandal with spare cord. Her hands shook. She wanted to hurry, but a loose fastening would send her crawling home instead of walking.
  A cloud crossed the sun. The sudden dimness made the mountains seem to draw nearer. She pictured Tirzah bending over Japheth's knee, her father's expression when he said the warning could not wait. Neither had promised that letting her go would make them feel brave. They had let her go because there was work she could do.
  She drank a little water. Then she repeated the message aloud. Lake. Stone. Rising water. Leave the low ground. Tell everyone.
  By late afternoon she reached a grazing enclosure and found a younger cousin counting animals. He looked astonished to see her alone.
  "Where are the others?"
  She told him enough to secure his help without spending the whole warning on one frightened listener. He offered to leave the animals. She asked him instead to finish bringing them uphill, then send his brother to Noah's house.
  "Is the whole mountain coming down?"
  "I don't know. Do this first."
  He nodded, grateful for a task smaller than his imagination.
  She remembered Tirzah saying that people sometimes offered the injury that could be treated while speaking of the thing that could not. She had never quite understood the saying. Now she saw the question in her cousin's face and knew she could not promise away the mountain. She could help him get the animals onto safe ground.
  At the last rise above the valley, her legs began to tremble so badly she had to rest against the animal's warm neck. It stood still at last.
  "Thank you," she whispered.
  The roofs appeared below. Smoke rose from supper fires. Nothing visible warned the people cooking that the shape of their next morning depended on a message still climbing down toward them.
  Sela tightened the repaired sandal and began the final descent.
  Sela reached the valley at dusk with her sandals split and her warning intact.
  She went first to Noah's house because Japheth had told her the message must be heard by everyone, and Noah's threshold was the place where scattered decisions could still become a household's work.
  Mother opened the door.
  For a moment Sela could not speak. The familiar room, its lamp and bowls, made the mountain seem suddenly unbelievable. She wanted somebody to tell her she had misunderstood the scale of what she saw.
  Then she remembered Japheth's face in the gully.
  “There is a lake behind a fall of stone,” she said. “Father is hurt. Mother stayed with him. Uncle Shem and the others are measuring it. We must leave the low ground.”
  Mother listened without interrupting. When Sela finished, she gave her water and called Noah.
  Ham arrived before the horn had finished sounding. Neria followed with Milcah, and the lower households gathered by lamplight. Sela stood beside the table, answering questions she could answer and saying she did not know when she could not.
  “How much water?” Ham asked.
  “Trees were standing in it with their branches covered.”
  “How long until it breaks?”
  “We do not know.”
  “Then perhaps it will hold.”
  Sela thought of the crack beside the path. “Perhaps. Father said we should not keep people below it while we find out.”
  Ham paced to the doorway. His storehouse stood on the low ground. Moving its grain would take every adult in the settlement. Leaving it would expose the only harvest they could trust.
  “We take the youngest uphill first,” Neria said. “Then the stores.”
  “If we tell everybody to abandon the fields, they will trample what is left,” Ham replied.
  Milcah looked at him. “Then tell them where to walk.”
  It was such an ordinary answer that several people moved before the argument could begin.
  Sela found herself carrying bedding to the upper houses. Children complained because their toys were missing. One old man refused to leave until his pruning knife was found. The boy injured at the cistern kept asking whether the lake was taller than a tree.
  She stopped trying to make everyone understand the same thing before helping them do the next thing.
  Near midnight she returned to her own house for blankets.
  The latch lifted easily from outside. Inside, the room smelled of herbs and wood. Her mother's necklace lay in its dish. Sela touched it but did not take it. Instead she packed food and two extra cloths for the people still on the mountain.
  At the door she looked back.
  Her parents had always been able to open this room. Now she understood that they had built it that way deliberately, long before she was born.
  She closed the door without locking it and went to find someone who could lead a rescue party at dawn.
  

Chapter 10
The Return Without Shame
Japheth was furious with the litter.
  He admired its construction, which made his objection less dignified. Dagan and two young men had fitted poles through a woven frame and padded it with blankets. They had arrived from the valley after meeting Shem and Ruvan on the ridge.
  “I can walk the easier ground,” Japheth said.
  “Then we shall all enjoy that when we reach it,” Dagan answered.
  Tirzah stepped aside to let them lift him. The humiliation on her husband's face hurt her almost as much as the injury. He had spent his life helping people cross places they could not cross alone. Receiving the same service seemed to him an accusation.
  “You carried that guard,” she reminded him.
  “He was falling into a gorge.”
  “Would you like to wait for that condition?”
  The young men became very interested in their knots.
  Japheth laughed reluctantly. “You have acquired Mara's temper.”
  “Only the useful part.”
  She walked beside him during the descent. Above them Ruvan and Shem brought the measurements: the lake had risen a hand's breadth overnight. Water was seeping through fresh openings in the debris. The narrow channel had widened in one place and clogged in another.
  “It is changing,” Ruvan said.
  “We need everyone who can dig,” Shem replied. “And somebody who understands how to let water move without following it downhill.”
  Japheth raised his head from the blanket. “I can draw that.”
  Tirzah looked at him.
  “From a chair,” he added.
  At the ridge they rested. A broad view opened across the valley. The upper houses were ringed with blankets, sacks, and people carrying things from the riverbank. Ham's horn sounded once, a working signal this time, and tiny figures answered from the path.
  Sela came up with food.
  She looked at the litter, and her father watched to see what it would do to her opinion of him. She set down the basket and adjusted the cloth beneath his shoulder.
  “You look uncomfortable,” she said.
  “A correct assessment.”
  “We can fold another blanket.”
  That was all. His daughter did not require him to stand in order to remain her father.
  He took her hand and held it longer than necessary.
  They reached the upper houses before evening. Mother had cleared a place where he could see the work without obstructing it. Noah sat nearby, and the two men began speaking of channels before the litter had been removed.
  Tirzah left them with water and went outside.
  Sela stood beneath the eaves, suddenly crying.
  “I thought,” she began, and could not finish.
  Tirzah embraced her.
  The girl had carried the message, persuaded adults, moved children, and organized help. None of it had meant she was unafraid.
  “You are home,” Tirzah said.
  Sela held her tightly. “So are you.”
  They remained there until the evening wind cooled the damp faces of both.
  

Chapter 11
The Ledger and the Orchard
Milcah counted the displaced households twice because the first count was wrong.
  “Wrong where?” Ham asked.
  “One tent was entered as one household. It contains two.”
  “They can share a measure.”
  She looked up from the board. “They are sharing a roof, not becoming fewer people.”
  The storehouse had been half emptied. Grain stood in covered heaps above the flood line, and workers slept beside it to keep animals away. The cistern remained below, inconveniently close to the river. Every cup required somebody to descend and carry it back.
  Ruvan laid his own figures beside Milcah's. He had measured Ham's orchard channel before leaving for the mountain. The amount, when multiplied across the nights, exceeded what several households had received together.
  Ham read the marks. His face hardened.
  “Fruit goes to the common stores.”
  “So do the crops in the outer fields,” Ruvan replied. “Those have been allowed to dry.”
  “You would rather lose every tree?”
  “I would rather we say plainly what we are choosing.”
  Neria stood by the doorway, holding a jar. She did not rescue her husband from the answer or her son from its cost.
  Ham pushed the board away. “You return from one journey and believe you understand the entire valley.”
  Ruvan's mouth tightened. Tirzah recognized Kes in the wish to be regarded as a man without having to remain a learner. She also recognized Adran in the way Ham had turned a question about water into a question about loyalty.
  “Ruvan has entered a use we were not counting,” Milcah said. “That remains true whatever age he was when he entered it.”
  Ham walked outside.
  Nobody followed immediately. For a moment the room held the difficult relief of a truth finally spoken and the knowledge that speaking it had settled very little.
  Later Tirzah found Neria beside the upper well.
  “He has worked himself empty,” Neria said.
  “I know.”
  “It does not make him right.”
  Tirzah waited.
  “After the vineyard,” Neria continued, “I thought every disagreement would become that disagreement. Sometimes I still listen for it beneath ordinary words.”
  The old wound remained among them, though the younger children knew it chiefly as a silence that interrupted their elders.
  “What will you do?” Tirzah asked.
  “Count the orchard.”
  That evening Neria helped Ruvan draw its channel on the common board. She marked the fruit trees, their ages, which could survive reduced watering, which were already failing. The work was precise and painful. These were trees she had planted while imagining a new world spacious enough to permit hope.
  Ham returned when the lamp was low.
  He studied the board, then sat beside them. “The far row has poorer roots than you have marked.”
  Ham studied the marks for the far orchard. Neria had entered every tree separately. He could recognize her hand in the small hooks she added to the figures. They were the same hooks she used when counting feed inside the ark.
  "You think I have forgotten the children," he said to his son.
  Ruvan set the board flat. "I think you remember what you planted every time you look out the door. The outer fields are harder to see."
  Ham glanced at him. That was a more difficult accusation because it was not an accusation at all. It was a description of how a man's attention might become unfair without his having resolved to be cruel.
  Neria touched the edge of the board. "The orchard matters. We should not need to make the children matter less in order to say so."
  "Those trees fed everybody last season."
  "Then write that beside their use," Ruvan answered. "Let everyone see both."
  Ham pulled the board closer. He remembered the first planting, the absurd little sticks he had set into mud while the mountain still seemed more certain than any crop. Neria had laughed at his spacing, then replanted three while he argued. Their orchard was a record of learning, not only a possession. The thought made sharing its account painful in a different way. He would be sharing his mistakes as well.
  "The north channel leaks," he said. "More than this shows."
  Ruvan looked up. "Since when?"
  "Since the frost. I thought I could mend it before anyone needed to count."
  Neria closed her eyes briefly. Ham saw her disappointment and wanted to defend the difficulty of the repair. He forced himself to name the amount first.
  Ruvan entered the loss.
  When they had finished, the board showed less certainty and more waste than any of them wished. It was a better board. Milcah came to check it, found two mismatched measures, and made them correct those too. Ham bristled, then stayed seated.
  Outside, children carried empty cups toward the upper seep. The sound of their voices reached the room while the adults adjusted figures that would have looked less troublesome if kept private.
  Neria brought fresh water for the lamp beside them. She set down the cup without a word of triumph. Her marriage had never benefited from an audience gathering to see whether she could prove her husband foolish.
  Ham looked at her hand on the table, then placed his own beside it. She let them remain together while Ruvan checked the last column.
  Ruvan gave him the stylus.
  Ham corrected the row. He did not apologize yet. He had begun to contribute to the truth, and Neria let that beginning stand without pretending it was the whole repair.
  

Chapter 12
The Woman Beside Noah
Mother's hands shook while she poured the evening broth.
  Tirzah saw it because she had been looking for smaller signs: the cup left untouched, the chair sought more often, the moment of confusion before an ordinary name returned. She waited until the others had eaten and took the pot from her.
  “Sit with me.”
  “There are bowls still outside.”
  “They will remain bowls while you sit.”
  The older woman smiled. “You have been spending too much time with Milcah.”
  They sat beneath the eaves. Displaced children slept in a row inside the house. Noah was telling the youngest a story about a stubborn animal, complete with an imitation that would have surprised people who knew him only through solemn retellings.
  Mother listened, her face softening.
  “People forget he can be ridiculous,” she said.
  Tirzah looked at her hands. “How long have you been feeling ill?”
  “Tired.”
  “How long?”
  She answered at last. Longer than Tirzah had wanted. The morning dizziness had begun before the spring failed. She had not wished to add her small trouble to the valley's larger one.
  Tirzah thought of Mara dying while she tried to make diligence become a cure. She asked what she could ask, listened without making the answer smaller, and brought Milcah to help. They arranged rest, food, and company. None of them named a disease they could not know.
  Noah came out while they were speaking.
  Mother looked guilty, as though being cared for had exposed a misused privilege.
  He sat beside her without asking whether it was serious enough to justify his time.
  “I thought you were listening,” he said.
  “I was.”
  “From farther away than usual.”
  Her hand found his.
  Before she rose, Mother asked Tirzah to bring the mending basket.
  "You are supposed to rest."
  "I am not proposing to raise a roof. There is something in it I want."
  Inside the basket lay a square of coarse cloth, older than anything Sela remembered. One corner bore the strong stitches Mother had taught Tirzah during the seven days beneath the quiet sky.
  "You kept this?" Tirzah asked.
  "You made the first row too fine. I wanted you to see the difference when it wore."
  "Did it?"
  Mother pointed. The finer seam had frayed. The coarse one held. Time had completed a lesson neither remembered to announce.
  Noah looked at the cloth and smiled. "You used to mend my sleeves before telling me they needed it."
  "It was simpler than waiting for you to notice the cold."
  Tirzah sat again. The older woman's weariness was plain now that she had stopped arranging her posture around everybody else's comfort. She wanted to ask about the early years, before the family became eight names inside a vessel.
  Mother spoke of kitchens where neighbors arrived for food and stayed to tell her why Noah was wrong. Some were rude. Others brought something helpful and expressed their doubts only while making sure she was not offended. One woman stopped coming because her husband disliked being laughed at by men who had never lifted a beam.
  "I thought kindness would settle more things," Mother said.
  "It settled some."
  "Yes. Not all the things I chose it for."
  Noah's thumb moved over the back of her hand. There were histories between them that Tirzah knew she would never hear completely. Marriage had given her one household's intimate language, not the right to possess every other.
  Mother held out the cloth.
  "Take this to Sela. She will need to learn what holds."
  Tirzah folded it carefully. The lesson had begun with thread, widened into a way to bear waiting, and returned to thread without becoming less useful.
  Noah asked Mother whether she remembered the first roof they repaired after leaving the ark. She corrected his account of which son had dropped the rope. They began disputing it with a fondness that made Tirzah feel briefly like a guest.
  She rose quietly, carrying the old square of cloth. On the threshold she heard Mother laugh. That sound stayed with her while she crossed the yard toward her own husband.
  When she reached Japheth, he was drawing the proposed spill channel on a board propped over his lap. She watched his careful lines for a while before speaking.
  “Your mother needs more rest.”
  He set down the stylus.
  “Will she recover?”
  “I do not know.”
  He accepted the words, but she saw how much it cost. They had survived a world ending; part of each still believed the eight should have become exempt from ordinary loss.
  “We can change her work,” Tirzah said. “We can stay with her. We can stop letting her hide exhaustion inside hospitality.”
  He nodded, then asked her to sit.
  She sat on the edge of the bedding. He put his head against her shoulder, returning to a posture he had used when young and frightened of admitting how much he needed her.
  “I thought we had more time,” he said.
  “We may. I do not want us to spend it only guessing.”
  The next morning Mother woke to find the pot already on the fire, the bowls washed, and a little girl asking whether she would like more broth.
  She laughed, then cried without hiding it.
  

Chapter 13
The Vessel Above the Clouds
They needed rope and straight timber for the mountain work.
  The valley's houses had both, but every beam removed would expose someone to weather. Japheth looked toward the high peaks and said what all the older people had been avoiding.
  “The ark.”
  Silence gathered around the drawing board.
  The vessel remained on the mountain, weathering beyond their daily sight. People had visited it in the first years to recover tools and stores. Later its distance grew larger than the walk required. The young knew it as a landmark at the edge of stories. The old knew too many rooms inside it.
  “There are interior fittings we can remove,” Japheth said. “Braces we no longer need. Sound rope in the dry compartments, if we are fortunate.”
  “You cannot climb,” Tirzah replied.
  He looked at her. “I know.”
  Admitting it cost him more than his injury.
  Shem offered to lead the party. Dagan would come, and Ruvan, with three workers for the return loads. Sela asked to go. Tirzah surprised her by saying yes before she finished arranging the reasons.
  “I will come too,” Tirzah said.
  Japheth understood before anybody else. Kes's pegs were still in the joint beside the old storage passage, if the wood had held. No object could preserve her brother, but the thought of that small useful work rotting while people lacked materials had begun to feel like another kind of loss.
  Before they left, Japheth gave Sela a map of the compartments. He had drawn it from memory, stopping only where changes in later repairs made his certainty unreliable.
  “Do not enter a space simply because it appears here,” he told her. “A drawing cannot tell you what years have done to a floor.”
  She rolled it carefully.
  They climbed for three days, following ridges where the air thinned and the grass gave way to stone. Tirzah found herself remembering the first ascent after the waters: raw earth, the animals dispersing, the strange courage required to stand without a wall beside her.
  At dawn on the fourth day, cloud drifted from the slope.
  The ark stood above them.
  Sela stopped.
  Nothing her grandfather said at supper had prepared her for the scale. The great hull lay angled against the mountain, its dark surface streaked by seasons. Plants grew near one side. A broken rail hung from the approach their family had made years before.
  “You lived in that,” she whispered.
  “We did.”
  Tirzah had expected terror. Instead she felt an almost unbearable tenderness toward the battered timbers. They had held the eight when no ground could be seen. Now they stood empty beneath birds that entered and left without consulting anyone's watch.
  Shem tested the approach.
  Tirzah waited for his signal, then put her hand against the wall.
  The wood was cold. This time, when she took her hand away, there was no rain outside it demanding her return.
  

Chapter 14
Twelve Small Pieces of Wood
The entrance passage smelled of old pitch and dust.
  Sela unrolled the map. A shaft of light crossed the upper boards, illuminating a fragment of cord and a bird's discarded feather. The drawing described movement: feeding routes, store paths, sleeping places. The empty interior described absence.
  “Here?” she asked.
  Tirzah nodded.
  The storage joint remained sound. She recognized it before she saw the pegs. The wood had darkened, and one edge had split slightly, but the fastening Kes made during his season at the field still held the frame.
  She knelt.
  For years she had remembered the sharp sound of driving a peg home. She had not remembered how low she had bent, or the awkward position of the mallet, or how Japheth stepped back to let the work belong to her.
  Sela crouched beside her.
  “Which was yours?”
  Tirzah touched the third peg. “I think this one.”
  She smiled at the admission. Memory had grown exact in some places while quietly blurring others.
  They could recover the whole short brace by removing its outer ties. Shem inspected the surrounding structure and marked what could safely be taken. Ruvan and Dagan worked together, supporting the brace before loosening it. Nobody pried at the pegs themselves.
  When the piece came free, Tirzah held it across her knees.
  The twelve pegs were small, ordinary, usable. Her brother's best season did not look legendary. It looked like work completed by a young man who briefly permitted himself to learn.
  “He could have stayed,” Sela said.
  “Yes.”
  “Did he know you used these?”
  “I sent word.”
  “Did he answer?”
  Tirzah shook her head.
  For a while they sat without supplying a reason. She would not invent a letter after so many years merely to make silence gentler.
  Shem led them to the lower passage before they returned to the entrance. The air cooled as they descended. Empty enclosure doors stood open at different angles, some wedged by settling timber. A nest occupied a feeding hatch.
  "Not there," he told Dagan when the young man reached toward a loose board. "That partition is holding more than it appears to."
  Tirzah stopped near the old lamp shelf. The hook Noah had changed after the accident remained fixed above a darkened patch of wood. She pointed it out to Sela.
  "Your father smelled hot oil before anything else. A container had shifted. The lamp was down. Ham slipped while coming to help and opened his forehead against the partition."
  Ruvan looked up. His father's faint scar had always been part of his face. Hearing its origin made him see a young man where he usually saw authority.
  "He says he was helping repair a fastening," Ruvan murmured.
  "He was," Tirzah said. "He was also being very difficult about sitting still afterward."
  Shem laughed. The sound traveled farther than expected through the emptied compartments.
  Sela examined the protected hook. "This was the new arrangement?"
  "Yes. And a new schedule. We began checking in pairs. Rest stopped being something awarded after every possible danger was gone."
  "Father still forgets."
  "Learning something once does not always mean you keep it."
  Ruvan touched his own bandage, understanding a little too readily.
  They continued to the small enclosure where a bird had stopped eating during the storm. Tirzah could not identify the exact perch now. She remembered watching for a single peck as though it might justify everyone who had vanished beyond the walls.
  She told Sela what Mother had said: care for it, without asking it to carry all the dead.
  "Did it live?"
  "It ate. I do not know which bird it became when we went out. There were too many moving at once."
  The incompleteness troubled her daughter. Stories were supposed to keep the answer attached to the question. This place held events no memory could line up so obediently.
  At the far hatch sunlight entered through a crack. Dust crossed it in slow bright shapes. Once the opening would have terrified Japheth, a possible weakness in their only shelter. Now it let the nest's parents come and go.
  Sela watched a small bird pass through.
  "It has a great deal to do," Tirzah said softly.
  She heard the younger Japheth saying it above their wedding field and felt the old room loosen around her. The vessel did not need every life inside it to remain the one she remembered.
  In the sleeping compartment they found the narrow day board, left on a shelf when the family moved down to the valley. It still bore the shallow marks Tirzah had made to count the days. Some had worn nearly away. Beside the marks were notes of work: cloth mended, a sack checked, a lesson exchanged with Neria. She had imagined the ark preserving only terror. Here was evidence of other hours.
  Sela traced a mark without touching it.
  “Were you happy sometimes?”
  “Sometimes.”
  “Inside all that?”
  “Yes.”
  They returned to the entrance where the recovered fittings were being bundled. Sound rope had survived in two covered compartments. Much else had to be left. The party carried what they could safely lift rather than treating the vessel as a treasure room.
  Tirzah wrapped the brace in cloth.
  At the threshold she looked back once.
  She did not ask the empty hull to release her brother. It had never contained him.
  She carried out the work he had left, ready to give it another use.
  

Chapter 15
The Light Beneath the Boards
Ruvan discovered the damaged floor by nearly trusting it.
  He had returned for a length of rope that slipped from the bundle. In the dim passage, one plank appeared no different from those around it. His heel broke through as he stepped.
  He caught the rail. His leg dropped into darkness.
  The sound brought Sela running from the entrance.
  She stopped before reaching him, remembering the gully and Tirzah's sharp instruction. Ruvan hung with both arms braced, his face strained. Below him lay an empty compartment several body lengths deep.
  “Don't pull on me yet,” he said.
  “I wasn't going to.”
  Her voice sounded steadier than she felt.
  She called Dagan and Shem. Then she brought a broad board from the recovered fittings, laying it across tested supports so the rescuers could approach without placing their weight on the damaged planks. Shem secured a rope around Ruvan's chest while Dagan supported his leg.
  They lifted him slowly.
  On sound ground he sat breathing hard. The broken edge had cut his calf. Sela opened the healer's box, then stopped and looked for Tirzah.
  Her mother was beside her already.
  “Tell me what you see.”
  Sela described the cut, the bleeding, the way Ruvan could move his foot. Tirzah guided the work, taking over where the younger hands hesitated. They cleaned and covered the wound. Ruvan watched a patch of light on the wall rather than the cloth.
  “You can say it hurts,” Sela told him.
  “It hurts.”
  She glanced at him. “You answered very quickly.”
  “I am trying a new method.”
  She laughed in spite of herself.
  Later, outside the hull, he sat beside her while the others adjusted the loads.
  “I thought I knew where I was stepping,” he said.
  “Father told us a drawing couldn't tell us what the years had done.”
  “You remembered.”
  “After you fell.”
  He smiled. “That seems to be when I remember most useful advice.”
  Below the mountain the cloud spread like a pale sea. Sela had never understood why Tirzah sometimes grew quiet at a beautiful view. Now beauty and fear seemed able to occupy the same distance.
  “When I saw you,” she began.
  Ruvan looked toward her.
  She disliked how difficult a few honest words could become. “I didn't want the next story about you to be one I told without you.”
  He reached for her hand, leaving enough space for her to withdraw it.
  She did not.
  Tirzah saw them from the entrance and looked away before their first private moment could become a family event.
  They would have enough to learn without being watched through every lesson.
  That evening the party began the descent, carrying rope, timber, the old brace, and a knowledge of the ark no story at supper could have given its children.
  

Chapter 16
The Garden Left Open
Tirzah found her tree shedding leaves in the heat.
  They returned with the recovered supplies at midday. Japheth had completed his drawings; Ham had assembled digging parties; Noah had persuaded the remaining households to move above the low riverbank. Work was happening in every direction except the garden.
  Tirzah set the old brace on the table and went outside.
  The leaves beneath the tree were curled, brittle at their edges. Its roots reached toward the spring that no longer fed them. Flowers Mara would have called unnecessarily beautiful lay flat in their beds. Sela had watered the herbs beside the door, but no child with a jar could save an entire summer.
  Japheth came slowly behind her, leaning on a fitted staff.
  “I meant to send someone with water,” he said.
  She turned. “For the tree?”
  “I knew you would be hurt.”
  Below them people carried drinking water uphill, each journey stealing an hour from other necessary work. Tirzah looked at the tree and understood how easily love could begin demanding an exception.
  Adran had believed his garden deserved water because his daughter liked it. She had hated him for making preference look like necessity.
  “Not while the children need it,” she said.
  Japheth lowered his head. “I did not want to tell you there was something else I could not keep.”
  The confession moved her more than the tree.
  She came beside him and touched his face, rough with several days of forgotten shaving.
  “You are not failing because things remain beyond you.”
  “You say that as though you believe it easily.”
  “I am practicing.”
  They sat on the bench drawn into their future before they left the ark. It had been repaired often. One leg was newer than the others, and the seat bore Sela's childhood knife marks where she had tried to carve a bird.
  “I saw the day marks,” Tirzah told him.
  He smiled faintly. “I thought those would be gone.”
  “Some are. Enough remain.”
  She described the sleeping place, the empty feed passages, the light reaching through a crack that had not been there during the storm. He listened, not asking to be comforted by an invented account of things still perfect.
  When she told him about Ruvan, his grip tightened on the staff.
  “He is safe,” she said.
  “And Sela?”
  “Learning. Very much as we learned.”
  He glanced toward her. She let him discover the other meaning.
  They stayed on the bench until the heat began to ease. Then Tirzah collected sound seeds from the dying beds and wrapped cuttings from plants that might survive transplanting. The garden could not be carried away, but parts of it could be shared.
  Japheth held the basket while she worked.
  The door behind them stood open. She left it so when they went to the upper houses, allowing the room to receive evening without anybody remaining to supervise it.
  

Chapter 17
Where the Water Might Go
The digging parties reached the lake after the recovered supplies had been carried up from the valley.
  Ham led the strongest workers. Shem brought tools, Ruvan brought his measurements, and Tirzah established a sheltered place above the work where injured people could rest. Sela arranged the clean cloth and covered the water jars without being reminded.
  Japheth's plan lay on the ground beneath stones: a shallow spill channel through the western saddle, widening toward the old streambed. They would lower its outer section first, leaving a bank between the excavation and the lake until an escape route and guiding walls were ready.
  Nobody would work below the obstruction. Nobody would open the bank while workers remained in the channel. These rules were repeated often enough that impatient people began hearing them as obstacles rather than protection.
  “We could finish sooner if we cut from both sides,” a young man said.
  Ham looked toward the lake. For a moment Tirzah expected him to agree.
  “You heard the plan,” he answered. “Dig where you can come back from.”
  The first day passed in hard earth, blunt tools, and disappointment. The saddle's crust gave way to dense clay. A narrow cut that looked impressive from above held too little space for the amount of water they needed to move.
  Ruvan measured the lake again.
  Higher.
  At dusk clouds gathered beyond the peaks. Everybody watched them without pretending the color meant less than it did.
  Tirzah found Ham alone beside the plan.
  “You think we are too late,” she said.
  “I think about how many things I have asked people to leave.”
  “Things can be carried back.”
  “Not if the river takes them.”
  She sat on the stone nearby. His face looked older than it had at the cistern, when authority seemed capable of solving thirst by organizing it.
  “My father would have kept the grain behind his gate,” she said. “He would have told everyone he was protecting it for them.”
  Ham looked at her sharply.
  “You are here,” she continued. “Your storehouse is emptying for people you do not find easy. I am telling you the difference because I know the resemblance frightens you too.”
  He rubbed his hands together. Old rope scars crossed the palms.
  “Ruvan thinks I care more for trees than children.”
  “Ask him what he thinks.”
  “He will tell me.”
  “That is usually the difficulty.”
  Ham laughed once, without pleasure but without anger either.
  Later he brought food to his son and asked him to show the lake measurements again. Ruvan explained the marks. Ham listened until the explanation was finished.
  The first drops fell during the night, soft enough to sound like insects on the shelter roof.
  By morning they no longer sounded soft.
  

Chapter 18
Rain on Another Roof
Tirzah woke with the ark in her mouth.
  For one breath she tasted pitch, heard confined animals, felt the floor lifting beneath her. Then the shelter's open side returned, with the gray mountain beyond it and Sela crouching over a jar.
  “Mother?”
  Tirzah pressed her hands against the bedding. The ground was still. This rain belonged to another danger, another morning.
  “I am here,” she said, partly to herself.
  The lake had risen overnight. Runoff came down the cliffs in pale sheets. Mud moved under workers' feet, and the unfinished spill channel collected water that had to be bailed away before digging could continue.
  Ham stopped the lowest work. The decision angered people who feared stopping more than falling.
  “We lose the day,” one said.
  “You may lose your footing,” Ham answered. “I will not exchange you for a day.”
  They moved to the safer upper cut. Ruvan and Dagan inspected the guiding walls. Shem sent two runners to the valley with the warning that the lake was rising faster. Every low path must be abandoned now, including the one to the cistern.
  During a brief pause in the rain, Shem asked Tirzah to come to the higher lookout. He had covered the lake measurements with a cloth, but the mark he wanted her to see was on the rock itself. Water had climbed almost to it.
  "I keep thinking of what we were promised," he said.
  Tirzah watched the gray surface. The covenant had not said that every river would remain within its banks, or that children would never need to flee a low house. She knew the words well enough to resist making a smaller disaster into evidence that the whole promise had failed. Knowing did not quiet her body.
  "I do too," she answered.
  Shem sat on the stone. He looked exhausted, with the carefulness of a man whose weariness had begun to slow his thinking.
  "Noah would pray before we begin again," he said.
  "Then pray."
  He bowed his head. The prayer was short. He asked for steadiness, for help seeing what they had missed, for the people carrying children uphill. He did not announce that the next day's work would succeed because his family had once been chosen to survive.
  Tirzah listened. Then she spoke the names of those at home and the names of those digging. When she came to Japheth, her voice caught.
  Shem waited.
  The rain began again before they stood. It was only rain: water falling through cold air, making the rock slick, entering soil already too heavy with it. Her frightened mind wanted a message in every drop. She let the prayer remain a request rather than an explanation of weather.
  On the way down they found a young worker sitting alone beneath an overhang. He had been sent to rest and was ashamed of obeying. Shem lowered himself beside him.
  "Good place," he said. "Room for two?"
  The young man moved over. Tirzah brought them covered cups and returned to the shelter. Behind her she heard Shem asking the worker about a sister at home. The conversation did not make the lake smaller. It made the next hour large enough to contain something other than fear.
  Sela was securing the shelter's open side. Tirzah took one end of the rope and helped. Mother had taught her to strengthen stitches before the cloth was strained; now she watched her daughter pull a knot tight, then test it instead of assuming it would hold.
  Thunder rolled far away. She kept her hand on the rope until the sound passed.
  At noon a worker arrived with a crushed hand.
  Sela looked at the swelling and went pale. Tirzah sat him down, asked what happened, and called for help lifting the tool that still hung from his wrist by its strap. Nothing about the work felt legendary. It felt wet, urgent, and imperfect.
  After they had done what they could, Sela went outside.
  Tirzah found her behind the shelter, retching.
  “I thought I could do it,” the girl said.
  “You did help.”
  “I wanted to be like you.”
  Tirzah drew back enough to see her face. “Then you must include this part. I have been frightened. I have needed somebody else to take the cloth.”
  Sela wiped her mouth, ashamed of its messiness.
  “Mara would have told you to wash and drink,” Tirzah said. “Then return when your hands are steady. She would not have made you a marvel.”
  Thunder rolled beyond the eastern cliff.
  Tirzah flinched before she could stop herself. Sela saw.
  For a moment neither pretended to be the stronger one.
  They went back together.
  That evening Shem gathered the party above the excavation. The rain was easing, but the lake had reached a shallow mark Ruvan had expected it to take days to cross.
  “We open the channel tomorrow,” Shem said, “if we can finish the outlet.”
  Ham looked toward the dark fall of stone. A muted cracking sound came from somewhere inside it.
  Nobody mistook that sound for thunder.
  

Chapter 19
The Weight Behind the Gate
Before dawn Dagan found muddy water emerging through the main obstruction.
  It had been clear the previous evening. Now it carried clay and small pieces of wood. He returned uphill with the news, and Ruvan understood before the others: the water was taking material out of the barrier itself.
  They had less time than the drawings assumed.
  Ham sent everyone away from the original ravine. Shem placed watchers where both the obstruction and the spill channel could be seen. A horn would order retreat. Nobody was to interpret its signal as a request for one last useful act.
  The western outlet remained unfinished at its lower bend. Three enormous stones narrowed it. They had to be shifted before the lake was allowed through, or the new channel might send water back across the worksite.
  Ruvan examined the recovered rope. They looped it around the largest stone, bracing a timber lever on sound rock. Ham took one side with Dagan. Sela carried wedges from the shelter, keeping to the marked safe ground.
  The stone moved the width of a finger.
  They rested, repositioned the lever, pulled again. Mud sucked at the base. The rope tightened until its fibers sang.
  Tirzah watched Ham's hands and saw the bridge across the old gorge. Then his hands had pulled a man who meant to force her home. Now they were pulling against a mountain for people who had challenged his rule at the cistern.
  She wished virtue could become simple by repetition. It remained a choice with aching shoulders.
  The stone rolled at last.
  Its movement knocked Ruvan sideways. He stumbled beyond the firm patch and slid into the half cut channel. Ham caught the rope rather than his son, bracing it so the lever would not follow him down.
  “Stay there!” he shouted.
  Ruvan lay on the slope, breathing hard. The cut on his calf had reopened. He could climb if the earth held long enough.
  Sela approached the marked edge with the second rope. Dagan secured her to a timber anchor before she threw its end. Ruvan caught it on the second attempt.
  For several moments Tirzah could see only hands.
  Hands securing knots. Hands shaking with strain. Her daughter's hands, no longer small, feeding rope toward someone she loved.
  Ruvan came up covered in mud.
  Ham grasped his shoulders and held him so fiercely that the young man winced.
  “I am here,” Ruvan said.
  Ham let go enough to see his face.
  Behind them the watcher sounded one short note: hurry, not retreat. The seep through the obstruction had become a stream.
  The lower bend was open. Workers cleared their tools. Shem counted everyone onto the high shelf before giving the signal.
  Ham and Dagan moved to the remaining earthen bank. They would open a narrow notch from above, using long handled tools, and withdraw before the water widened it.
  Tirzah went to the shelter and lifted the healer's box.
  She did not look away when the first muddy thread crossed the cut.
  

Chapter 20
The River Unbound
At first the new channel seemed too small to matter.
  Water slid over its lip, darkening the clay. It pooled against a guiding stone, found the next opening, and began moving toward the outlet. Ham and Dagan withdrew to the shelf. The watching workers leaned forward as though attention might persuade the current to grow.
  Then the lip collapsed.
  A thick rush of water entered the cut. It tore at the sides, carrying soil ahead of it. The guiding walls held at the first bend. At the second, one section shuddered and scattered its smaller stones into the stream.
  Ruvan shouted that the flow was turning.
  Shem sounded the retreat horn.
  Everyone moved uphill. Tirzah helped the injured worker while Sela carried the box. Behind them water spilled across the abandoned tool ground, sweeping away a blanket and a wooden bowl.
  Ham stopped beside the shelter.
  For one terrible instant Tirzah thought he was turning for the recovered rope. Instead he lifted the worker's remaining pack and followed them, leaving the tools to go.
  They reached the upper shelf as the main obstruction broke.
  The sound was not one crash. It was a series of heavy movements becoming continuous: stones grinding, wood tearing, water finding every gap at once. A dark front burst into the original ravine and struck its first bend with enough force to throw spray above the trees.
  The new spill channel had lowered some pressure. It had not made the mountain harmless.
  Sela gripped Tirzah's arm.
  From their height they watched both streams join below, the old river suddenly too small for the water bearing its name. The torrent rushed toward the valley. Everything depended now on the warning, on the emptied houses, on people obeying a message carried by a girl in split sandals.
  Tirzah heard no screams. The absence did not prove enough.
  Below, in the upper valley, Neria heard the first low roar while cutting bread.
  The knife stopped in her hand. Ham was on the mountain. Ruvan was with him. She had agreed to remain because someone had to keep the displaced households from becoming a camp without food or order. Now order seemed a very small occupation.
  Canaan came through the door. "It is moving."
  She set down the knife. "The last houses?"
  "Clear. Dagan's aunt has the youngest. I will count them here."
  He stood at the threshold with the household board. Neria watched people arrive, one family at a time, more frightened by the noise than the warning had made them. A little boy ran toward the path for a toy left in the lower house. Canaan caught him without scolding and asked him to help count the cups instead.
  The torrent reached the first bend. Muddy spray rose above the river trees. Something large struck the cistern wall; the wall broke outward and disappeared. Neria saw a roof tilt, slide, and turn in the current. For one moment she could identify the storehouse by the angle Ham had insisted was best for winter light. Then it was a mass of wood passing beneath another shape.
  She did not have time to mourn it. A man was helping his wife up the final path, moving slowly because she carried their child. Milcah sent two people to meet them. The man slipped. His cry reached the shelter almost simultaneously with the crash below.
  Neria ran as far as the safe edge. The rescuers brought him up while his wife clutched the child with such strength that the little one began to wail.
  "Here," Neria said. "Here is ground. Sit here."
  They obeyed because she gave them a place their bodies could understand.
  The man held his arm at a terrible angle. Milcah took over, asking plain questions. Neria returned to the doorway. Canaan's board lay open, and she made him read the names aloud twice. Every answer came from somewhere nearby. One family had been entered beneath the wrong awning; they were found, irritated at being called missing while having done exactly what they were told.
  Neria laughed, then cried against the doorpost where she hoped no frightened child would see.
  Noah came beside her. He did not say the ark had been worse. He took the cups from the small boy and began passing water.
  By the time the runner departed for the mountain, the bread was cut. Neria wrapped two portions for him.
  "Tell Ham we are here," she said. "Tell him everybody is here. And tell him to come by the safe path, however long he thinks it takes."
  She went back to the table and wiped the knife. Below, the river continued carrying away things whose owners had lived long enough to miss them.
  They waited until the immediate surges diminished. Shem forbade descent while the ravine continued shedding stone. Ham paced the shelf, stopping each time Ruvan looked at him.
  “I should have gone back,” he said.
  “You sent runners,” his son answered.
  “I should have seen everybody uphill myself.”
  “You were needed here.”
  Ham shook his head. The mountain had released water; it had not released fathers from believing their eyes could guard every person they loved.
  At dusk the first returning runner reached them.
  The lower fields were under water. The cistern wall had broken. Ham's storehouse was gone. One household had delayed too long, but neighbors had brought them uphill before the first surge. A man had broken his arm in the scramble. People were being counted.
  “Alive?” Ham asked.
  “All I saw.”
  They received the limitation as part of the answer. Shem sent the runner to rest and prepared for descent at first safe light.
  That night Sela sat beside Ruvan under a borrowed blanket. Tirzah could see their heads leaning together against the cold.
  She found the third seed in her pouch and closed her hand around it.
  The valley below was changed again.
  She did not know what was left. This time, however, the people she most feared losing were near enough to answer when she called their names.
  

Chapter 21
Everybody at the Count
Milcah met them on the ridge with her board pressed against her chest.
  Ham saw her face and stopped before anybody spoke.
  “Counted,” she said. “Everybody.”
  He sat down on the path.
  For years Tirzah would remember that more clearly than the bursting lake: Ham lowering himself into dust because relief had taken away the strength fear lent him. Ruvan crouched beside him. Neria came uphill carrying a cup and set it in her husband's hands.
  Noah waited beneath the upper awning with Mother beside him. She looked pale, but she insisted on seeing them arrive rather than hearing afterward that they had done so. Japheth stood leaning on his staff, searching each face before allowing himself to smile.
  Tirzah went to him.
  He put his arms around her awkward pack and held her anyway.
  “You have brought half the mountain,” he murmured.
  “Only the useful part.”
  Sela came next. He touched her cheek, then looked at Ruvan's bandaged leg. He began to say something about watching where one stepped and stopped when his daughter raised an eyebrow.
  “I have been improving the instructions,” she said.
  He laughed.
  The lower valley lay beneath streaks of mud and standing water. A channel had cut through Ham's orchard. Some trees leaned with their roots exposed. Others remained upright, strangely unchanged among the wreckage. Of the storehouse, only two posts could be seen above a drift of reeds.
  Their own spring house stood beyond the deepest flow, but water had crossed the garden. Sela wanted to go immediately. Tirzah waited for Shem's inspection. She had seen enough damaged ground to distrust a house merely because its roof remained visible.
  Meanwhile injured people needed care. The man with the broken arm had been helped by neighbors; Tirzah checked what she could and arranged for experienced hands to remain with him. The boy from the cistern had lost his shoes and was more distressed about that than the flood. She gave him a pair someone had carried uphill, explaining that they could search for his own later.
  Neria organized cooking. Ham helped without taking charge of the ladle. Ruvan brought water from an upper seep nobody had needed before. Milcah corrected the stores board, crossing out what was lost and recounting what survived.
  No one had died.
  The fact did not restore a house or erase hunger. It changed the weight of every task that remained.
  At supper Noah asked Sela to tell the journey from the beginning.
  She glanced toward Tirzah, then spoke in her own order. There were things her parents would have emphasized differently. Tirzah let her finish.
  When the girl came to the warning, Mother reached across and touched her hand.
  “You left room for us to believe you,” she said. “You did not make knowing everything a condition of telling us what you knew.”
  Sela looked down, accepting praise as awkwardly as her father accepted assistance.
  Around them people passed bread, asked for blankets, called missing cups by their owners' names.
  Everybody at the count was still somebody at the table.
  

Chapter 22
The Heir to No Palace
Canaan found the necklace while helping clear Tirzah's house.
  The water had entered at the lower corner, floating the shallow dish from its shelf. The necklace lay beneath a mat near the door. He lifted it carefully, expecting a fastening broken by mud, but the metal had held.
  “This is yours?” he asked.
  Tirzah recognized her mother's gift before she recognized how quickly she had feared losing it.
  “It was my mother's. Adran gave it to me the last time I saw him.”
  Canaan placed it on a clean cloth. He was Ham and Neria's son, older than Ruvan, quiet where his brother questioned. As a child he had learned there were moments when mentioning his own name made adults uncertain where to look.
  He had heard Noah's words about him retold by cousins who understood little and enjoyed how much a few words could trouble him.
  Tirzah had never participated. Silence, she knew now, was not the same as giving him a place to speak.
  “Thank you,” she said.
  He nodded and returned to the damaged mat.
  After a while he asked, “Did your father's house decide what you would be?”
  She sat on an upturned jar. “It tried.”
  “You left it.”
  “Yes.”
  “What if the thing people think about you has no place you can leave?”
  She looked at him then.
  Canaan kept working, his hands stiff with the effort of making the question appear casual.
  Tirzah did not offer an explanation of Noah's words she did not possess. She did not tell him he was imagining the glances he had learned to expect.
  “You can tell me what they say,” she answered.
  He spoke slowly. A cousin had said that Canaan ought to fetch the water because service belonged to his future. Another had turned a refused request into a joke about the old curse. Adults had sometimes laughed because the words embarrassed them less when treated as children's foolishness.
  “I helped move the stores,” he said. “I did not know whether doing useful work would make them stop or give them more to say.”
  Tirzah felt anger rise, clean and dangerous. She waited before speaking so it would not become another burden he must soothe.
  “Nobody in this house may use your name that way.”
  He looked at her.
  “And I should have said so sooner.”
  Canaan carried the repaired shelf outside to test its feet on uneven ground. Ham came from the hall and found him adjusting the wedges.
  "For water jars?" he asked.
  "And the smaller tools. The shelf lifts out if we must move again."
  Ham crouched to examine it. Canaan kept his hands on the work, waiting for the inevitable suggestion. None came.
  "Tirzah says someone has been speaking to you badly."
  Canaan looked up. "She asked what I wanted told."
  "I know. She told me very little."
  The reassurance mattered. He had learned how easily concern could become an adult's excuse to take possession of a private humiliation.
  Ham sat on the doorstep. "I wish I had made the whole thing remain mine."
  Canaan studied him. He knew the old story, the tent, the garment, the words that followed. He also knew there were places it stopped being a story anyone wished to explain.
  "I do not know what you can change about it," he said.
  "Nor do I. But I can stop acting as though not talking has kept it from reaching you."
  The younger man set down his tool.
  Ham looked at the shelf rather than asking his son's eyes to make him feel forgiven. "When I was young, I thought exposing another man's weakness made me stronger than it. There are choices I still do not know how to live beside. That does not mean you should be made to answer for them at a water jar."
  Canaan sat on the other side of the doorway.
  "I don't want to become a story everybody tells to prove they are kind now," he said.
  Ham turned toward him. "Then tell me when we make that mistake."
  "You will be angry."
  "Perhaps. I am trying to leave some room after the anger."
  They watched the river until the light began to change. Canaan spoke of the tool board he wanted to make, its marks large enough for anyone learning to read them. Ham asked questions about how the pieces would be carried. He offered help with a crossbar and waited until it was accepted.
  When they lifted the shelf together, the first wedge fell out. Both men laughed, surprised by the relief of a problem that could actually be corrected.
  Tirzah saw them from the garden. She did not go closer to find out whether the conversation had ended well enough. Canaan had asked for a place to speak, not for an audience to certify what speaking had accomplished.
  That evening she told Noah what Canaan had permitted her to tell. He listened with a grief that did not ask her to soften the report. He spoke to the household the next morning: no child was to be humiliated for adults' pleasure, and no old saying excused cruelty in the present.
  He did not pretend his words could be recalled as though never spoken.
  Canaan did not become cheerful immediately. He helped rebuild the damaged water shelf, then asked whether he could design a better system for storing tools.
  Japheth brought him a blank board.
  They began with what his hands could make now, leaving the argument about an unseen future without permission to own the whole room.
  

Chapter 23
The Work Her Brother Left
The common shelter needed a frame for its main entrance.
  Families had begun moving from emergency coverings into structures they could inhabit through the next season. The new hall stood on high ground, open toward the fields, with a roof broad enough to shelter meals, lessons, and disputes before weather made all three more difficult.
  Japheth worked from a chair while younger hands raised the posts. Canaan fitted tool shelves along the dry inner wall. Milcah arranged store compartments where a person could inspect every portion without seeking a private key.
  Tirzah brought Kes's brace.
  Ruvan unwrapped it on the table. Twelve pegs, darkened by years, fastened a short crosspiece to its support. The wood was too small to bear the new roof alone, but sound enough to form part of the doorframe beside the inner store passage.
  “Here?” Japheth asked.
  Tirzah looked at the entrance.
  It would open onto work everyone could see. People would pass through carrying water, grain, linen. Nobody would know the pegs mattered unless someone told them. That pleased her more than a special shelf would have.
  “Here.”
  Japheth showed Ruvan how to fit the old brace to the new frame without weakening either. The work required a supporting piece; memory could not make a small timber carry more than its strength allowed.
  Sela held the new support while Canaan drove its pegs. Tirzah watched their different hands complete the joint.
  When it was finished, she touched the old wood.
  “He made twelve,” she said.
  The nearby workers waited.
  She told them about Kes learning to sweep, about shaping wood badly and then better, about the row of pegs beneath the lamp. She told them he had left. She did not end his story at its best day, nor allow its ending to erase that day.
  Ruvan glanced toward Ham, who stood beside the other post.
  Ham listened without making himself the lesson.
  Afterward Tirzah took the bronze scissors from her case and handed them to Sela. The girl turned them over, recognizing an object she had been permitted to use but never keep.
  “Mara gave these to me,” Tirzah said. “I will borrow them when I need them. You will care for them.”
  Sela looked at the box. “And that?”
  “We shall share it. Your father made too many useful spaces to leave them idle.”
  Japheth smiled from his chair.
  That evening they ate inside the unfinished hall. Rain threatened beyond the ridge, but no one rushed to make the room solemn. A child spilled broth. Neria sang while wiping it up. Ham complained that the song made his counting difficult, and she sang louder.
  Tirzah leaned against the doorframe, hearing the old household return in a place none of them had yet learned to call home.
  Kes's work held quietly beside her hand.
  

Chapter 24
The Measure Everybody Could See
Ham brought the private orchard board to the hall.
  He arrived before sunrise, when only Milcah and Canaan were working inside. He set the board beneath the new window and waited until the others came.
  “We will keep one account,” he said.
  Milcah read the figures. Ham had entered every surviving tree, the channel labor, the stored fruit, and the grain taken to feed his own workers. Beside the figures he had written what he had not known and what required checking.
  Ruvan studied the board without speaking.
  “I heard an accusation when you asked a question,” Ham told him. “I made answering it feel like deserting me.”
  Neria sat very still.
  “You were angry,” Ruvan said.
  “I am angry often. You should not have to make the river rise in order to speak while I am.”
  The absurd image made Ruvan laugh, then look down. Ham waited. He had learned at last that an apology did not require immediate relief from the person who received it.
  Noah came into the hall and stopped beside the board. He read without making the moment a public judgment of his son. The wound from the vineyard remained real; repair in a different matter did not cancel it.
  Ham turned toward him.
  “There are things I have not known how to say.”
  “We can begin here,” Noah answered.
  They went outside together. Nobody followed.
  In the weeks after, the common account acquired errors of its own. A jar broke before its contents were measured. Somebody forgot to enter grain taken for a sick household. Two people used different marks for the same size basket.
  Milcah made them correct the marks. She did not permit the public board to become proof that everyone using it had become honest forever.
  Neria arranged a rotating group to check the stores. Shem divided work according to strength and experience, adding rest where older people had been trying to disguise its necessity. Ruvan taught two younger cousins his measurements so the valley did not depend on one injured leg and one diligent hand.
  Tirzah watched these small changes with an affection too cautious to call them victory.
  At home she cleaned her mother's necklace. Sela asked whether she would wear it to the next gathering.
  “Perhaps.”
  “You needn't decide it is good or bad before putting it on.”
  Tirzah looked at her.
  “You told me that about people,” Sela added.
  That evening Tirzah wore it beneath her work dress. Adran had given her something freely at the end. She could carry that kindness without reopening the gate of his house.
  Japheth noticed the gleam at her collar and said nothing until they were alone.
  “It suits you.”
  “You always say that.”
  “Not always. There was a hat on the southern road.”
  She laughed, and he sat with her beneath the tree that had lost half its leaves and remained alive.
  

Chapter 25
The Invitation Without a Price
Ruvan asked Sela to walk beside the restored river in the first cool weather.
  He brought no measure board. She noticed immediately.
  “How will we know where we are?”
  “I hoped we might look.”
  They walked beyond the damaged orchard to a bend where new reeds were beginning to grow. The torrent had scoured the banks, exposing pale stones and the roots of trees that once stood farther from the current. It was a place that refused to pretend nothing had happened.
  Ruvan carried a small basket. Inside lay a wooden comb and a strip of repaired linen.
  Sela looked at the linen. “That is mine.”
  “The piece you cut too short before we left.”
  “Mother told you?”
  “You left it on my channel board.”
  He had sewn it to another strip, neatly enough to be useful, badly enough to show he had learned while trying.
  She laughed with a tenderness that unsettled both of them.
  “I made the comb,” he said. “The cloth was more difficult.”
  “I can see that.”
  “I wanted you to know I have not mistaken you for somebody who never cuts linen too short.”
  Sela grew quiet.
  They sat beneath a willow newly rooted near the bend. Ruvan asked about the place Sela wanted to teach. She described a room with a broad shelf, enough covered water for work, and space for learners to stand without knocking a bowl aside.
  "Who will pay for the cloth?" he asked.
  She looked at him, disappointed. She had been speaking of a future and he had brought it abruptly to an account.
  "We will need to decide," he said. "I would rather ask before I make it sound easy."
  Sela leaned back against the tree. She could hear her mother praising exactly that kind of honesty and wished praise made receiving it feel more pleasant.
  "People could contribute what they can," she said. "Nobody should be refused because they came empty handed."
  "Then the rest of us must agree to carry the difference."
  "You sound like Milcah."
  "She is usually right about portions."
  Sela laughed reluctantly. Ruvan showed her the linen again. He had repaired the short piece because making a thing useful seemed a better beginning than admiring its owner from a distance. He did not know how to promise wealth; he could promise to learn how much work a pleasant idea would require.
  They discussed the families still needing roofs. The new path south might offer more land. Staying in Ham's settlement would place them near help but also near opinions that arrived before requests. Ruvan admitted he had imagined his wife fitting naturally into the rooms he already knew.
  "I do not fit naturally everywhere," Sela answered.
  "I have noticed."
  She bumped his shoulder.
  He became serious again. "I do not want to ask you to be less difficult just because I want the next part of my life to be easy to picture."
  Sela thought of the water board. She had been attracted to his certainty first, then to the discomfort with which he learned to correct it. She did not know whether that would always be enough. She knew that no ceremony could substitute for discovering how they behaved after a mistake.
  "We should be allowed to change the plan," she said.
  "Then we will make one we can change."
  A bird landed in the willow above them. Ruvan looked up, and the sudden ordinary movement broke the intensity neither quite knew how to hold. Sela picked a loose thread from his sleeve. He let her keep it.
  For a while they were simply two young people beside a river, with a difficult future not yet required to become anybody else's example.
  He held the basket between them. “I would like to build a household with you.”
  She had imagined the question often, always with more graceful words ready. Now she found herself looking at the river.
  “You think I will live in your father's settlement.”
  “I think we should decide where there is work and water.”
  “I want a place where I can learn. Where people can come when they are hurt.”
  “Then we should make room for that.”
  She turned back. “I will disagree with you.”
  “You have established that possibility.”
  “About things that matter.”
  “Then do not stop because I become frightened.”
  For a while she studied him, seeing the boy who had disputed channels, the young man hanging above an empty compartment, the son who had placed unwelcome numbers before his father.
  “I love you,” she said. “I do not want that to mean I am allowed fewer questions.”
  “It should mean you can ask the ones you would not trust to anyone else.”
  She took the basket, then his hand.
  When they told Tirzah and Japheth, her father asked first where they meant to live. Sela laughed, surprised by the absence of ceremony.
  Tirzah understood the importance of the question. A promise lived in rooms, beside water, within the daily reach of people who would have to learn how to share it.
  “We haven't chosen,” Sela said.
  “Good,” Tirzah answered. “Then choose together.”
  Later, alone, Japheth touched Tirzah's hand.
  “Was I so young?”
  “You were very sure about your woodworking.”
  “And very uncertain about you.”
  She leaned against him. “You asked anyway.”
  Beyond the door their daughter began explaining to Ruvan why the healer's shelf must be close to the light. The sound pleased Tirzah so much that, for a moment, she could not answer when her husband spoke again.
  

Chapter 26
The Chair at the Fire
Mother wanted the chair moved nearer the door.
  Not because she wished to leave. She wanted to watch people arrive without having to turn her head each time somebody called a name. Noah carried it himself, though Shem offered, and set a folded cloth beneath one uneven leg.
  “Better?” he asked.
  She nodded.
  The illness had deepened through the weeks of rebuilding. Some mornings she ate and asked for the household news. Other mornings the effort of sitting exhausted her before she had lifted a cup. Tirzah and Milcah stayed nearby, helped by Neria and the younger women. They had made every practical change they knew. They could not make knowledge become power merely by needing it.
  Sela brought the repaired strip of linen and sat beside her.
  “He stitched this,” she said.
  Mother inspected it with serious interest. “He will improve.”
  “I think so.”
  “Let him improve at ordinary work too. Men sometimes expect affection to excuse a badly washed bowl.”
  Sela laughed. Noah, listening from the next chair, looked injured on behalf of his sex.
  “I wash bowls,” he said.
  “You have improved.”
  For a little while they were an ordinary old couple entertaining a granddaughter. Tirzah stood at the hearth and wished the moment could last without anybody noticing what she was asking of it.
  That night Mother called her closer.
  “The garden?”
  “The tree is alive. Some beds will have to begin again.”
  “You know how.”
  “I know more than I did.”
  The older woman looked toward the lamp. “That is usually the most we can say.”
  Tirzah knelt beside the chair. She remembered the first bowl offered in Noah's house, the absurd suspicion with which she received kindness that contained no calculation. She had arrived carrying a box and a history of being priced. This woman had asked whether she wanted more broth.
  “I never told you enough,” Tirzah said.
  Mother's hand moved over hers. “You lived here.”
  “There are words I ought to have said.”
  “Then say the ones you have. Do not spend the time accusing yourself of the others.”
  Tirzah spoke of the wedding cloth. The coarse stitches inside the ark. The question about her own mother's planting after the first seed rotted. The way Mother put their bowls together when Tirzah and Japheth had stopped speaking, without asking either to perform forgiveness before supper.
  “You made a house I could stay in,” she said at last.
  Mother closed her eyes, not asleep. “You helped keep it that way.”
  Japheth came in and knelt on her other side, lowering himself carefully. He held the hand that had once steadied him over his first clumsy tasks. He had been preparing speeches for days; now he asked whether she was comfortable.
  “Mostly,” she answered.
  He adjusted the blanket.
  Near dawn she asked Noah to tell her something about the field before the ark was finished. He described a day when a cart overturned and Ham insisted the road had moved. Shem denied remembering the incident until she laughed, then admitted he had been driving.
  The family gathered slowly, not because anyone called a ceremony but because staying elsewhere had become more difficult than coming in.
  Mother died after sunrise, with Noah beside her.
  Her breathing ceased quietly. No mountain shook. The pot on the fire began to boil over, and Neria rose to move it because the room still contained things that could burn.
  Tirzah saw Japheth look toward the door, as though his mother might already be outside attending to somebody overlooked.
  She put her arms around him.
  This time he did not try to stand beyond the reach of grief. He leaned into her with all the weight she could hold, and Shem came beside them to hold more.
  They buried Mother on high ground where the valley could be seen. Noah chose the place after walking there alone. He did not offer the family an explanation of why surviving the Flood had not kept this morning away.
  When they returned, her chair remained beside the door.
  No one moved it for several days.
  At the first meal they almost forgot to serve the youngest child because everyone was expecting her to notice. Milcah saw, filled the bowl, and began crying while she did it.
  Neria took the next bowl. Tirzah passed bread. Japheth poured water for Noah and sat close enough to receive silence without requiring conversation.
  The empty chair did not teach them how to continue.
  The child asking for another piece of bread gave them one place to begin.
  

Chapter 27
The Wedding Under an Unfinished Roof
Sela offered to postpone the wedding.
  Ruvan had not asked her to. The family had spoken of finishing the hall roof before the celebration, then the illness changed every date that once seemed settled. Now she looked at the empty chair and could not bear the thought of laughter sounding indifferent.
  Noah listened to her and asked whether she wanted to marry Ruvan still.
  “Yes.”
  “Does he wish to wait?”
  “He says he will do what I need.”
  “And what do you need?”
  She looked at the floor. “I don't know how to be glad here.”
  Noah's face changed with understanding.
  He did not tell her what Mother would have wanted as though the dead had become available to settle every decision. Instead he said, “She was glad when you showed her the cloth. I was there.”
  That was a memory Sela could trust.
  They married near the end of the cool season, beneath the hall's unfinished roof. Workers had left one side open for the new beams, and light entered across the tables in broad pale bands. The weather held. People who distrusted that good fortune brought coverings anyway.
  Sela wore a plain dress she had made with Neria's help. Tirzah lent her the necklace, explaining only that it had belonged to women who loved her before she was born. She did not burden the clasp with every story it carried.
  Ruvan waited beside the entrance frame. The old brace and Kes's pegs stood within reach of his hand.
  Ham came to him before the others gathered.
  For a while father and son looked at the work around them: shelters made from recovered timber, stores counted in common, people preparing food in a hall no household possessed alone.
  “I will try to be less useful with my advice,” Ham said.
  Ruvan smiled. “You may still give some.”
  “You may still refuse some.”
  The words were harder for Ham than the humor allowed him to show. Ruvan embraced him, then let him go without promising never to disagree again.
  Tirzah walked with Sela from the upper house. The girl held her mother's arm more tightly than she expected.
  “Are you frightened?” Tirzah asked.
  “Yes.”
  “About Ruvan?”
  “About everything becoming different. About keeping enough of myself. About whether love can become another kind of obedience before you notice.”
  Tirzah stopped beside the door.
  “It can,” she said.
  Sela looked at her, startled by the answer.
  “Speak when you notice,” Tirzah continued. “Listen when he notices. Do not make peace depend on pretending you have no mind.”
  “You and Father argue.”
  “We do.”
  “But you always seem certain afterward.”
  Tirzah laughed softly. “You have not always seen afterward.”
  Inside, Ruvan looked toward them and forgot the words he had prepared. Sela saw it happen. She smiled before reaching him, and his shoulders relaxed enough to let him breathe.
  The promises were spoken plainly. A life together. Room for truth. Care that would remain work after its first brightness passed. Nobody claimed the valley's next danger would find them ready merely because they had survived the last.
  Afterward Neria sang. Ham tried to keep time and failed, which delighted several grandchildren. Canaan brought the bread trays he had designed to stack without tipping. Milcah insisted everybody eat before speeches became a second kind of hunger.
  Noah sat near the open side, watching the household move around him. Sometimes he smiled. Sometimes his gaze went to the empty place beside him. Neither expression canceled the other.
  Near sunset Tirzah found Japheth outside.
  “Is the bed frame sound?” she asked.
  He looked at her, then laughed so loudly two children turned.
  “Ruvan built it.”
  “You have inspected it.”
  “Once.”
  She raised an eyebrow.
  “Twice, but the second was structural.”
  She leaned against his shoulder, remembering the lamp beside the first room they shared.
  Inside, Sela and Ruvan began helping clear the tables. Someone tried to stop them because it was their wedding. Sela answered that the bowls would not become lighter through admiration.
  Tirzah heard Mara so clearly in the sentence that her eyes filled.
  She took Japheth's hand. Their daughter was carrying voices she had never heard, and making new words of her own.
  

Chapter 28
The Country Beyond the Bend
By the next planting season, the valley could no longer hold every household where it stood.
  The river had changed its course. Some fields recovered, others remained buried under gravel. The common stores were sound but diminishing. New marriages needed houses. Children grew into workers while their elders grew less able to pretend rest was optional.
  Shem proposed a journey beyond the southern bend, following the water into land they had seen only from ridges.
  Nobody called it exile. That did not make departure easy.
  Ruvan offered to help survey. Sela asked to come and spent an evening explaining to Tirzah how the healer's work could continue while she was away. She had already spoken with Milcah and arranged who would carry the box when necessary.
  Her mother listened with the unsettling pleasure of discovering she was no longer the person who needed to make every useful arrangement.
  “You have thought about this,” she said.
  “I would like to keep thinking after I have seen the place.”
  Tirzah smiled. “A reasonable method.”
  The survey party followed the river for eight days. They passed the channel scoured by the lake, a gorge of red stone, and a basin where wind bent the grass into dark traveling shapes. At night they made fires small enough to leave little mark and recorded each place where water could be reached without a dangerous descent.
  On the fourth day of the survey, Sela and Ruvan quarreled over the crossing.
  He had measured the stream at its narrowest point. The water there was deeper but moved between sound banks. She preferred a wider shallow stretch downstream, where children could cross without being carried.
  "The ground is softer there," he said.
  "Then we should test it."
  "I have tested this."
  She looked at him. The words were not loud, but they contained a closed door.
  For a moment he saw her face become Tirzah's in one of the stories about Adran, and he resented the comparison before she had made it.
  "You think every decision I make is my father speaking," he said.
  Sela stared. "I think we should test the other ground. That is what I said."
  He looked down at the measure cord. Their marriage was not old enough for them to recognize every inherited fear when it entered a new conversation.
  They ate separately at midday. It was a poor arrangement, because each had packed part of the other's food. Dagan watched them discover this and silently moved the baskets closer.
  Later Ruvan went downstream alone, tested the shallower crossing, and found firm stone beneath its muddy edge. He returned with the information, already rehearsing a graceful way to give it.
  Sela met him beside the packs. "I should have asked what worried you about it," she said.
  He stopped. "I should have let the question remain a question."
  They inspected both places together. The narrow ford would serve loaded animals if a guiding rail was fitted. The broad one needed markers and a safer approach for children. Neither was the only useful answer.
  That night they shared food beside the fire. Ruvan told her he feared being foolish in front of her more than he feared being wrong when alone.
  "I am not making a record of how many times you disappoint me," she said.
  "I know. I seem to be keeping one myself."
  She took his hand. The marriage they had imagined as a house was becoming a practice in how to enter and leave a difficult sentence together. They were less graceful at it than the wedding promises sounded.
  At dawn they reached the southern basin. Mist lifted from the water. Above it stretched a slope of dark soil, broad enough for houses and planting, with woodland farther inland and a stony rise safe from seasonal overflow.
  Sela stood in the grass, listening. No old room spoke beneath the place yet. She could choose what to notice first.
  She bent, took a handful of soil, and asked Ruvan where water went when the rains were hard.
  He unrolled the board. This time they began the questions together.
  Sela sent no message promising paradise. When they returned, her report included insects, muddy banks, a winter wind exposed along the north slope, and good soil above the seasonal waterline.
  “There are trees,” she told Japheth. “Not enough sound straight ones where we want the houses. We should carry some fittings.”
  He listened with increasing delight. She had learned to look at land as somewhere people would have to live, not merely somewhere a story could end beautifully.
  Ruvan unrolled the map in the hall. Canaan had drawn a system for tools that could travel between settlements, and Neria proposed sending seed portions with each household rather than concentrating the next harvest's hope in one place.
  Ham looked at his son's mark beside the southern field.
  “You intend to stay there?”
  “We are considering it.”
  Sela stood beside him. “Together.”
  Neria reached for the map and moved a small stone to the path between the settlements. “Then we need stopping places. People will travel carrying children, not only measurements.”
  Her answer was generous enough to conceal the hurt until later.
  Tirzah found her outside after supper.
  “I once wanted space,” Neria said. “Inside the ark. I thought no distance could ever frighten me if it meant being outside.”
  “And now?”
  “I would like every house to fit within the reach of my voice.”
  Tirzah sat beside her. “I would too.”
  They did not use one another's honesty as a reason to forbid their children the road.
  The next morning Noah asked to see the southern map. He followed the river with a finger, then looked around the room at the descendants filling it. There were lives here that had begun only because eight people emerged from the ark. He could not walk ahead of every one.
  “Go with care,” he said. “Tell us what you find. Bring back what you learn.”
  Japheth began designing a traveling bridge frame for the shallow crossings. Shem assigned the first road work. Ham contributed pack animals and, after a struggle visible only to Neria, did not attach commands to their loan.
  Sela returned the necklace before leaving.
  “You can wear it again,” Tirzah said.
  “I know. I want you to have it now.”
  Tirzah put it in its dish. Beside it she set the third seed, still wrapped in cloth.
  For the first time in many years, she considered a place for it beyond her own garden.
  

Chapter 29
The Last Seed from Avar
Japheth asked Tirzah whether she wished to move south.
  He asked while they were washing bowls, and she disliked him briefly for choosing an ordinary task to contain a question that felt enormous.
  Then she remembered how often their life had changed beside bowls.
  “Do you?” she answered.
  He dried the one in his hands. “I would like to help build there. I would like to see Sela's house. I do not want us to leave only because we fear missing her.”
  Tirzah looked through the doorway at the garden.
  The tree had survived the drought and the torrent, losing branches on one side. New shoots appeared below the damaged limb. Its shadow remained uneven. That had become part of its beauty, though she would not have chosen the damage in order to acquire it.
  “I do not know if I can leave this again,” she said.
  Japheth set down the bowl and came beside her.
  “This would not be leaving Avar.”
  “I know.”
  “You could come back.”
  “I know that too. Knowing does not always reach the frightened part first.”
  He nodded. They stood together until she could look at the tree without imagining it already gone.
  The house had grown through their years. She knew where every shelf was fitted, which board creaked in winter, how to open the latch in darkness. She had once needed these details as proof that she was no longer confined. Now they belonged to her because she had lived, not because she must remain forever to keep freedom true.
  “I would like to visit,” she said at last. “Then decide.”
  “So would I.”
  She opened the box.
  The third seed rested in its pouch. She had carried it through the mountain danger without using it, as though the mere possibility of a last beginning could be kept alive by refusing to test it.
  She asked Noah whether a seed stored so long might grow.
  “It may not,” he answered.
  The honesty freed her from a small hope she had been forcing to wear certainty.
  “Then I shall carry other seeds too.”
  They prepared cuttings from the living tree and portions from the garden's newer plants. She collected seeds of the flowers Mara had mocked lovingly, useful herbs she no longer hid behind ornamental beds, and grain shared by Neria. None needed to descend from Avar to deserve soil.
  Before leaving she gave the upper garden's care to two young cousins who had been learning from Sela. She explained the drainage, the dry shelf, and the slow leak in the jar that should never be trusted merely because it looked sound.
  They repeated the instructions with enough accuracy to let her go.
  Japheth fitted their pack frame, then sat before his knee forced him to do so. Tirzah noticed and kissed the top of his head.
  “You are improving.”
  “I may soon become impossible to criticize.”
  “You have misunderstood the scale of my talent.”
  He laughed.
  On the last evening they ate with Noah. Shem and Milcah were there, Ham and Neria, Canaan with a new stack of traveling trays. The house contained Mother's absence without arranging every chair around it.
  Noah asked Tirzah what she intended to plant.
  She showed him the pouch.
  “And if it fails?” he asked gently.
  She considered. Years before she would have heard an accusation against hope.
  “I will be sad,” she said. “I will still have a garden to tend.”
  Noah smiled.
  In the morning she opened the door herself, leaving it ready for the cousins who would come to water the plants.
  Japheth waited on the path.
  She took his arm, and they followed the river south.
  

Chapter 30
The Lamps Along the River
The southern settlement stood above a bend broad enough to hold the evening sky.
  There were no walls. Not because its people had become incapable of fear, but because they had not yet needed to make fear into architecture. A fence protected the seed beds. Roofs sheltered the stores. The first meeting place was an awning stretched between posts, open on every side.
  Sela met her parents on the path.
  She embraced Tirzah, then tried to take Japheth's pack without making him feel old. He surrendered it with such exaggerated dignity that she laughed and forgot to be careful.
  Ruvan came behind her, carrying a child who had fallen asleep while being taken to supper. It belonged to one of the neighboring households. Sela and Ruvan had no child of their own yet, and the settlement had already learned to share its smallest demands across doors.
  Their house faced morning light. Beside the entrance stood a water shelf close to the working place. Japheth noticed it immediately.
  “Your grandmother advised that,” he said.
  “Mother told me.”
  He touched the shelf, discovering a way his mother's practical kindness had traveled beyond her lifetime without requiring anybody to call it a miracle.
  The healer's corner contained clean cloth, covered containers, and a wide window. Sela had made a separate place for lessons, insisting that people learn useful work before injury made every instruction urgent. The wooden box stood open on a bench. Its hinge moved smoothly.
  “Ruvan repaired it,” she said.
  Tirzah inspected the work. “He has improved.”
  Sela smiled, hearing the voice of another woman within the words.
  They stayed through the first planting. Japheth helped fit a bridge across the shallow crossing, guiding work from a chair when his knee required it. Tirzah spent mornings in the garden and afternoons teaching whoever wished to learn. She told stories when people asked, choosing truth over the comfort of making every old danger more foolish than the new ones.
  One evening a girl asked about Leth.
  Tirzah described the serving girl's burned fingers, the breakfast bowl, the sliver of bronze. She said plainly that Leth was not one of the eight aboard the ark. The girl's brave help did not purchase an ending Tirzah could rewrite.
  “Then why tell it?” the child asked.
  “Because I can open a door today partly because she opened one for me.”
  The girl considered this, then went to help someone untangle a carrying strap. Tirzah watched her go without claiming the small act proved the world had finally become kind.
  They planted the third seed where afternoon shade would protect its first shoots if any came.
  Sela helped loosen the soil. Ruvan carried water. Japheth sat nearby with the same attentive silence he had offered when the first seed failed. Tirzah put the old seed into the hollow, covered it, and planted the newer ones in neighboring beds.
  She did not mark its place with a shrine. Three stones were enough.
  Weeks passed.
  One of the cuttings took root. Grain emerged in green lines. Flowers began as small leaves no grand story would have paused to admire. The old seed remained beneath the soil, and Tirzah let it remain without digging every morning for an answer.
  She and Japheth decided to spend part of each year in the south and return when the upper household needed them. Neither house had to become the only place love could exist.
  Before their first return journey, the settlements arranged a gathering beside the new bridge. Noah did not make the long walk; Shem brought his greeting and Milcah brought a store list nobody escaped merely because the day was festive. Ham and Neria arrived with fruit from the surviving orchard. Canaan brought tools that could be lent from one household to another, each entered openly on his traveling board.
  At dusk people set lamps along the path to the crossing so late travelers could find their footing.
  Tirzah stood beside Japheth and watched the lights extend around the river bend. Beyond them stretched dark land, still unknown. She thought of the western quarter burning beneath her father's terrace. Once light had shown her what powerful men could take. Here, each flame marked a place somebody had prepared for a stranger's foot.
  “Would you choose this?” Japheth asked.
  She turned toward him. The question carried their wedding field, the narrow sleeping place, the spring house, and every hour in which choosing had felt less like a promise than an effort to speak honestly.
  “I would choose you here,” she said. “And I would ask you to come back with me when I need the other place.”
  He smiled. “Then ask.”
  She rested her head against his.
  Behind them Sela called everyone to eat. Ruvan lifted a bench while Ham explained a better way to lift it, then caught himself and asked whether help was wanted. Neria laughed. Canaan brought the bread. Milcah moved a bowl nearer a child who could not reach it.
  Tirzah looked across the gathering and saw no perfect household. She saw people who had learned some things painfully and would still have to learn others. She saw room at the table and more land beyond it than anyone could oversee.
  Later, after the meal, Sela brought her to the seed bed.
  Between the three stones stood a small folded shoot.
  Tirzah crouched, surprised by the strength of her own feeling. The leaves were too young to tell her everything. She would not turn their appearance into proof that love could preserve whatever it touched.
  Sela knelt beside her.
  “Do you think it is the old one?”
  “I think we should let it grow.”
  Japheth came with water, walking slowly so none spilled. Ruvan moved the lamp closer. Its light reached their hands, the damp earth, and the tender leaves opening without knowledge of Avar.
  Tirzah spoke of her mother, then spoke Kes's name. Mara. Leth. Mother, who had made room before Tirzah knew how to trust it. Adran, remembered without being excused. She gave Sela the names again, along with what kindness and harm she knew belonged to each.
  When she finished, her daughter took the water and poured carefully around the shoot.
  Beyond the garden, travelers crossed the bridge beneath the lamps. Footsteps moved into land Tirzah would never wholly know, carrying children, tools, stories, and seed.
  She could not promise where all those roads would end.
  She rose beside Japheth and went to open the door for the people still arriving.

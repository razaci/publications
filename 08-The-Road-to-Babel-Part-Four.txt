CHAPTER 1
The House Beside the Narrow Water
The day Aviel decided to leave the hills, his son brought home a boat that could not float.
  It was a small boat, built from a split branch and three scraps taken without permission from Aviel’s workbench. Radan had joined them with pegs driven too close to the edges. The wood had cracked. Nevertheless, he carried it with both hands, the way a man might carry something entrusted to him by a king.
  “It needs a wider river,” he said.
  Aviel turned the little hull beneath the light. “It needs a sound bottom.”
  “You say that about everything.”
  “Water says it first.”
  From the doorway, Hessa watched her husband hide a smile. Their daughter Ishri stood behind her, twisting a strand of red wool around one finger. The strand came from a garment Hessa had been repairing for so long that more of it belonged to the repairs than to the original cloth.
  Beyond the house, neighbors were arguing beside the channel. Another household had divided its land. Another fence had crossed the place where everyone once walked. Aviel knew each voice. He had mended their carrying poles, shaped their door fittings, and built shallow boats for the swollen stream. He also knew there was no work here for all the boys becoming men.
  Radan followed his gaze.
  “Barek says there is a plain where the rivers have room.”
  Hessa’s brother had returned two nights earlier with dust in his hair and enough news to keep the lamps burning. Far below the hills, in Shinar, households had gathered. There were grain stores, kilns, waterways broad enough for loaded boats. A city was taking shape. A man who could build would find work there.
  Aviel had listened without promising anything. Then he had gone out before dawn and stood beside the channel until the sky paled.
  Now he put his son’s boat on the bench.
  “We will make this sound,” he said.
  Radan’s face fell. He had wanted a journey and received another lesson.
  “Then we will find it a wider river.”
  Hessa looked away first. Through the open door she could see the notch in the wall where Ishri’s height had been marked each year. She had expected to grow old measuring children here. Departure had seemed like something that happened to other women.
  Ishri unwound the wool from her finger.
  “Does the city have a place for songs?”
  “People live there,” Hessa said. “So I expect it does.”
  Outside, the neighbors’ argument became louder. Inside, Aviel removed a cracked peg and began again.
CHAPTER 2
What the Road Would Allow
Barek arrived to help them pack and immediately declared that they were taking too much.
  He said this while carrying an extra blanket, a jar of honey, and a knife he claimed to have lost three seasons before. Hessa recognized all three as things he intended to keep for himself.
  “You may advise us when you have room in your hands,” she told him.
  He set the honey beside the door with injured dignity.
  The household’s belongings looked smaller when laid outside. A loom frame. Two stools. Cooking pots blackened at the base. The long measuring cord Aviel used for boats. A mat worn smooth where Hessa slept. Radan’s repaired little hull, now wrapped in cloth to protect it from a journey it had demanded without being ready to survive.
  Ishri carried out a stone.
  “No,” said Barek.
  “It is mine.”
  “So is the hill. Shall we tie that behind the cart?”
  The stone had no use anyone could name. Ishri had found it beside the spring after a storm. It was pale beneath its ordinary dust, and its shape fitted her palm. Hessa watched the girl close her fingers around it.
  “There is room for one stone.”
  Barek spread his arms. “Then I withdraw all responsibility for the weight of this expedition.”
  He carried the loom himself.
  That afternoon their oldest neighbor came to say farewell. His father had heard Noah speak about the waters, and he spoke now as if memory were a jar that must not be dropped in transit.
  “You are going toward rivers,” he told Radan. “Remember that water does not become harmless because we put names on it.”
  Radan promised. He promised quickly, in the way of someone already imagining the boats he would build.
  Hessa offered the neighbor bread. Aviel asked after his sore foot. Neither tried to hurry the leave-taking.
  At dusk Ishri walked through the empty rooms. She sang one verse of the song her mother used when sorting seed. It sounded different without the bedding and jars. The wall returned her voice more clearly than it had ever done when the house was full.
  She stopped before the last verse.
  “What is it?” Hessa asked.
  “If I finish, it will be over.”
  Her mother sat on the floor beside her.
  They remained there while Barek argued with the cart outside. A wheel had settled crookedly in its fitting. Radan laughed. Aviel told him to bring the wedge.
  At last Hessa began the last verse herself. Ishri joined on the second line.
  The house held their voices for another little while.
CHAPTER 3
The Ford at Evening
On the fourth evening, the road disappeared beneath water.
  There had been a ford here when Barek traveled north. He pointed toward a dark line below the current as if the river might be persuaded to remember it.
  “It was only this deep.”
  He held his hand below his knee.
  “It is not,” Aviel said.
  Across the river, other carts waited. Someone had set fires on the bank. Smoke hung low, unable to rise through the damp air. A child was crying because a goat had slipped its tether. The goat, watching from a rock, appeared to consider this a useful improvement in its circumstances.
  Barek wanted to cross before dark.
  Aviel unfastened his measuring cord.
  Radan stepped into the river before his father could speak. The first footing held. The second did not. Water struck above his knees, and the gravel moved beneath his heel.
  Aviel caught his sleeve and pulled him back so sharply that he fell on the bank.
  For a moment Radan looked younger than he had looked at home.
  “You could have told me,” he said.
  “I am telling you.”
  Aviel’s hands shook as he knotted the cord around a tree. He was angry enough to make the knot twice. Then he took a pole and sounded the shallows while another traveler held the line.
  They found a place farther upstream, where the current spread over a broad shelf. It took two hours to unload the cart, carry the grain across in smaller portions, and move the axle without losing it to the river. Barek, soaked to the waist, stopped giving advice and began doing exactly what he was asked.
  Halfway through the crossing, Ishri’s stone slipped from her hand.
  She reached down. Hessa seized her wrist.
  “Let it go.”
  The stone vanished without a splash anyone could distinguish from the current.
  On the far bank, Ishri stood staring at her empty palm.
  Hessa wanted to tell her it was only a stone. Instead she wrapped the girl’s fingers in the dry end of her garment.
  That night Radan sat beside his father while Aviel dried the measuring cord near the fire.
  “I thought I could see the bottom.”
  “Sometimes you can.”
  “And sometimes?”
  Aviel turned the wet cord so it would not scorch.
  “Sometimes you see the sky lying on top of it.”
  Across the camp, Ishri watched the river catch the firelight. By morning it would look almost beautiful.
CHAPTER 4
The First Brick
The hills gave way slowly. Then one morning they were gone.
  Ishri woke beneath an enormous sky. Grass stretched away from the road, broken by grazing animals and the distant shapes of moving households. The world looked unfinished. She searched for a high place that might tell her where she stood and found none.
  Barek pointed south.
  “Shinar.”
  He said it with satisfaction, as if naming the plain completed the hardest part of the journey.
  By afternoon they passed their first kiln. Heat breathed from a low opening in the clay. Men stood back while another drew out fired bricks with a long implement. Near the water, women pressed clay into molds. Children carried straw and argued about whose footprints would remain in the drying yard.
  Radan crouched beside a stack.
  “Stone made by hands.”
  The kiln worker glanced at him. “Clay made less obedient to rain.”
  He handed Radan a brick that was still warm. The boy nearly dropped it, then held it against his palms as if it had a pulse.
  The worker asked where they were going.
  “To the city,” Barek said.
  “Which work?”
  “Boats,” Aviel answered.
  The man looked at Aviel’s tools. “Then go to the landing below the stores. Ask who needs a repair before you ask who needs a new vessel. You will eat sooner.”
  Hessa remembered that kindness. It cost the man little, and it changed the direction of their first days.
  Farther on, smoke rose above a spread of roofs. At the center, a broad raised foundation caught the late sunlight. Workers moved along its edge, small against the width of the work.
  Radan climbed onto the cart for a better view.
  Barek caught the wheel before it shifted beneath him.
  “There,” the boy whispered.
  He was looking at more than the foundation. He saw work large enough to make his father’s narrow bench seem like a thing he had already outgrown.
  Aviel looked toward the landing. Hessa looked for the places where women were carrying water. Ishri looked for trees.
  They entered the city along the same road, each arriving somewhere different.
  At the last turn, a man carrying an empty basket walked past them, arguing with another about a missing measure of grain.
  Nobody announced their arrival.
  The city was busy living without them. Tomorrow they would ask it to make room.
CHAPTER 5
A Roof Borrowed from the Rain
Their first roof in the city belonged to a man who insisted it was sound.
  Hessa looked up at the daylight showing through it.
  “In dry weather,” she said.
  The man answered that dry weather was common.
  Barek asked whether this argument also reduced the price. It did not. They took the room because the next room cost more and the children were asleep against the cart.
  That evening Aviel went to the landing. He returned with a split carrying frame, a promise of grain when it was repaired, and clay up to his ankles. It was less than Radan had expected of the city.
  The boy went outside.
  At the end of the lane, a woman was setting jars beneath a cloth awning. Ruma, she called herself. Her son Tovi had clay on his elbows and a disagreeable expression that improved when Radan asked about the kiln.
  “Not that one,” Tovi said, pointing. “That one eats wood and gives back disappointments.”
  Ruma told him that disappointments did not stack themselves.
  He went back to work.
  Ishri helped carry a jar. She held it too far from her body and trembled under its weight. Ruma moved her hands without scolding.
  “Closer. Let your bones do their share.”
  When Ishri repeated the words to Hessa later, she imitated the woman’s voice so well that Hessa laughed for the first time since the ford.
  In the next lane stood a fruit tree, smaller than the ones they had left in the hills. Its owner was an older woman named Tirzah. She caught Ishri studying the leaves and offered her a little water in a cup.
  “Does it bear fruit?” the girl asked.
  “When it remembers.”
  A gray-bearded man behind Tirzah said the tree remembered more faithfully than he did.
  “Japheth forgets what does not suit him,” Tirzah explained.
  Ishri understood the joke because her own father did much the same.
  That night, rain came through the borrowed roof. Barek moved the sleeping mats. Aviel put a jar beneath the worst leak. Hessa stood beside the doorway and smelled wet clay, smoke, and the river beyond the houses.
  Ishri began the seed song.
  From outside came Tovi’s voice, telling someone to cover the unfired pots. Then a woman shouted for a cloth. Then a child laughed.
  The song fitted between the noises. It did not have to fill the city to belong there.
CHAPTER 6
The Work That Grew Taller
Aviel found work. Then the work found more work.
  A repaired frame led to a boat whose ribs had loosened. The boat led to a request for a wider hull. Before the second season ended, he had a bench beneath an awning near the landing and three men waiting for things he had promised to finish yesterday.
  Hessa’s loom stood against the wall of a better room. Ishri’s height was marked beside its door. They had lived there long enough for the first mark to seem small.
  Radan worked with his father until a tower foreman came to borrow a measuring cord.
  The man watched the boy fit a curved rib to a boat’s side.
  “You have an eye.”
  Radan tried not to show how much the words pleased him.
  “They need eyes above the foundation,” the foreman continued. “And hands that can follow them.”
  Aviel asked what work was offered. The man spoke of timber fittings, load frames, platforms. He spoke of a city whose name would endure. Radan listened to the second part as though it guaranteed the first.
  That evening Hessa found him measuring the length of his own arm.
  “What are you doing?”
  “Learning my reach.”
  She sat beside him. “For what?”
  “The tower.”
  He said it softly, waiting for her to oppose him. She did not. Instead she asked when he would begin, how he would be paid, and who would teach him to work above the ground.
  He had answers to two questions. He had not thought to ask the third.
  Aviel consented after speaking with the foreman again. He had no wish to make his son inherit a bench merely because he had made a living at it. Yet he disliked the speed with which Radan left the next morning.
  At the workyard, a builder named Eshar showed the boy where to stand while a load was raised. He moved Radan back a pace when the rope tightened.
  “If that fitting parts,” Eshar said, “you will not have time to improve your position.”
  Radan remembered the ford. He stepped back another pace.
  Above them, men called measurements along the platform. The same words passed from hand to hand until timber moved exactly where it was wanted. Radan felt the pleasure of it: many people doing one difficult thing, each trusting the others to understand.
  That evening he returned with dust on his shoulders and a splinter in his thumb.
  He showed Ishri the splinter as if it were the first token of a great inheritance.
CHAPTER 7
The City That Wanted a Name
The speech took place beside the tower foundation at noon.
  Hessa had gone to collect a measure of grain. She remained because the crowd pressed around her and because Radan stood near the front, his face lifted.
  Vadan spoke from a place where everyone could see him. He did not begin with threats. He spoke of winters when households had eaten separately and starved separately. He spoke of missing children, broken roads, and the difficulty of keeping promises to people who lived too far away to hear them.
  Hessa knew those difficulties. So did most of the listeners. When he spoke of a common store, people nodded.
  When he spoke of a city, they cheered.
  Then he turned toward the rising work.
  “Let those who come after us find our name here.”
  Radan cheered with the others. Hessa looked beyond the platform toward the roofs. From here she could not see their house. She wondered whether the people who came after them might first want clean water and a roof that did not leak.
  That evening she asked Radan what the tower was for.
  He answered immediately. “So we will not be scattered.”
  “Do you think your father will leave because the roof is low?”
  “That is not what it means.”
  “Then tell me.”
  He grew impatient. He spoke of strength, of a name no one could forget, of reaching so high that travelers would find them from a great distance.
  Aviel kept sharpening his blade.
  Ishri watched the metal catch the lamplight. She knew the sound it made against the stone. She knew when her father sharpened carefully and when he sharpened because he wished to postpone speaking.
  At last he put the blade down.
  “A traveler who can see a tower may still be hungry when he reaches it.”
  “There will be stores.”
  “Then keep the stores useful.”
  Radan looked at his mother. He wanted someone to tell him that the thing he loved could remain good simply because he loved it.
  Hessa reached for his hand. He let her take it, though reluctantly.
  “You may build,” she said. “But ask what each new height is asking of the people below.”
  Outside, workers were returning through the lanes. Someone sang a loud work song. Another voice joined. Radan’s fingers tapped the rhythm against his mother’s palm.
  He was already listening beyond the room.
CHAPTER 8
The Red Cloth Above the Yard
Hessa made Radan a red cloth to tie around his head.
  It kept the sweat from his eyes. It also allowed Ishri to find him when she stood below the tower and searched the moving figures above.
  “There,” she would say. “There he is.”
  The tower grew through seasons. Its shadow reached places where people had once sat in sunlight. New ramps curved around its sides. Bitumen darkened the joints between fired bricks. Along the approaches, the clay smelled different beneath the heat of the day.
  Radan learned to make fittings. He learned the patience of checking a peg after someone else had checked it. He also learned that men who hurried were sometimes praised more loudly than men who prevented a thing from going wrong.
  On a windy afternoon, Barek joined his crew. Hessa’s brother had found work at several trades and argued with someone at each. He declared timber work a sensible profession because wood listened better than overseers.
  Radan liked having him there.
  Near the end of the day, a load caught against the platform. A man below called for it to be lowered. A man above called for more pull. Both believed he could see the problem clearly.
  Radan took a step toward the fitting.
  Barek caught the red cloth at the back of his head.
  “Stand.”
  The load shifted. A corner struck the rail hard enough to split it. Pieces fell into the yard. Nobody was beneath them.
  The crew stood silent until the load was secured.
  Afterward an overseer called the delay unnecessary. Barek asked whether the overseer preferred a broken railing above his own head. The argument cost them the last portion of their day’s pay.
  Radan said nothing until they were walking home.
  “You should have spoken differently.”
  “Yes,” Barek said. “I should have spoken before they raised it.”
  At home, Hessa washed the red cloth. She found a small tear where her brother had gripped it.
  “How did this happen?”
  Radan told her.
  She folded the damp cloth over her knee and looked at Barek.
  He began explaining the lost pay.
  “Be quiet,” she said.
  He obeyed, surprised.
  Then she rose and put her arms around him.
  Ishri watched from the doorway. The next morning she found Radan sooner than usual. Hessa had mended the tear with a strip of brighter wool.
CHAPTER 9
The Place Left at Supper
A worker named Soran died while Radan was eating his midday bread.
  Radan heard the shout first. He put the bread down, turned, and saw men moving toward the foot of a temporary stair. The stair remained standing. The tower remained standing. All the things that had seemed strong a moment earlier were still where they had been.
  Soran was not.
  The man had been carrying a small load. There was no dramatic weight to blame, no storm, no enemy at the gate. One footing had come loose. A rail had failed to hold him.
  Radan reached the yard after others had covered his face.
  He knew Soran’s laugh better than he knew the names of Soran’s children. The man had taught him a knot, borrowed a knife, and promised to return a peg that Radan had forgotten lending.
  Now someone was asking whose household should be told.
  Radan did not know.
  He hated that ignorance more than anything else in the yard.
  Barek went with the men who carried the body home. Radan remained until work ended, unable to bear either returning to the platform or leaving without permission. When the overseer dismissed the crew, he felt a shameful little relief.
  At supper he could not swallow.
  Hessa asked whether he was ill. Aviel looked at the boy’s untouched bowl and waited.
  Radan told them in pieces. He began with the loose footing. He spoke of the height. Only after these things did he say the man’s name.
  Ishri stopped chewing.
  “Was he old?”
  “No.”
  She considered this. “Did he have a song?”
  Radan almost snapped at her. Then he remembered Soran singing badly while dressing a timber, insisting that a poor voice kept the wood humble.
  “Yes,” he said.
  He tried to recall the tune. He could remember where the man had laughed between lines. He could not remember the words.
  Later Aviel took the red cloth from the bench and placed it beside Radan’s sleeping mat.
  “You can leave that work.”
  Radan stared into the darkness.
  “If I leave, someone else will stand there.”
  “Then tell the men about the stair.”
  “They know.”
  “Tell the men who have the power to repair it.”
  Radan said he would. The promise was easier than the morning would be.
  In another house, Soran’s bowl had been washed and put away. The city continued to need supper. Radan lay awake thinking how cruel that ordinary need could feel.
CHAPTER 10
A Mark That Meant Tomorrow
Hessa’s loom was paid for in work she had not yet finished.
  At first the arrangement had seemed generous. A household needed cloth. Hessa needed a sound frame. The stores provided the frame, and future lengths of woven cloth would answer its cost.
  Then grain grew dearer. Fuel for the kilns was carried from farther away. The count changed. A keeper explained the changes patiently, as though patience proved them just.
  Hessa set the account board on the table.
  “I have made this length already.”
  “It has been entered.”
  “Then why is it here again?”
  The keeper pointed to a mark she did not recognize.
  Barek, who recognized even less, offered to shout. Hessa sent him outside.
  She returned the next day with Sela, Tirzah’s daughter. Sela read the marks slowly and asked the keeper to bring the earlier board. He said it was stored elsewhere.
  “Then we will wait.”
  They waited until the man fetched it.
  The mistake was small. It was corrected reluctantly. Yet Hessa left troubled, because the mistake had required more knowledge to undo than most households possessed.
  Sela walked with her as far as the lane.
  “Keep a copy.”
  “I cannot read all of it.”
  “Learn the marks for what you have supplied. I will show you.”
  That evening Hessa began. Ishri sat beside her and scratched the shapes in dust. Aviel made them a smooth board. Radan watched, then offered to help count the completed cloth.
  His mother accepted without speaking of the tower.
  They worked until the lamp shortened. A few marks began to belong to Hessa instead of merely confronting her.
  During the following seasons, the family spoke often of leaving. Each time another obligation stood in the road. A boat unfinished. Cloth promised. A portion of grain owed. Radan’s crew needed one more platform secured before anyone could be spared.
  Barek brought home stories of households turned back at the gate because their work had not been properly answered.
  Ishri thought of the stone she had lost at the ford. She had imagined then that the hardest things to leave were the things one loved.
  Now Hessa watched a keeper draw a new mark beside their names.
  “What does that mean?” she asked.
  “Tomorrow,” he said.
  But it meant another week of cloth. Another week became another season.
CHAPTER 11
The Morning They Understood
On the last morning when everyone in the household understood the same words, Hessa complained about a bowl.
  Someone had chipped its rim. Everyone denied responsibility. Barek examined it and said the bowl had always looked untrustworthy.
  “You are the untrustworthy thing at this table,” Hessa told him.
  Ishri laughed. Aviel smiled into his food. Radan wound the red cloth around his forehead and asked whether there would be bread when he returned.
  “If you return before Barek,” said his mother.
  Barek took another piece to defend himself against this injustice.
  Nothing in the room appeared to be ending.
  Outside, a basket seller argued over the price of reeds. At the landing, Aviel’s unfinished boat rested on supports. Men called for clay near the tower. The shared language moved through the city with the ordinary ease of water through an open channel.
  Radan kissed his mother’s cheek without noticing that he had done it. Then he and Barek left together.
  Aviel took Ishri to the landing. She had begun helping measure boards. She could keep a cord straight better than men who claimed to have been born knowing the task.
  “Hold this end,” her father said.
  She did.
  Near midmorning, a grain carrier asked Aviel to move a timber from the path. Aviel answered that he would move it when the cord had been marked.
  The carrier frowned.
  He repeated himself.
  Aviel heard every sound clearly. He could see the man waiting for an answer. Yet the sounds no longer reached anything he knew.
  For a moment he believed he had become ill.
  Then Ishri looked up.
  “Father, what is he saying?”
  Aviel understood his daughter.
  The carrier spoke more loudly. Someone at the next boat shouted. Across the landing, two men who had been joking a moment earlier began pointing at each other with frightened hands.
  Aviel looked toward the city.
  He did not hear thunder. He saw no cloud breaking above the tower. The bricks shone in the same sunlight. The Lord had confounded the people’s language, and the first thing Aviel understood of it was that a man asking him to move a piece of wood now looked at him as if he had refused.
  Ishri caught his sleeve.
  “Mother,” she said.
  They left the cord where it lay.
CHAPTER 12
A Voice Inside the Wrong Words
Hessa heard them coming before they entered the house.
  She recognized Aviel’s tread and Ishri’s quick breathing. She went to the doorway with a question already forming. The girl ran into her arms.
  Hessa held her tightly.
  “What happened?”
  Ishri answered. Her face was full of relief. Then the relief disappeared.
  Hessa had not understood.
  Aviel spoke behind the girl. His voice had the same low weariness it carried at the end of a long working day. Hessa knew the voice intimately. She knew when it concealed pain and when it concealed amusement.
  The words inside it were gone.
  She looked from husband to daughter.
  “Again,” she said.
  They tried.
  Ishri began to cry. Hessa tried to calm her, but the familiar phrase she used when a dream frightened the girl met no recognition. The girl pressed her hands to her mother’s cheeks, as though touch might restore what sound had lost.
  Aviel went still.
  He had been thinking of instructions: fetch the boy, find Barek, stay in the house. He could not give any of them to his wife. He could not even tell her he would return.
  Outside, the lanes were filling. People called names. Some names still found the right faces. Other words produced only bewilderment. A man was beating on a door that nobody would open, unable to explain that his child had been inside before morning.
  Hessa pulled her family farther into the room and closed the door.
  For several breaths they stood facing one another.
  Then she took Aviel’s hand and put it against the wall where their children’s heights were marked.
  She touched Radan’s mark.
  Aviel nodded at once.
  She took the red scrap left from mending his headcloth. She pointed toward the tower. Again Aviel nodded.
  Ishri spoke urgently to her father. Hessa watched him shake his head. The girl seized his arm. He loosened her fingers, one by one, then placed her hand in Hessa’s.
  Hessa understood what he meant to do.
  She wanted to tell him that they should all go. She wanted to tell him the streets might become dangerous. Instead she fetched his long measuring cord and pushed it into his hands.
  He bent his forehead against hers.
  Then he stepped outside.
  The door closed gently. Hessa had never heard a gentle sound carry so much fear.
CHAPTER 13
Above the Broken Instructions
Radan thought the foreman had changed his command.
  He was standing two levels above the main yard, helping guide a load of timber into place. Barek held the near rope. Three other men steadied the far end. They had done the work together for weeks.
  The foreman called from below.
  Radan looked down. The man pointed toward the inner platform. Radan asked whether he meant the fitting there.
  The foreman shouted again.
  Radan understood Barek when his uncle told him to keep his hands clear. He understood one of the men beside him. The other two spoke words that sounded strange enough to be a joke, until he saw that they were frightened.
  The load began turning.
  Barek called for slack. One man pulled harder. Another let go too much. Timber pressed against the rail, and a peg bent in its hole.
  Radan remembered Soran.
  He dropped to his knees and jammed a spare wedge beneath the edge nearest him. Barek saw and drove another beside it. The wood settled. A groan passed through the frame.
  For several moments nobody dared move.
  Below, the foreman was still shouting. Radan wanted him to be quiet. He wanted one clear sentence to reach everyone at once. He had never understood how much safety lived inside such a sentence.
  Barek pointed toward the far rope, then closed his hand. The nearest worker understood and held it. Radan touched the fitting he meant to free, raised one finger, and waited until the men were looking.
  They lowered the timber in small movements. Each movement had to be shown before it could be made.
  When the load rested, Barek began leading them toward the stair.
  At the first turn they found men coming up. A man above wanted to come down. Neither group could explain what it had seen. Bodies pressed in the narrow passage. One frightened worker shoved another toward the edge.
  Radan planted both feet and pushed his open palms between them.
  Barek grasped the upper man’s wrist. He pointed down, then backed aside. Slowly the passage cleared.
  They reached a lower platform above the yard. From there Radan could see people gathering around a damaged hoist. A young woman stood beside a gray-bearded man, using a rope to show something she could not explain to him.
  Radan searched the crowd for his father.
  His own red cloth hung from a nail where he had placed it at midday.
  He went back to fetch it.
  When he turned again, Barek was gone.
CHAPTER 14
The Search Along the Wall
Aviel reached the tower and found no one who could answer him.
  He spoke Radan’s name. He held his hand above his own head to show a tall young man. He pointed to the red cloth scrap Hessa had given him. An exhausted worker looked at the scrap and shook his head.
  Aviel tried another man.
  The tower’s bulk rose above him, unchanged by the disorder beneath it. Some loads had been left where they hung. Men were securing them without a shared command. Others sat on the lowest steps, staring at their hands.
  A woman Aviel recognized as Sela passed carrying boards marked with accounts. He called to her.
  She understood.
  The relief struck him so strongly that he almost forgot his question.
  “Radan,” he said. “My son. Red cloth. Barek is with him.”
  Sela looked toward the workyard, then at the crowd.
  “I saw a red cloth above the lower stairs. Before they cleared the hoist.”
  “How long?”
  “I do not know.”
  She did not pretend to know more. Aviel clung to what she had actually seen. He thanked her and moved toward the stairs.
  An overseer blocked him. Aviel tried to explain. The man pointed to the platforms and shook his head. Behind him, workers were bringing a rope down. Aviel realized the man might be protecting him rather than refusing him.
  He waited until the rope was clear. Then he tried again.
  The overseer spread both hands toward the men descending. Aviel understood: wait for them.
  He waited through the heat of the afternoon.
  He watched each face. He called his son’s name until his throat hurt. Twice he saw red and went forward; twice another young man looked at him with a stranger’s confusion.
  At last Barek appeared beyond the side yard.
  Aviel ran to him.
  Barek began speaking before they met. He was angry, frightened, and almost crying. Aviel could not understand any of it.
  He caught Barek’s shoulders and held up the red scrap.
  Barek’s face changed.
  He looked behind him.
  Then he looked at Aviel.
  They had both come searching for the same boy. The small understanding did nothing to tell them where he had gone.
  Barek pointed back toward the stairs. Aviel pointed toward home.
  They stood between the two directions as the first long shadows reached the yard.
CHAPTER 15
The Boy Beneath the Red Cloth
Radan left the tower by a stair he had not used before.
  He had looked for Barek on the lower platforms and found a worker with a bleeding hand. He helped the man bind it. Then another worker pointed toward a passage. Radan followed because he thought the gesture meant that Barek had gone that way.
  By the time he reached the ground, the main yard was behind him.
  The city had become unfamiliar without changing its streets. At a corner, people who knew him spoke words he could not enter. At another, a stranger understood his question and directed him toward home.
  He ran.
  He found the house empty.
  For a while he remained in the doorway, too frightened to search and too frightened to stay. Hessa’s loom stood against the wall. A bowl sat beside a folded cloth. Ishri’s latest height mark was there, exactly where it had been that morning.
  All these things seemed to be waiting for people who might never come back.
  He went to the landing.
  The unfinished boat still rested on its supports. His father’s cord was missing. He saw no sign of a struggle, but the absence felt like evidence of something he could not bear to imagine.
  At last he returned to the lane near Ruma’s pots.
  There he found Hessa.
  She seized him so hard that his shoulder struck the doorway. He heard her voice, understood her words, and began crying before he could answer.
  Ishri came from behind her mother.
  “Where is Father?” Radan asked.
  The girl stared at him.
  He asked again.
  Hessa touched his cheek and shook her head. She spoke to Ishri, then stopped when the girl’s eyes filled.
  Radan understood his mother. He could not understand his sister.
  Tirzah stood nearby. She spoke to him, asking whether he had been hurt. He shook his head. Ishri watched their easy exchange with a loneliness he had never seen on her face.
  He reached for her hand.
  She jerked it away. Then she caught his fingers again, angry that he had frightened her, angry that he had come back carrying a voice she could no longer recognize.
  Hessa pushed the red scrap into his palm. She pointed toward the tower, then touched her husband’s place on the bench.
  Radan understood.
  His father was still looking for him.
  Outside, the streets were darkening. Radan took the red cloth from his forehead and tied it high on a pole beside the doorway.
  Then he stood beneath it where anyone coming down the lane could see him.
CHAPTER 16
A Doorway Full of Hands
Barek saw the red cloth first.
  He struck Aviel’s arm and pointed. Aviel turned into the lane at a run. Radan came toward him, then stopped, suddenly uncertain of what would happen when he spoke.
  Aviel caught him against his chest.
  The boy was taller than his father now. He smelled of dust, sweat, and heated timber. Aviel pressed one hand to the back of his head and held him as if he were still small enough to carry across a ford.
  Radan said that he had looked everywhere.
  Aviel did not understand.
  Radan tried to explain the stair, the injured man, the empty house. Each explanation moved his father’s face from relief toward helplessness.
  At last he stopped.
  Barek put a hand between them. He spoke to Radan, and the boy answered. Aviel watched their words find each other while his own son remained beyond him.
  He had imagined that finding the boy would end the day’s terror.
  Hessa came into the doorway with Ishri. The girl ran to Aviel and asked where he had been. He answered. Hessa watched their voices join and felt the same distance opening from the other side.
  For a little while nobody moved into the house.
  Then Tirzah came forward carrying a bowl.
  Radan understood when she told him to sit down. She touched Aviel’s arm and pointed toward the mat. He understood the gesture. She handed Hessa the bowl and motioned for Barek to fetch another.
  Food became the next thing they all knew how to do.
  They sat in the borrowed room near the jars. Ruma and Tovi were away helping people elsewhere in the city. Tirzah moved between bowls, speaking where she could and showing where she could not.
  Ishri broke her bread and gave half to Radan.
  He offered it back, believing she wanted him to divide it more evenly. She shook her head and pushed it toward him. Then she put a hand on her chest and pointed at the bread.
  A gift.
  He took it.
  The misunderstanding was small. Hessa knew there would be thousands like it. A request for water. A warning about a hot pot. A pain that could be pointed to and a grief that could not.
  Aviel bent beside his son and touched his forehead to Radan’s.
  He could not tell him what the search had cost. He could make the boy feel that he had been found.
  Later they walked home together. The red cloth came down from the pole. Radan carried it in his hand so that, when they crossed a dark lane, Ishri could keep hold of the other end.
CHAPTER 17
The Account Without a Voice
In the days that followed, the tower’s work ceased.
  Some men tried to begin again. They brought people who understood them, secured tools, and argued that the city needed time to recover. Yet the work depended on more than the men beside a single fitting. Clay had to arrive from one yard, bricks from another, timber from the landing, food from the stores. Instructions stopped at places no one had ever thought to guard.
  The unfinished height remained above them.
  Radan went to the yard once more. He carried a wedge he had borrowed. The foreman who received it spoke to him with tired urgency, pointing toward the platform. Radan could not understand the words.
  He understood the invitation.
  For a moment he wanted to accept. On the platform he had known what his hands were for. At home, he could not tell his father what had happened on it.
  He put the wedge down and left.
  At the stores, Hessa found a different difficulty. The keeper who had once explained every mark no longer understood her questions. He pointed at their board and held up a hand.
  Wait.
  She had waited through too many seasons.
  She brought the copy Sela had helped her make. She laid it beside the keeper’s account. She pointed to each length of finished cloth, then to the marks acknowledging its delivery.
  The keeper studied them. He fetched another man. The second man understood Hessa’s words, but he had not made the account and knew less about it than she did.
  She explained again.
  Behind her, people gathered with jars and boards. Some argued. Some simply stood, exhausted by the effort of needing something ordinary.
  A boy slipped between them carrying an empty bowl. The keeper looked at it, then at the waiting households.
  At last he set the accounts farther apart and began matching the marks instead of listening for an explanation.
  Hessa saw the mistake before he did. She tapped it twice. He frowned, compared the earlier board, and rubbed the mark away.
  When he returned her copy, the work had been answered.
  She went outside with grain and the cleared board. Aviel stood waiting with Ishri. Hessa held the board up.
  He did not understand her words of relief. He understood the look on her face.
  That evening they packed the loom.
  Nobody in the house mistook the gesture.
CHAPTER 18
The Road Out of the Shadow
They left the city after sunrise.
  Barek walked beside the cart. He carried less than he had carried into Shinar and gave fewer opinions about the weight. Hessa kept the account board wrapped beneath the loom. Aviel carried his tools and the measuring cord. Ishri held a packet of seed Tirzah had given her with patient gestures and a word the girl had begun trying to learn.
  Radan looked back at the tower.
  It stood above the roofs, immense and unfinished. He could still name fittings he had worked on. Somewhere in that height was a peg he had driven while believing that being part of something large would make his own life secure.
  He had not been foolish to want good work. The thought mattered to him. He did not know how to keep it beside the other truth, that he had ignored what the work was becoming because he wanted it to need him.
  At the gate, households were choosing roads. Some moved toward relatives whose speech they understood. Others traveled with people they had met only during the confusion. Children were passed between carts. Names were called until the callers became hoarse.
  A woman recognized Barek and asked him to join her group. Her brothers had work for him near a branch of the river.
  He listened. He looked toward Hessa.
  She understood the offer. They still shared words. For once she did not make the decision easier with a joke.
  “Will you go?”
  “Not today.”
  She nodded.
  Beyond the gate, Aviel stopped to tighten a strap. Radan knelt beside him and reached for the cord. His father handed it over. They worked at the knot together, awkward for a moment and then finding the old sequence in their fingers.
  Ishri watched from the cart.
  When the strap was sound, Radan gave the cord back. He spoke a sentence his father could not understand. Aviel waited, then pointed toward the road.
  Radan nodded.
  The Lord scattered the people from the city across the earth. To Ishri, in that first morning, the scattering was a wheel crossing the threshold of the gate and a woman behind them realizing that the household she had hoped to follow had already chosen another road.
  They waited for her.
  Barek found room for her bundle. Hessa gave her water. Aviel checked the cord around the load again.
  The tower’s shadow fell behind them before noon.
  Its height had made leaving seem impossible. The cart moved at the same slow speed it had moved on the road into Shinar.
CHAPTER 19
The Song with Missing Words
On the third evening outside the city, Ishri tried to sing the seed song.
  She remembered its tune. She remembered when her mother lifted her voice on the long line before the end. But the words she meant to put beneath the tune would not come back to her.
  She began twice and stopped twice.
  Hessa looked up from the grain she was sorting. Radan sat nearby, repairing the small boat he had carried from the hills. It had spent years on a shelf in the city. Now its cloth wrapping lay open on his knees.
  Hessa sang the first line.
  Radan knew the words. Ishri heard her mother’s voice carrying sounds that no longer belonged to her.
  She rose and walked beyond the fire.
  Aviel followed after a while.
  “I cannot remember it,” she said.
  He sat beside her. The plain was dark except for the scattered fires of other travelers.
  “I remember the sound.”
  “I want the words.”
  He had no answer that would restore them.
  Behind them Hessa stopped singing. Ishri heard movement, then footsteps. Her mother sat on her other side and opened one hand. In her palm lay several seeds from the evening’s sorting.
  She pointed to a seed and said a word.
  Ishri said the word she knew now.
  Hessa repeated it badly. Ishri almost smiled. Then Hessa repeated her own word and waited.
  This time Ishri tried.
  They worked with seed, hand, ground, rain. Aviel joined, needing more repetitions than his daughter. Hessa laughed once at his attempt, then covered her mouth, afraid she had hurt him.
  He laughed too.
  Later she sang the first line again, slowly. She touched the seeds where the line named them. She pressed her palm against the ground where the line spoke of planting.
  Ishri began to understand what she was being given.
  It would take many evenings. The old song would not simply return, and the new language did not have to vanish to make room for it. She could learn her mother’s words as something offered now.
  At the fire, Radan lowered his repaired little boat into a shallow bowl. It floated.
  He called to Ishri. She could not understand the sentence, but she recognized his delight.
  She came back and touched the hull with one finger. It rocked, steadied, and stayed above the water.
  Radan pointed toward their father. Then he tapped the bottom of the boat.
  Ishri looked at Aviel. He was smiling.
  She understood enough to smile with him.
CHAPTER 20
A Place for the Door to Open
By the time they reached the smaller river, Hessa could ask Ishri whether she had eaten.
  She asked too often. Ishri complained in the language they now shared imperfectly. Hessa understood the complaint and asked again, delighted by the achievement.
  Radan had learned enough of his father’s speech to argue over a boat’s width. They still reached for gestures when the argument became complicated. Barek said this improved the argument, because neither man could be unreasonable at his former speed.
  They found Tirzah’s household near a garden that had begun to take root.
  The houses were modest. A tree leaned slightly over a bench. A potter’s firing had left a trail of smoke above the roofs. Ishri recognized Tovi before she recognized the kiln, though he had grown broader and looked more willingly useful than he once had.
  Ruma recognized their loom.
  “You brought a good frame out,” she told Hessa through Radan’s careful help. “That is more than many people brought.”
  Tirzah came from the garden. She put both hands around Ishri’s face, then stood back to look at how much the girl had grown. Ishri offered the packet of seed.
  Some remained from Tirzah’s gift. Some had been gathered along the road. The girl explained this in words she had learned slowly beside the evening fires.
  Tirzah listened without hurrying her.
  That was how the new place first welcomed them.
  Aviel and Radan built their house near the landing. They argued about the doorway. Radan wanted it broad enough to carry a loom through. Aviel pointed out that the loom could be taken apart.
  Hessa stood between them and measured its full width herself.
  They made the doorway broad.
  On the day they moved in, Barek came with the woman whose brothers had offered him work outside Babel. He had traveled to see them and returned with news, a useful tool, and a decision to build his own room nearby.
  He set the tool on Aviel’s bench.
  “It is borrowed,” he said.
  Aviel understood the words. He had learned them specifically for dealings with Barek.
  That evening the households shared food beneath the leaning tree. People spoke in several tongues. A request crossed the table by words, pointing, and a child who enjoyed translating badly enough to be corrected.
  Ishri began the seed song.
  Hessa joined. Radan followed on the next line. Aviel missed a word and recovered. The song was slower than it had been in the empty house beside the hills, but it reached the last verse.
  Far away, the tower stood unfinished. Here, a lamp waited inside a house whose door could admit the things its people actually needed.
  After supper Radan carried his little boat to the river. Ishri went with him.
  He tied a cord through its bow before setting it on the current. The water took it gently. He paid out enough cord to let it find the middle, then held it steady while the evening sky moved over its sound little hull.
  Ishri stood beside him, one hand on the cord.
  Neither asked the other to let go.

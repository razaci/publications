Chapter 1
The Country of His Hand
By dusk, Lazara and Elias had crossed three villages without leaving the giant’s hand.
  Wheat filled the hollows between his knuckles. Children drove goats along the white ridges of his fingerprints, and an orchard covered his wrist. The fruit sellers called their country Himland. They said the name easily, with their mouths full, as if a country could be anything other than a man who had fallen and never risen.
  Elias stopped beside a roadside shrine and tugged at his boot.
  “Stone?” Lazara asked.
  “My sock.”
  “An ancient curse.”
  “Your stitching.”
  She laughed before she could stop herself. Eleven days of walking had made laughter feel like something they had forgotten to pack. He sat, and she knelt to pull the bunched wool straight. His ankle was swollen above the leather. She kept her fingers there a moment longer than necessary.
  They had left their room in the southern city with a loaf, two blankets, and the brass lantern her mother had tended until her hands could no longer hold it. Three nights before, its wick had lit without oil. In the glass, a ship-shaped mark appeared whenever Lazara turned east. They were following it toward the Ark, which people discussed in the same embarrassed voices they used for answered prayers.
  At the last village a baker gave them bread and refused payment.
  “Take the lower road,” she said. “The shoulder road belongs to the surveyors now.”
  “What’s below?” Elias asked.
  “The way he fell.”
  Hundreds of bells hung beneath the lower arch. None moved, though the wind bent the grass. A boy of fourteen sat on the wall with a bundle between his feet. When they passed, he climbed down and followed.
  Lazara waited until his footsteps became impossible to mistake.
  “Do you have somewhere to be?”
  “The same place you do.”
  “Your name?”
  “Tovin.”
  Elias broke the baker’s second loaf and offered him half. They went on as three.
  The ravine opened onto a lake. Across the water a colossal woman’s face and shoulder emerged from the depths. The giant beneath their feet had reached toward her. His fingertips had stopped short of hers.
  In a shelter beside the shore, Lazara found a book beneath glass.
  He was still reaching for her when the stone took him.
  Elias read it over her shoulder. His hand closed around hers.
  Below the shallows, stairs descended into the giant’s palm. At their foot stood a door bearing the mark of the Ark.
  Lazara lowered her lantern.
  A light answered.
  Then came three knocks.

Chapter 2
The Door Under Water
Elias took off his coat.
  Lazara caught his sleeve. “You haven’t even tried the handle.”
  “It’s twenty feet down.”
  “And you swim like a cupboard.”
  He looked at the dark water, then at her. “A reasonably buoyant cupboard.”
  She wanted to kiss him for being afraid and remaining ridiculous. Instead she put the coat back around his shoulders and stepped onto the first stair.
  The lake drew away from her boot.
  It did not drain. It stood aside, curved and trembling, opening a passage down to the door. A silver fish hung at the level of her cheek, working its mouth. Tovin made a noise that might have been laughter if his teeth had not been chattering.
  “Stay behind me,” Elias told him.
  “Beside us,” Lazara said.
  They descended together. The water smelled of cold pennies. Halfway down, Lazara’s lantern showed a plain gold ring embedded in the stone wall, enormous enough to encircle a house. The giant had worn it under his skin when the stone closed over him.
  At the door, Elias lifted the latch.
  A woman with cropped gray hair stood in a tiled vestibule, holding a mug. Behind her, a kettle shrieked on an ordinary stove.
  “You took your time,” she said.
  “We didn’t know we were expected.”
  “Nobody ever does. Come in before I lose the corridor.”
  The lake shut behind them with a sound like a blanket being shaken. Tovin pressed his back to the door.
  The woman introduced herself as Mara. She gave them tea, then examined the lantern without touching it. There were black burns on the underside of her wrist, round and regular as buttons.
  “You carry a keeper light.”
  “My mother’s.”
  “Then somebody taught her patience.”
  Lazara thought of her mother repairing cuffs by the window, pausing between stitches when pain tightened her jaw. She had called it stubbornness. Perhaps it had been both.
  Mara spread a map beside the stove. The lake lay between two hands. Beyond it, ridges formed a chest, a throat, a face turned toward the northern sky.
  “His name was Aram. Hers is Sere.”
  “Was?” Elias said.
  Mara drank her tea.
  “That is the question people are preparing to answer badly.”
  Above the stove a radio crackled. A clipped voice asked for Channel Nine. Mara switched it off.
  “We leave before dawn.”
  “For the Ark?” Lazara asked.
  “For his heart. If the Ark is to reach this country, somebody has to open its mooring.”
Chapter 3
A Table for Four
Mara had four cups but only three chairs. Elias ate sitting on an upturned coal scuttle. Whenever he moved, it complained beneath him, and each time Tovin tried harder not to smile.
  Lazara watched the boy’s hands. He crumbled his bread before eating it, reducing each piece to something he could manage. There was a brass button sewn into his bundle with blue thread. A woman’s coat button, too large for his shirt.
  “Who packed that?” she asked.
  “My sister.”
  “Is she waiting somewhere?”
  He put another crumb in his mouth.
  Mara reached for the kettle. “Leave him something to tell us tomorrow.”
  Later, while Tovin slept beside the stove, Elias helped Lazara wash the cups. The water in the basin warmed only where her lantern touched it.
  “Do you believe her?” he asked.
  “About the giant?”
  “About us going inside him.”
  She dried a cup carefully. Elias had made it almost to the door of their room before telling her he wanted to come. Until then she had assumed he would stay with his workbench and his customers and the comfortable life they had only just begun to afford.
  “You can say you’re frightened.”
  “I’m frightened.”
  “Me too.”
  He leaned his forehead against hers. The cup stood damp between their chests.
  “I want a house with you,” he said. “That seems an unreasonable thing to think about tonight.”
  “What sort?”
  “A very small one. Roof. Door. Unremarkable walls.”
  “A cupboard for your swimming equipment.”
  He laughed quietly. She felt it through the hand she had laid against his ribs.
  They kissed with the basin pressing into Lazara’s hip, and for a moment the entire miraculous world narrowed to the familiar roughness of his cheek. She had missed being allowed to want something ordinary.
  From the table, Mara said, “There used to be houses here.”
  They separated, embarrassed.
  Mara turned a small iron key between her fingers.
  “Under the lake?” Lazara asked.
  “Before it was a lake. Before the Office decided an empty reservoir was easier to manage than a living town.”
  “Were you there?”
  “My husband was.”
  She put the key back beneath her collar and rose to bolt the door.
  No one asked another question. Elias rinsed the last cup. Lazara spread their blankets close enough that she could find his hand in the dark.
Chapter 4
The Office at the Wrist
The road to the wrist passed through a vineyard growing out of a wound.
  White stone curved above the vines, smooth at the edges and jagged within. Mara said Aram had caught a falling tower there. Generations of winegrowers had turned the scar into terraces.
  By noon they reached a customs station. Its flagpole leaned over a courtyard full of empty benches. A man in a black waistcoat waited beside a folding table, his papers held down by an apple.
  “Keeper light?” he asked.
  Mara put a sealed card in his hand.
  His expression changed. He led them through a back door and down a staircase so steep Elias had to descend sideways with the packs.
  In the room below, five telephones sat beneath five silent clocks. A woman wearing a headset looked up from a ledger. Her nameplate read AGENT VENN.
  In this country’s strange federal bureaucracy, messages to the living giants passed through a liaison office shared by the CIA and FBI. The acronyms survived on old forms, though the people stamping those forms seemed as worn and provincial as any station clerk. Ordinary citizens never called a giant. They filed requests and waited, sometimes for generations.
  Venn examined the lantern.
  “It’s waking,” she said.
  Mara nodded.
  “How long?”
  “Three days, if the tremors keep their interval.”
  Venn’s pencil stopped. Upstairs, somebody dragged a chair across the floor. Lazara could hear every scrape.
  “What happens in three days?” Elias asked.
  Venn turned the ledger so they could see the map. Red pencil surrounded the villages on Aram’s hand.
  “Director Rusk intends to move the mountain.”
  “He’ll crush them.”
  “He calls it a controlled translation.”
  “Does that improve it?”
  Venn closed the book. “I didn’t say it was my phrase.”
  She gave Lazara a narrow strip of gray paper with nine raised dots along one edge.
  “At the throat station, put this into the receiver. Ask for an open hearing. Say you carry testimony from both shores.”
  “We don’t,” Lazara said.
  “Then you had better find some.”
  A bell rang upstairs. Venn opened another door onto the vineyard.
  As they stepped through, she caught Elias’s sleeve.
  “If you reach the heart, read the instructions before you touch anything. My predecessor believed courage excused haste.”
  “What happened to him?”
  Venn looked at the lantern.
  “Your mother carried his light home.”
Chapter 5
Where the Planes Fell Upward
That evening the supply aircraft arrived upside down.
  Lazara heard them before she saw them: a deep chopping noise that made the vineyard leaves turn their silver backs. Three immense planes moved beneath the clouds with their wheels pointing at the moon. Rings of blades revolved around their hulls, each ring appearing to turn against the next.
  Tovin ran for a ditch.
  “They’re delivering flour,” Mara said.
  A cable dropped from the belly of the nearest aircraft, which was also its roof. Crates descended until workers on the ridge caught them with hooked poles. The plane remained inverted, patient as a beetle caught under glass.
  Elias stared openly.
  “I repair clocks,” he said. “I’m going to find that very difficult to return to.”
  “The northern air is folded,” Mara told him. “Everything crosses it differently.”
  A final crate descended apart from the others. Its cable shivered. The workers shouted and backed away.
  Something inside knocked.
  Lazara was moving before Mara caught her arm.
  “Listen first.”
  Three knocks. A pause. Two more.
  Tovin climbed from the ditch. “That’s a person.”
  The foreman hurried toward them, palms out. The crate bore a transit label with a black bar covering its destination. It was not to be opened here.
  Elias took a hammer from his pack.
  The foreman said, “You will cost me my position.”
  Lazara held his gaze. “Help us make it look damaged.”
  He hesitated, then took the hammer himself.
  Inside lay a young courier with a dislocated shoulder, strapped between padded boards. Her name was Iona. She bit a folded scarf while Mara steadied her arm. Elias turned away for the brief, sickening moment when the joint went back into place.
  Iona’s first words afterward were an apology for the crate.
  Her second were for the letters.
  They had been sewn into her coat lining: evacuation orders recalled before delivery, grain accounts, petitions marked resolved without ever being read.
  Under them lay a charcoal drawing of two enormous hands.
  Between the fingers, someone had written a sentence in a child’s careful script.
  She heard him. She has always heard him.
  Mara sat down on the crate’s broken lid.
  Lazara had never seen her look old before.
  Above them the planes climbed through the folded air, carrying their empty cables into the dusk.
Chapter 6
The Orchard That Remembered
They hid Iona in a cider press. The foreman promised to send a cart for her when the next shift arrived. Before they left, she gripped Lazara’s wrist with her good hand.
  “My brother drew that. He lives on Sere’s shore. Find the white mill.”
  Lazara tucked the drawing inside her coat.
  At midnight they reached the orchard above Aram’s wrist. Apples glowed among the branches, each one lit from within by a small moving scene. In one, a woman bent over a crib. In another, an old man shook rain from his hat while someone hurried to open a door.
  “Don’t pick them,” Mara said.
  Elias had already reached toward a branch. He let his hand fall.
  “They’re his memories?”
  “Some of them. Some belong to people who have lived here.”
  They slept under a tree that remembered summer. Its leaves kept the rain off their blankets.
  Near dawn, Lazara woke to Elias standing beside the lowest branch.
  An apple held the kitchen of his childhood home. His mother was slicing a pear with the thin knife she used for everything. She said something to a little boy outside the frame. Lazara could not hear it.
  Elias touched the apple with one finger.
  “She always gave me the larger half.”
  Lazara went to him. He had told her his mother died during the winter fever, but not how he had spent the last morning refusing to leave for work, or how she had scolded him until he went. Those details came now, awkwardly, between long silences.
  “I keep thinking she wanted me to stay,” he said. “And I didn’t understand.”
  “She may have wanted you to have breakfast and go on living.”
  He shook his head. Lazara did not argue him out of it. She put her arms around his waist and stayed until the orchard grew pale.
  When he finally turned, his face was wet.
  “Would it be terrible if I wanted to stay here?”
  “No.”
  “Would you stay?”
  She looked at the little kitchen shining inside the fruit.
  “For a morning.”
  He nodded slowly. Together they sat beneath the branch until Mara woke.
  When they left, Elias took nothing from the tree. But for the first hour he told Lazara about his mother’s terrible singing, and how she had sung anyway, and how he had always joined in before the end.
Chapter 7
The Town of Sensible People
The next town had no bells.
  Even the church tower held a slate board on which the hour was written by a man who climbed up every sixty minutes. When Lazara asked why, the innkeeper gave her the patient look reserved for people determined to misunderstand good sense.
  “Sound travels in the stone. We prefer not to disturb what supports us.”
  The innkeeper’s name was Dema. Her husband had been mayor, and his portrait hung above the counter. Under it sat a tray of unanswered petitions.
  Mara asked about a crossing to the white mill. Dema denied that there was a mill on the opposite shore. There was no opposite shore worth discussing. She could provide beds if their business was lawful.
  That night an earthquake slid a cup from their bedside table.
  Elias caught it before it broke. Downstairs, chairs scraped. By the time Lazara reached the window, people were standing in the square in their nightclothes, watching dust fall from the church.
  “The quarry,” Dema said when they came down. “A distant detonation.”
  Mara pointed at a crack opening beneath the counter. “Your floor is moving.”
  “It settles.”
  From the lane came a child crying for her grandmother. Elias left his cup on the counter and ran outside.
  The grandmother’s house had pulled apart at its seam with the neighboring building. She sat trapped behind a wardrobe while plaster sifted onto her hair. Elias found a carpenter’s jack. Lazara squeezed through a window, cutting her palm, and stayed with the woman while the men lifted the fallen beam.
  “What do you call this?” she asked Dema afterward.
  The innkeeper stood in the doorway, holding a basin for the blood.
  Dema looked at the rescued woman. She looked up at her husband’s portrait visible across the lane.
  Finally she said, “I call it happening.”
  Before dawn she brought them the petitions. Her husband had filed every one after a tremor, for seven years. The Office had answered with measurements showing that no significant motion had occurred.
  At the bottom of the tray lay his last request: permission to warn the schools.
  Dema laid that sheet separately in Lazara’s hand.
  “Put this where someone has to read it.”
  She unlocked the church tower, climbed the stairs herself, and took down the slate board.
  Behind it hung the bell.
Chapter 8
A Name at the White Mill
The ferry was a door laid across six barrels. Its owner insisted this was a boatmaking tradition. Elias inspected the barrels, added two more, and declined to argue about tradition.
  Halfway across, Tovin said, “My sister is at the mill.”
  Lazara put down the paddle.
  “Why didn’t you tell us?”
  “Because if she wasn’t, I wouldn’t have to say it.”
  The lake lifted gently beneath them. Lazara resumed paddling.
  The white mill stood on Sere’s thumb, where an old channel still turned a wheel. A young man with charcoal on his fingers came out carrying a basket of wet shirts. He recognized his own drawing before anyone spoke.
  “Iona?”
  “Alive,” Mara said. “At the cider press above the western landing.”
  He set down the basket very carefully.
  His name was Ben. He brought them inside, called for someone named Neri, then climbed a ladder so quickly the rungs shook.
  Tovin remained by the door.
  A young woman appeared at the top of the ladder. For a moment neither sibling moved. Then Tovin crossed the room with such force that she staggered when he reached her.
  “You stupid little fool,” she said into his hair.
  “I know.”
  “I told you to wait.”
  “I know.”
  She held his head between her hands as if checking every feature for damage. There was a white burn down her forearm. Tovin touched it and began to cry.
  Lazara went outside. Elias followed with two cups of water. They sat on the steps and gave the reunited siblings the privacy of being unheard.
  Ben joined them later with a tin box. Inside were mill records going back eighty years.
  Every spring, Sere’s shore received nine knocks from somewhere beneath the lake. The millers answered on the great iron axle. After the reservoir works, the knocks stopped.
  “But three months ago,” Ben said, “she began again. In the stones. At night.”
  Mara opened the oldest ledger. The words beneath the first recorded exchange were written in a steadier hand than all the entries that followed.
  Tell him I held on.
  Elias looked toward the water. “Did anyone tell him?”
  Ben shook his head.
  “The Office closed the channel before my grandfather was born.”
Chapter 9
The Woman Beneath the Shore
Neri insisted on coming with them to the listening room.
  “I spent four years cleaning their instruments,” she told Mara. “I know which ones lie.”
  The room occupied a pocket of hollow stone below the mill. Along one wall, a metal diaphragm rose from floor to ceiling. Ben struck the mill axle with a wooden mallet, waited, and struck again.
  Lazara pressed a palm to the wall.
  At first she felt only the cold. Then pressure moved through it, slow enough that she mistook it for her own pulse.
  The lantern brightened. Lines spread across the diaphragm, making letters in condensation.
  ARAM.
  Neri sat abruptly on the steps.
  Mara knelt before the metal. “We are small travelers. We are going to him.”
  For a long time nothing happened.
  Then: DO THE ORCHARDS STILL FLOWER.
  Lazara thought of Elias’s mother cutting a pear.
  “Yes,” she said. “People live there.”
  The answer took so long to form that her knees began to ache.
  I KNOW.
  Outside, the millwheel turned. Lazara could hear water falling through wood.
  “Did he know you were alive?”
  The metal remained blank.
  Mara touched Lazara’s shoulder, but she stayed. Elias settled beside her on the floor. He unrolled their blanket beneath her knees without interrupting.
  At last the letters returned.
  HE HEARD ME FALL.
  Below them, smaller:
  HE DID NOT HEAR ME STAY.
  Lazara bowed her head. She had imagined the giants as a tragedy already finished, a picture on a page. But Sere had endured each year that millers copied their numbers, every spring when the branches budded above Aram’s wrist. Waiting had not become smaller because the bodies were immense.
  “What can we carry to him?” Elias asked.
  A crack opened in the wall. Inside lay a strip of pale metal, no longer than his hand. On it were two shallow marks curving together.
  Lazara took it. The metal was warm.
  The diaphragm clouded one final time.
  HIS RING HAD TWO LINES.
  Sere had scratched them there before their wedding, Mara translated quietly, so he could find the right ring among his brothers’ gifts.
  Elias closed Lazara’s fingers around the strip.
  “We’ll show him.”
  When they climbed back to the mill, the rain had stopped. Tovin was waiting with Neri’s coat over his bundle.
  This time, when he joined them on the road, he had someone’s permission.
Chapter 10
What Elias Had Signed
They took shelter in a chapel built inside Sere’s broken bracelet. The curved roof held a strip of stars. Someone had left dry wood stacked beside the hearth and written PLEASE REPLACE WHAT YOU USE above it.
  Elias searched the woodpile for kindling long after the fire was established.
  Lazara recognized his silence. He used it when a clock could not be repaired and he had already taken payment.
  “Tell me.”
  He sat on the hearthstone.
  “Rusk’s instruments. Some of the regulators are mine.”
  Mara looked up sharply. Tovin stopped chewing.
  “They ordered timing mechanisms,” Elias continued. “Nine pulses, synchronized. I was told they controlled irrigation gates.”
  “How many?” Mara asked.
  “Six sets.”
  “When?”
  “Last winter.”
  Lazara remembered the money. They had bought the good blankets with it. He had brought home oranges in a paper bag, six of them, and they had eaten one over the sink because neither could wait.
  “I saw the diagram later,” he said. “A man came for a repair. There were towns marked in the pressure zone. I asked him what the gates were for.”
  “What did he say?”
  “He said I had signed a confidentiality agreement.”
  “And you said?” Lazara asked.
  “I said nothing.”
  The fire shifted. One of the logs broke, throwing a little swarm of sparks.
  “Why are you telling me now?”
  “Because I recognized the sound in the customs room. The relays. It’s the same system.”
  She stood and walked to the open end of the chapel. Stars shone between the stone bracelet’s edges. Behind her, Elias did not follow.
  She wanted him to follow. She wanted him to understand without making her say it that she had been carrying a future in which he told her things before they grew teeth.
  At last she returned.
  “I wish you’d let me be there when you were frightened.”
  “I was ashamed.”
  “I can stand beside ashamed.” Her voice broke. “I can’t stand beside a man who keeps moving himself somewhere I can’t reach.”
  He put his hands flat on his knees.
  “I don’t know how to undo it.”
  “Start by showing Mara the mechanisms.”
  He took out his notebook.
  Lazara sat beside him. She did not take his hand immediately. When she did, he held it as carefully as a thing he had almost lost.
Chapter 11
The Fair of Unfinished Things
The fair stood at the joint of Aram’s smallest finger, where traders pitched their stalls around a spring of warm blue water.
  There were mirrors that showed rooms the buyer had never entered, cages full of folded weather, and a woman who repaired umbrellas damaged by events that had not happened yet. A tiny storm followed a dog between the benches, raining exclusively on its tail.
  Tovin forgot himself enough to laugh.
  They needed rope, lamp glass, and a good reason for four travelers to be moving toward the throat. Mara bought a surveyor’s measuring chain. Elias traded his spare tools for a coil of climbing cord. Lazara kept their remaining coins for food.
  At a stall hung with children’s shoes, a merchant offered her an envelope.
  “From your mother.”
  Lazara stopped.
  He knew her mother’s name. He knew the little habit she had of folding shopping lists into squares. The envelope was folded that way, its corner tucked beneath itself.
  “How much?”
  “Your lantern.”
  Elias turned toward the stall.
  Lazara reached for the envelope, then hesitated. Her mother’s letters always bore a faint stain where she rested the side of her inked hand. This one was clean.
  “Read it to me,” she said.
  “Private correspondence.”
  “Tell me the first word.”
  The merchant smiled without opening it. “Daughter.”
  Her mother had called her Mouse, even after she grew taller than her.
  Lazara backed away. The merchant’s face changed, losing its careful softness.
  “People pay dearly to hear what they need.”
  Elias stepped between them. “She said no.”
  They left the fair by an alley smelling of fried onions. Lazara made it almost to the end before her legs weakened. Elias caught her under the arms and helped her sit on a crate.
  “I wanted it,” she whispered.
  “I know.”
  “I knew it was wrong, and I still wanted it.”
  He knelt in the mud so their faces were level.
  “Of course you did.”
  Tovin returned from the food stalls with four hot onions wrapped in leaves. Mara did not ask how he had paid. She only handed him the change from her own purse and told him to take it back.
  They ate sitting together behind the fair, sharing a pinch of salt from Elias’s palm.

Chapter 12
The Bridge of Little Bells
The bridge crossed a gorge between two fingers. Bells hung from its rails in such numbers that the cables bowed beneath their weight. Some were small enough for a cat’s collar. Others had been made from pots, split shells, and the cut ends of brass pipes.
  Each belonged to someone who had crossed and returned.
  “Why were the bells at the lower arch silent?” Tovin asked.
  “Those were for the people who didn’t,” Mara said.
  There was no other route to the throat before Rusk’s three-day deadline. They tied themselves together, Elias first, then Tovin, Lazara, and Mara.
  Halfway across, the gorge exhaled.
  A warm wind rose carrying the smell of turned earth. The bridge tipped. Bells crashed against one another. Lazara dropped to her knees as a plank tore free ahead of Tovin.
  The boy vanished to his waist.
  Elias lunged backward, caught his bundle, and went down hard. Lazara braced her boots against the rail posts. The rope tightened across her ribs so sharply she could not draw breath.
  “Tovin, look at me,” Elias said.
  The boy kicked at empty air. The bundle’s stitching began to rip.
  “Your hand. Give me your hand.”
  “I can’t.”
  “You can be frightened while you do it.”
  Tovin let go of the broken plank. Elias caught his wrist.
  Mara crawled past Lazara and looped the measuring chain around a sound post. Together they hauled until Tovin’s knees scraped onto the boards.
  For several minutes no one moved.
  Lazara could see blood on Elias’s sleeve. His left hand shook. When she touched his shoulder he flinched, then bent his forehead to the wood.
  “Give me a moment.”
  She knelt beside him, blocking the wind with her body.
  Across the gorge, a supply aircraft slipped upside down through the clouds. Its windows shone with people eating supper. Lazara hated it briefly for being so far from their terror.
  When they reached the other side, Tovin took the brass button from his bundle. He tied it beneath a bell with a piece of blue thread.
  “Does it count?” he asked.
  Mara touched the little improvised clapper.
  “It will ring.”
  Elias could no longer lift his pack. Lazara put it on over her own before he could argue.
  They walked the last mile in silence, close enough for their shoulders to touch.
Chapter 13
The Room Above the Clouds
The throat station occupied an old observatory. Its windows looked into clouds from which enormous shadows occasionally rose, too distant and too slow to be mistaken for aircraft.
  At the gate a guard asked for their papers. Mara showed her card. He read it twice, then called for a stretcher when he saw Elias’s arm.
  “I can walk,” Elias said.
  “The stretcher is for your luggage, sir.”
  Lazara began laughing and could not stop. She laughed until she was wiping her face with her sleeve while the guard waited, kindly pretending to inspect the card again.
  Inside, a medic bound Elias’s shoulder and told him to keep the arm still. Tovin fell asleep in a chair before anyone could offer him a bed.
  Venn arrived at dusk, carrying an overnight bag.
  “I was transferred,” she said.
  “Because of us?” Lazara asked.
  “Because I asked a question in writing.”
  She fed the gray strip into a receiver the size of a wardrobe. A green light moved across its face. Somewhere beyond the walls, a bell too low for hearing made Lazara’s breastbone ache.
  The living giant who answered gave his name as Oren. Venn listened through the headset and repeated his words in a voice carefully stripped of expression.
  He had watched Aram and Sere fall. He had believed the small settlements were fragments of their dreams, changing too quickly to possess separate lives. He did not understand why a dream would fear the mountain moving.
  “Tell him I repaired a woman’s window this morning,” Elias said. “It will still need painting whether he believes in her or not.”
  Venn listened.
  “He asks how long your people last.”
  “Long enough to love someone,” Lazara said. “Not long enough to finish.”
  Venn’s fingers tightened around the headset.
  For nearly an hour there was no reply. They ate cold potatoes while the green light crossed and recrossed the receiver.
  Then Venn straightened.
  “He says the people at the lake once struck the stone to be heard. He thought it was rain.”
  Mara lowered her cup.
  “Will he listen now?”
  The answer came faster.
  “He will receive testimony. At dawn. Through the heart relay.”
  Venn looked at the clock above the door.
  They had twelve hours.
  Below the windows the clouds opened briefly, revealing thousands of lights on the hand of a man who had been trying to hold his wife.
Chapter 14
The Director and His Maps
Rusk arrived while they were eating breakfast before departure. He wore a field coat with mud on the hem. Lazara had expected polished shoes and a cold smile. The mud unsettled her more.
  He asked permission to sit.
  Venn remained standing beside the receiver.
  “I know what you’ve heard,” Rusk said. “I would prefer you also heard what I know.”
  He spread his maps between the plates. Red lines crossed the giant’s chest. Beside each were dates, pressure readings, and names of villages already evacuated.
  “Aram’s heart is slowing. When it stops, the lower body will collapse into the basin. I have fewer than two days to raise him into the northern fold.”
  “And the people still there?” Lazara asked.
  “The aircraft are moving them.”
  “Not all of them,” Mara said.
  “No. Not quickly enough.”
  He said it without looking away.
  Rusk had lost a daughter in an earlier collapse. He carried her photograph but did not show it until Mara asked who the girl’s shoes in his bag belonged to. Then he placed it beside the maps. She had a gap between her front teeth and a paper crown tipped over one eye.
  Lazara wished his grief made his plan impossible to oppose. It did not.
  “You closed the channel to Sere,” she said.
  “Before my appointment.”
  “You kept it closed.”
  “Because an answer takes hours. An earthquake takes seconds.”
  Elias put his notebook on the table. His drawings matched the relay diagram on Rusk’s map.
  “I built your timers. They cannot distinguish the evacuation signal from the pulse that breaks the mooring.”
  “They do exactly what I ordered.”
  “That is what I’m telling you.”
  Rusk looked at him for a long moment.
  Then he offered them a place on the last northbound aircraft. Two seats, guaranteed. The lantern would remain with his technicians.
  Under the table, Lazara found Elias’s hand.
  He squeezed once.
  “We’re going to the heart,” she said.
  Rusk folded his daughter’s photograph first, protecting it inside his notebook.
  “I hope you get there,” he said. “I am not postponing the lift.”
  After he left, Venn locked the outer door.
  “There’s a service stair,” she said. “And I can give you twenty minutes before I report you missing.”
Chapter 15
The Stair of Other Lives
The service stair descended through the giant’s throat.
  At each landing a narrow doorway opened onto somewhere else. Lazara saw a kitchen where an elderly Elias shelled beans beside a window. At another, a woman wearing Lazara’s blue dress stood alone in their old room, looking toward the door.
  “Keep your feet on the stairs,” Mara said.
  Tovin stopped before a landing where he could hear Neri singing. In that room she had no burn on her arm. Their mother sat beside her, sewing the brass button onto a coat.
  Lazara waited beside him.
  “Can they see us?” he whispered.
  “I don’t know.”
  “If I went in, would it be stealing?”
  She had no wise answer that would not sound cruel. She crouched and laid his hand against the place where the bridge rope had burned her wrist.
  “You helped us get this far. I’d miss you.”
  He looked at the mark. Then he took a step downward.
  At the next landing, Elias stopped.
  Inside stood their small house. The roof was sound. A bowl of oranges sat on a table. Lazara was sleeping in a chair with a book open on her lap, and beside the hearth a child turned a wooden wheel.
  The real Lazara felt him tremble.
  “I didn’t know I wanted that much,” he said.
  She could not look away from the sleeping woman. That other Lazara had loose hair and unscarred hands. Someone had put a blanket over her knees.
  Mara continued two steps, then waited without speaking.
  “Could we?” Elias asked.
  “Go in?”
  “Have it. Some of it. If we get back.”
  Lazara leaned against his uninjured side.
  “I’d like to try.”
  He kissed the top of her head. Together they went on.
  Behind them the rooms grew dark, one by one, as if a patient host had finished showing a house no one could afford.
  At the foot of the stair they found an iron notice bolted to the stone.
  HEART RELAY ACCESS REQUIRES TWO KEEPERS.
  Below, in smaller lettering:
  ONE TO TEND THE LIGHT.
  ONE TO RELEASE THE RETURN.
  Mara read the plate. Her face changed.
  “That was what they left out of the orders,” she said.
Chapter 16
The Name Mara Had Kept
They rested beside a conduit warm enough to dry their clothes. Mara sat apart, her iron key lying in her palm.
  “My husband’s name was Jon,” she said.
  Lazara settled beside her. Elias and Tovin stayed close enough to listen.
  “He went into the relay with another keeper. I was their operator. There was a problem with the return gate. The orders said one keeper had to remain.”
  She turned the key over.
  “We all believed it. It had the seal. It had three signatures.”
  “What happened to the other keeper?” Elias asked.
  “He came back carrying a light. Your mother met him at the southern crossing. He died before he could tell her much.”
  Lazara looked down at the lantern. A dent marked its base, shaped like a thumbnail.
  “Was Jon left alive?”
  “Yes.”
  The word seemed to hurt Mara’s teeth.
  “I kept the station open for nine days. I heard him sometimes. Then Rusk’s predecessor ordered the reservoir flooded. They said the line carried echoes. That keeping it open would give people false hope.”
  “And you closed it.” Elias’s voice was quiet.
  “I closed it.”
  The conduit hummed beneath their feet.
  Mara had spent years guarding the lake door, waiting for a light to return. Lazara understood now why she had said they took their time. There had been no appointment. Only a woman counting mornings.
  “I’m not asking you to forgive me,” Mara told them.
  “I wasn’t preparing to,” Elias said.
  Lazara glanced at him. He was looking at his notebook, at his own mechanisms drawn with such tidy care.
  “But you’re here,” he added. “Tell us everything else.”
  Mara pulled a folded maintenance sheet from inside her sleeve. She had memorized it years ago without understanding the part crossed out in red.
  The return gate could be opened manually from the outer platform. Its operator needed a chain long enough to bridge the pressure gap. The Office had removed the chain to keep untrained personnel from interfering.
  Elias reached for the measuring chain Mara had bought at the fair.
  He laid it beside the drawing.
  For the first time since the lake, Mara covered her face with her hands.
  They let her cry. Tovin fetched water. Lazara stayed beside her until she could read the measurements aloud.
Chapter 17
The City in the Ribs
The passage opened into a cavern crossed by white arches. It took Lazara several minutes to understand that the arches were ribs.
  Between them, people had built a city.
  Laundry fluttered from balconies bolted into bone-colored stone. A school bell rang. A woman selling soup called down that they looked half dead and should come upstairs before finishing the job.
  The city was named Haven. It held the families of former relay workers, people evacuated from the flooded basin, and those the Office had misplaced on its maps. None had received an aircraft schedule.
  Mara knew some of the older faces. They recognized her slowly and without greeting. One man closed his shop door when she approached.
  At the soup stall, the owner filled her bowl anyway.
  Lazara spread Dema’s petitions on the table. Elias drew the relay, using a spoon to hold the paper flat. Before long, a dozen residents had gathered.
  “We need the heart platform,” he explained. “And a chain that will hold a return gate.”
  A woman with leather braces on both knees fetched a measuring rod. A boy brought a spool of steel wire. The soup seller knew a welder. Someone sent for the schoolteacher, who kept the oldest maintenance records because their backs were useful for arithmetic lessons.
  By afternoon the little table was buried in tools.
  Lazara helped roll blankets in the schoolhouse while teachers organized an evacuation toward the upper ribs. Tovin carried messages, delighted to be useful where nobody asked him to be brave.
  Elias worked one-handed beside the welder. Lazara brought him soup. He drank it without taking his eyes off the gate assembly.
  She put her hand over the drawing.
  “Look at me.”
  He did.
  “Eat the bread too.”
  For a moment he looked offended. Then he smiled, took a bite, and chewed with exaggerated obedience until she laughed.
  Outside, a tremor shook dust from the high ribs. The schoolteacher calmly moved the children away from the windows.
  Far beyond the city, something amber shone in the darkness.
  The people at the table fell quiet.
  It brightened, faded, then brightened again after an interval so long Lazara found herself holding her breath.
  Aram’s heart.
  Someone began counting.
Chapter 18
The Keeper Who Stayed
They reached the heart platform at midnight.
  An amber heart hung above a black gulf, threaded with roots of stone. Its light revealed ladders climbing into darkness and walkways that ended where sections had fallen away. The air smelled of hot copper and rain.
  Beside the control cradle sat an old man wrapped in a gray blanket.
  Mara stopped so suddenly Lazara struck her shoulder.
  The man looked up. His beard had grown to his chest. An empty cup stood at his feet.
  “You came back,” he said.
  Mara made a small wounded sound and ran.
  Jon could not stand quickly enough. She reached him on her knees, knocking over the cup, her hands everywhere at once: his face, his neck, the thin wrists emerging from the blanket.
  “I closed it,” she said. “I closed it.”
  “I know.”
  “I thought—”
  “I know.”
  He held her head against his chest. Lazara looked away and found Elias staring at the floor, his mouth pressed tight.
  Jon had survived because the relay slowed everything inside its circle. To him the years had passed as months, though his body had aged in uneven pieces. Haven’s workers had brought food until the last bridge fell. Since then he had lived on condensed water and the little ration cupboard that refilled whenever the heart completed its long cycle.
  “Poor cooking,” he said. “Very reliable.”
  Mara laughed into his blanket. It broke apart halfway through.
  When he could speak without her clutching him harder, Jon explained the cradle. The old light had nearly burned out. Lazara’s would restore the pathway to the upper mooring, where the Ark waited outside ordinary distance.
  “But I cannot hold the return,” he said. “Not with these hands.”
  Elias spread the maintenance drawing on the floor.
  “We brought a chain.”
  Jon studied it. He looked from the chain to Mara, then shook his head with an astonished, exhausted smile.
  “All this time.”
  Above them the amber heart pulsed.
  Lazara took her mother’s lantern in both hands. She was almost at the cradle when a loudspeaker crackled across the gulf.
  “Keeper party,” Rusk said. “Step away from the controls.”
  On the far platform, uniformed technicians began switching on a row of white lamps.
  The first of Elias’s timers started ticking.
Chapter 19
Testimony from Both Shores
Rusk’s voice carried too clearly in the chamber.
  “The lift begins at dawn. My technicians can take the lantern from here.”
  Jon pushed himself upright. Mara steadied him, staring across the gulf with an expression Lazara hoped never to receive.
  “Your maps don’t show Haven,” Lazara called.
  A technician lowered his clipboard.
  “There is no current settlement below the ribs,” Rusk replied.
  Lazara pointed toward the city’s lights. From this angle they were hidden behind the great heart, but a child’s voice reached them from a distant walkway, calling for her father.
  Rusk turned.
  Mara took the gray strip Venn had given them and pushed it into the heart receiver. The device lit green. At the throat station, Venn answered at once.
  “Open hearing,” Mara said. “Both shores. Record everything.”
  Rusk ordered her to disconnect.
  Instead Lazara laid the petitions beside the receiver. She read Dema’s husband’s last request, including the date, the number of children in the schools, and the location of the cracked road. Elias read the mill ledger. Tovin, leaning close to the speaking grille, gave his name and Neri’s and the name of the room where they had been born.
  They were ordinary details. Lazara had expected testimony to require beautiful language. Instead she found herself explaining that the grandmother in Dema’s town kept three cats, and one had refused to leave the wardrobe even when the beam fell.
  Across the gulf, technicians had stopped working.
  Venn’s voice returned. “Oren is listening.”
  Elias held up his notebook.
  “I made the timers controlling this lift. I knew there were people within the pressure boundary. I accepted the payment. Put my name in the record.”
  Lazara moved closer to him.
  Rusk said, “This is not a trial.”
  “No,” Elias replied. “It’s a warning.”
  The heart pulsed. For the first time, the light traveled outward through the roots and touched the stone beneath their feet.
  From the receiver came a sound like mountains shifting in sleep.
  Venn spoke slowly, translating each phrase as it arrived.
  “Oren asks the children to move away from the lower ribs. He asks whether the keeper can hold the light until he reaches the fold.”
  Lazara looked at the cradle.
  “How long?”
  A pause.
  “Until the ninth pulse.”
Chapter 20
The Shape of a Rescue
Elias counted the relay contacts. Nine brass teeth circled each of his regulators. They were built to close a circuit once the last tooth passed beneath a spring.
  Rusk’s men had linked six regulators in series. At the ninth pulse, the system would release enough force to draw Aram’s entire body into the northern fold. Anything anchored to him would go too; anything loose would be shaken free.
  Elias did the figures twice.
  Lazara knew from his face that he wished the second answer had changed.
  “How many can the aircraft take?” she asked Rusk.
  “Before dawn? Seven hundred.”
  “Haven has more than two thousand.”
  “The outer villages are not empty either.” Rusk rubbed both hands over his face. “I know.”
  Jon beckoned them toward the old maintenance panel. On its underside lay another map, scratched directly into the brass. A vessel hovered above a mountain. Lines dropped from its keel toward the keeper stations.
  “The Ark was made to come here,” Jon said. “It is a harbor that moves. The lift was meant for the ship, not for him.”
  Rusk came across on a service bridge, taking the steps too quickly for his technicians to follow. Up close he looked sleepless and cold.
  “Those circuits haven’t answered for a century.”
  “Because there hasn’t been a keeper light on both sides of the break.”
  Lazara held up Sere’s strip of metal.
  “She gave us this.”
  Jon touched the two curved marks. His breath caught.
  “A memory conductor.”
  Mara said, “Explain it so we can use it.”
  Sere’s testimony could complete the broken line between their hands. Lazara’s lantern could bring the Ark through that line. Oren, speaking through the upper station, might hold the fold steady long enough to let a ship pass without moving the mountain.
  There were more uncertainties than certainties. Rusk named every one. Jon answered those he could.
  At last Lazara said, “Your way has the same unknowns. Only you’ve already given up on the people underneath.”
  Rusk looked toward Haven. A procession of small lights was climbing between the ribs.
  He called his chief technician.
  “Redirect the aircraft to the lower city. Leave the transfer relays armed.”
  Elias shook his head. “The final circuit must be disconnected.”
  “If your ship fails, I will still have a chance to lift him.”
  “And kill everyone still boarding.”
  Rusk’s jaw tightened.
  He gave them four pulses to prove the Ark could come.
Chapter 21
The Small Work Before Morning
There was no sleep after that.
  Haven’s welders fixed Mara’s measuring chain to a length of salvaged cable. Elias checked every link while Lazara held the lamp. The welder’s name was Ada. She had once repaired the millwheel on Sere’s shore and remembered Iona as a child stealing cherries from her cart.
  Lazara told her the courier was safe.
  Ada put down her torch for a moment. Then she pulled her visor over her face and went back to work.
  Tovin brought blankets from the school. His arms shook under the pile. Elias took half with his good hand.
  “You can make two trips.”
  “I’m trying to be useful.”
  “You’ll be useful longer if you can still walk.”
  The boy considered this with the seriousness of someone receiving a difficult technical instruction. On his next trip he brought fewer blankets and two mugs of tea.
  Lazara carried a message to the city square. The people there did not cheer when she described the Ark. They asked where to line up, whether they could bring animals, how steep the boarding ramps would be, and whether a woman’s father could remain in his wheeled chair.
  She went back to Jon for answers.
  The Ark had broad gangways. Animals could come. The chair could come. They needed food, medicine, tools, and seed, but furniture must stay.
  At the last instruction, a carpenter began to cry. He had made the bed in which both his children were born.
  Lazara stood beside him, unable to improve the instruction.
  He wiped his face with his apron. “I’ll bring the tools.”
  By the time she returned to the heart, Elias had finished checking the chain. He looked at her bleeding palm, which she had forgotten again, and fetched clean cloth.
  They sat on a crate while he wrapped it.
  “We keep meeting beside things that need washing,” he said.
  “Your choice of romance.”
  He tied the bandage with his teeth, then kissed the inside of her wrist.
  She touched his cheek with her good hand.
  For a few breaths they stayed there, the crowd moving around them, the enormous heart shining overhead. Lazara said a prayer without closing her eyes. It was mostly their names, and the names of people she did not yet know how to leave behind.
  When she stood, Elias rose with her.
Chapter 22
The Gate Across the Dark
The return gate lay beyond the damaged walkway.
  Mara insisted on taking the outer station. Jon caught her sleeve before she could start.
  “Do not make this a punishment.”
  She looked at him.
  “You can do the work,” he said. “Do it because of that.”
  Her mouth trembled. She kissed his forehead and checked the chain once more.
  Elias went with her. Lazara hated this arrangement immediately, though she could see he was the only person who understood the regulator. They tied the safety cord above his hips and secured it to a sound pillar.
  “Wait until I say it’s held,” he told her.
  “I will.”
  “Even if the light changes.”
  “I will.”
  He studied her face, then touched the bridge of her nose with one finger, an old private gesture that meant he was trying not to cry.
  The gap between platforms was six feet across. Beneath it the darkness breathed warm air. Mara crossed first on a narrow service beam, carrying the chain. Elias followed with his injured arm held against his chest.
  Halfway over he stopped.
  The beam had twisted below his boot.
  Lazara tightened her grip on the cord. It was impossible to know whether pulling would save him or throw him off balance.
  “Elias.”
  “I know.”
  “Tell me what you need.”
  “A minute.”
  She gave him the minute. It was the hardest work she had done all night.
  He lowered himself until he was sitting astride the beam. From there he moved slowly, one leg dragging after the other, looking more irritated with every inch. Mara caught his collar when he reached the platform.
  “Elegant,” she said.
  “I’m considering a career in bridges.”
  Lazara laughed shakily and let the cord loosen.
  They fastened the chain to the gate wheel. When Mara turned it, pale light appeared around a doorway on Lazara’s side of the gulf. Through it she saw wet grass and a strip of ordinary dawn.
  Home, or at least a way back to weather.
  “The return is open,” Mara called.
  Elias bent over the regulator, adjusted a contact, and looked up.
  “It’s held.”
  Only then did Lazara carry the lantern to the cradle.

Chapter 23
The Heart Takes Light
The cradle fitted the lantern so precisely that Lazara could see where other hands had worn the brass smooth.
  She slid Sere’s strip beneath its base. The two curved scratches brightened, reflected in the glass.
  For a moment nothing else happened.
  Lazara thought of her mother trimming a wick. No ceremony. No trembling proclamation. Just an old pair of scissors and the patience to make something ready for another night.
  She lifted the latch and opened the lantern’s little door.
  Amber light climbed her wrists.
  Across the gulf Elias said her name, but he did not tell her to stop.
  The heart expanded. Its roots filled with radiance. Far above, between the ribs, the sky became visible where only stone had been.
  Lazara heard Sere’s words inside the receiver, translated by no one she could see.
  Aram. There were two lines on your ring.
  The great heart faltered.
  Then came a sound so low it loosened dust from every ledge.
  I looked for you.
  I was there.
  I could not reach.
  I know.
  Lazara gripped the cradle. Tears ran down her face before she understood why. She had carried Sere’s message through mills, stations, and stairways, but hearing it arrive felt terribly private. She wanted to give them a room with a door that could be shut.
  Above the heart, the darkness unfolded.
  A keel appeared against the clouds. Then a wall of dark timber rose behind it, ribbed with copper and hung with gardens. Windows shone along its side, not a few but hundreds, warm as houses at supper.
  The Ark entered slowly, pushing aside the folded distance like a ship parting reeds.
  In Haven, someone shouted.
  Others began shouting too.
  A gangway lowered from the hull toward the upper city. Then another. Lamps along both rails lit in sequence.
  Rusk stood motionless on the far platform.
  “The fourth pulse,” his technician said.
  He looked at the regulators.
  Lazara could not let go of the cradle. The light had caught her hands and was drawing steadily through them, not burning, but leaving her so tired she could scarcely keep her head upright.
  “Rusk,” she said.
  He did not seem to hear.
  “Disconnect it.”
  The heart began its fifth long beat.
Chapter 24
The Director Opens His Book
Rusk reached for the emergency lever.
  It would not move.
  The chief technician tried beside him. Together they forced it down halfway before the housing cracked and sprayed sparks across the floor.
  Elias shouted from the outer gate. “The sequence is latched. Cut the copper bus below the timers.”
  Rusk dropped to his knees.
  There were six regulators. Each had a separate supply, and the covers had been riveted shut to prevent tampering. Elias had followed the specification exactly.
  He shut his eyes for one beat.
  Then he called out the rivet positions.
  Rusk’s men fetched drills. Mara held the return wheel while Elias leaned across the gate rail, explaining which wires must remain intact. If they severed the wrong line, the Ark would lose its mooring before the evacuation finished.
  At the cradle, Lazara’s knees buckled.
  Jon pushed a stool behind her. She sat without letting go. He wrapped his blanket around her shoulders.
  “Can you take the light?” she asked.
  “I can help carry it. Your hands must stay where they are.”
  He put his palms over hers. They were dry and warm, like folded paper kept beside a stove.
  The strain eased enough for Lazara to breathe.
  Across the chamber, Rusk pried the first cover loose. A contact flashed. He cried out and snatched his burned hand away.
  His notebook fell from his pocket. The photograph of his daughter slid across the floor toward the gulf.
  A technician caught it beneath his boot.
  For a moment Rusk stared at the small rectangle of paper. Then he picked it up, set it on a dry step, and returned to the open regulator.
  He did not say her name. He did not turn his grief into a speech.
  He called for insulated cutters.
  Venn’s voice sounded from the receiver. Oren had reached the fold. He could steady the upper seam but needed the mooring kept alive through the ninth pulse.
  Lazara looked toward Elias. Across the distance he lifted his good hand.
  She lifted one finger in answer, all she could manage without breaking contact.
  The fifth pulse passed into silence.
  From Haven came the rolling sound of wheels on the Ark’s gangway.
  The first families were boarding.
Chapter 25
The Sixth and Seventh Pulses
At the sixth pulse, the children from Haven’s school reached the Ark.
  Tovin counted them at the gangway because their teacher had asked him to. He counted twice when a pair of twins insisted on changing places. One child carried a jar with a moth inside. Another had brought a door handle, certain the new place would require one.
  On the deck stood no glorious company of distant saints. There were ordinary people with ropes, stretchers, kitchen aprons, and tired faces. A woman in a wool cap took names on a slate. A man with one leg directed families toward the lower cabins.
  “Where’s the captain?” Tovin asked.
  “Working,” the woman said, handing him another slate.
  The aircraft began landing at the upper docks. Dema arrived with the grandmother and three indignant cats in a wicker basket. The baker came with flour. Ada’s family carried her tools and the carpenter’s dismantled headboard, which he had reduced to two carved birds small enough to fit in a pocket.
  At the seventh pulse, the white mill’s ferry rose through a fold in the lake and slid alongside the Ark.
  Neri climbed aboard with Ben and Iona, whose arm was bound in a sling. When Tovin saw his sister he ran so fast that he dropped both slates.
  She caught him by the shoulders.
  “Again?” she said.
  “This time I had permission.”
  “From who?”
  “Several responsible people.”
  Iona laughed, then winced and held her shoulder.
  Below them the heart chamber shook.
  On Rusk’s platform, four regulators lay open. The fifth had jammed beneath a fallen piece of conduit. Two technicians worked to lift it while a third cut the floor bolts.
  At the outer gate, Elias felt the chain jump.
  One of its links had begun to open.
  He called to Mara. She shifted her weight against the wheel, taking the strain long enough for him to wrap the damaged link in steel wire. His fingers were clumsy with fatigue. The wire cut through his skin.
  Across the gulf, Lazara watched his bent head and could do nothing to help.
  Jon’s palms remained over hers.
  “Look at the ship,” he said softly.
  “I can’t.”
  “Then look at him. That is allowed too.”
  She watched Elias finish the binding. He tested it once, twice, and nodded toward her.
  For another little while, the gate held.
Chapter 26
A Promise Without Fine Words
The eighth pulse struck while Rusk was cutting the last regulator free.
  The final copper line split with a bright blue flash. All six timers stopped.
  For one breath the chamber was silent.
  Then the pressure already gathered beneath the platform released through the floor.
  Elias saw the beam twist. He shoved Mara toward the doorway before the outer station lurched sideways.
  The safety cord caught him when he fell.
  Lazara screamed his name.
  He hung below the platform, his injured arm useless against his chest. The cord ran over a broken edge. Fibers parted one by one.
  Mara threw herself flat and reached for him, but her fingertips stopped a foot above his wrist.
  Jon could not leave the cradle without dropping the light. Lazara pulled against its hold until pain shot through her elbows.
  “Let me go,” she begged.
  Jon looked at her. There was no rebuke in his face, only terrible understanding.
  “If you break it now, the gangways will fall.”
  She could hear people boarding. Shoes on wood. A baby crying. Somewhere a man was shouting for someone to keep moving.
  Elias looked up at her across the gulf.
  “Stay there,” he said.
  She shook her head so hard tears struck the glass.
  “Lazara. Stay.”
  Mara crawled backward toward the gate wheel. If she released it, the return would shut. If she did nothing, Elias would fall.
  Then she saw the chain.
  She took a coil from the reserve length and pushed it over the edge. It struck Elias’s shoulder. He caught it with his good hand, missed the loop, caught it again.
  The cord snapped.
  The chain took his weight with a sound like a hammer striking an anvil.
  Mara cried out as it dragged across her palm. On the far platform Rusk’s technicians ran for the service bridge. They crossed one at a time, clipped onto a second cable, and helped haul Elias up.
  When he finally rolled onto the stone, he lay perfectly still.
  “Move,” Lazara whispered. “Please move.”
  He turned his head.
  Even at that distance she saw him trying to smile.
  “I’m finished with bridges,” he called.
  She laughed and sobbed together until Jon leaned his forehead against her shoulder and wept with her.
  The ninth pulse began to gather overhead.
Chapter 27
The Distance Between Two Hands
Oren’s voice came through the receiver as a pressure in the air. Venn translated over it, her words shaking.
  “He has the seam. Keep the heart steady.”
  Beyond the chamber, the lake rose in a single shining wall. Sere’s fingers glowed beneath it. Aram’s stone hand answered, its old channels filling with light.
  Lazara felt the temptation before she understood it. With the Ark open and the giants listening, she might ask for the whole mountain to rise. She could almost see Aram standing, Sere beside him, the long waiting ended in one magnificent movement.
  Then she heard the little wheels on the gangway.
  “Don’t stand,” she said into the grille.
  The heart’s surface rippled.
  “People are still on you. We haven’t finished.”
  A long silence followed. In it, the mountain became extraordinarily still.
  Venn said, “He understands.”
  Sere’s words traveled along the strip beneath the lantern.
  I cannot come farther.
  Aram answered through the great heart.
  Then I will learn this distance.
  The lower mooring brightened. Lazara saw what Jon’s map had tried to show: the Ark did not need Aram’s body to move. It needed a passage held between the memory of reaching and the knowledge of being answered.
  Two immense fingers shifted beneath the lake.
  It was a movement smaller than the width of a human house, stretched across a landscape. Oren held the fold while water rose above the settling shore. The mills stood. The villages trembled but remained. In Haven, a cracked wall finally fell into an already emptied street.
  Aram’s fingertip met Sere’s.
  Light ran between them.
  The Ark’s keel cleared the stone. Every gangway steadied.
  Lazara felt the cradle loosen around her hands.
  Across the chamber, Elias got to his feet with a technician’s help. Mara guided Jon toward the return door, but he would not leave until Lazara stood.
  She lifted the lantern.
  For the first time since her mother died, its flame divided. One small light remained in the cradle. The other burned behind the familiar glass.
  She had not been asked to leave everything she loved in order to carry it.
  The ninth pulse passed through the heart and into the hills.
  The intervals shortened. The great amber surface began beating with the slow, regular strength of something that intended to continue.
Chapter 28
The Last People Through
The return doorway opened onto a wet hillside beside the Ark’s final gangway.
  Mara took Jon through first. He stepped into the morning and stopped, overwhelmed by the wind on his face. She caught his hand before he could turn back toward the chamber.
  “No,” she told him. “You have done your part.”
  Rusk sent his technicians after them. One man returned for the director’s notebook. Rusk took it, checked the photograph inside, and ordered him onto the ship.
  Lazara crossed the service platform to Elias.
  Up close she could see how pale he was. His left hand had swollen beneath its bandage. There was a red line across his throat where the safety cord had caught his collar.
  She put down the lantern and held him.
  He leaned his weight against her without pretending it was only an embrace.
  “I heard you,” he whispered.
  “When?”
  “When you said please.”
  She pressed her face into the red scarf. It smelled of stone dust and the soap from their old room.
  “I thought you couldn’t.”
  “I couldn’t answer.”
  They stood that way until Rusk touched Elias’s good shoulder.
  “The gate.”
  Its pale outline was narrowing.
  Elias picked up the lantern. Lazara took his weight. They limped across the platform together while Rusk followed with Jon’s empty ration cup, absurdly unwilling to leave it after everything else.
  At the doorway Elias stopped.
  “The chain,” he said.
  It remained looped through the return wheel. They had used all the reserve length to save him. Someone would have to release it from the chamber side.
  Rusk looked back.
  Lazara’s stomach turned cold.
  Then a tremor ran through the floor, and a narrow root of amber stone rose beneath the wheel. It held the spokes in place.
  Jon, watching from the far side, began to laugh.
  “He heard the instructions,” he said.
  Aram was holding the return.
  All three stepped through.
  The doorway closed behind Rusk with a soft click.
  Tovin met them halfway up the gangway. He took the lantern, then remembered Elias’s shoulder and stopped himself from embracing him too hard.
  “Everyone?” Lazara asked.
  The boy held up the slates.
  “We counted the school twice. The towns matched their lists. There are still people in the outer farms, but the aircraft crews say the ground is stable. They’re staying to check.”
  Lazara looked toward the hills. The hands still touched across the lake.
  She climbed the remaining steps with Elias beside her.
Chapter 29
What Was Entered in the Record
They slept for sixteen hours.
  Lazara woke in a narrow cabin with Elias’s good arm around her waist and someone hammering gently on the other side of the wall. For one confused moment she thought they were home and a neighbor was repairing his shelves.
  Then she saw the round window.
  Beyond it, clouds moved below the Ark.
  She touched Elias’s face. He opened one eye.
  “Roof,” he murmured.
  “Door.”
  “Unremarkable walls.”
  A hammer struck again.
  “Thin,” she said.
  He smiled and went back to sleep.
  During the next week, the Ark remained above the basin while aircraft returned families to villages that could safely be occupied. Haven’s residents were offered land near the white mill or cabins aboard until they chose. Nobody was hurried into choosing.
  Rusk submitted his records to an open inquiry. Venn copied every page before handing over the originals. Elias wrote his own statement and stayed to answer questions about the timers. He did not let Lazara answer for him, though she sat in the room each morning.
  Dema read her husband’s petition aloud at the first hearing.
  No one called the tremors insignificant.
  Mara brought Jon to the deck after supper, wrapped in three blankets despite his protests. They sat facing the lake. Sometimes they talked so quietly Lazara could not hear. Sometimes Mara fell asleep against his shoulder and Jon sat watching the sky with a look of amazed responsibility, as if he had been given something breakable and wonderful to keep until morning.
  Through Venn’s station, the living giants received a new rule for contact. No settlement could be treated as a dream or a surveying mark. Its residents would speak for themselves, in recorded hearings with replies delivered back to them.
  Oren asked whether people would forgive how long it had taken him to listen.
  Venn read the question to Haven’s assembly.
  Ada said forgiveness was complicated and that the east gangway needed lifting by four feet.
  Oren lifted it.
  After that, conversation became easier.
  At the mill, Ben built a new speaking axle with two handles at a human height. On the first evening, people from both shores gathered around it.
  The stone answered slowly.
  They brought chairs.

Chapter 30
The House Beside the Reaching
Spring came to Himland while the Ark still watched above the lake.
  Elias recovered enough movement to complain about the exercises, which Lazara took as an encouraging sign. He rented a workshop beside the white mill, repaired ordinary clocks, and refused every sealed commission that arrived without a plain explanation of its purpose.
  Their house stood a little way uphill. It had a roof, a door that swelled in wet weather, and two rooms whose walls Elias found entirely remarkable because he had plastered them himself.
  Lazara planted beans beneath the window. The first ones died. The second lot grew. She put no spiritual interpretation on this, though Tovin offered several.
  He lived with Neri near the mill and came most afternoons to borrow things he could have borrowed closer to home. Usually he stayed for supper. Iona returned to her courier route after her shoulder healed, carrying letters in a proper bag with no blacked-out labels. Ben complained that she expected him to mend every buckle and then mended them anyway.
  On the day the orchard flowered, Lazara and Elias married beside the lake.
  There was no grand procession. Dema brought a bell. The baker brought more bread than anyone could eat. Ada supplied a bench that was too heavy for four guests to move, which she considered an excellent defense against theft.
  Mara and Jon arrived last because he had insisted on walking the final stretch. She pretended to be annoyed and held his hand the whole way.
  Lazara wore her indigo coat over a clean dress. Elias had repaired its torn pocket with stitching so crooked she intended to keep it forever.
  Before the vows, he took a plain ring from a little wooden box. Two shallow lines curved across its surface.
  “Is that all right?” he asked.
  She looked beyond him.
  Across the lake, the immense hands remained joined. Orchards grew along Aram’s wrist. Sere’s shoulder held a new footpath, marked carefully around the houses that had survived. Neither giant had risen. They had learned to speak across the distances their bodies could no longer cross.
  “Yes,” Lazara said.
  Elias slid the ring onto her finger.
  When her turn came, she forgot the words they had practiced. He waited, smiling in the helpless way that always undid her.
  “I want to come home and tell you things,” she said at last. “Even when I don’t know how.”
  He nodded. His eyes shone.
  “I’ll be there.”
  That was enough for the woman conducting the ceremony. It was more than enough for Lazara.
  They kissed while Tovin rang Dema’s bell with far too much enthusiasm. Somewhere high above, the Ark answered. Its great hull had turned toward another folded horizon, another country waiting for a harbor.
  Lazara watched it go without feeling that she had been left behind.
  That evening they carried the remaining food into their little kitchen. Elias washed the cups. She dried them. From the lake came a single deep vibration, followed much later by another.
  Aram and Sere, saying good night.
  Elias set down the last cup and turned toward her.
  “What?” he asked, because she was staring.
  She went to him. There was flour on his sleeve and a tired crease beside his mouth. She knew where he hurt. She knew the way he laughed when he was trying to keep quiet. She had watched him hang above a gulf and return to her carrying nothing that could make them rich.
  “Nothing,” she said. Then, remembering her promise, she shook her head. “No. Not nothing. I’m glad you’re here.”
  He drew her close.
  Outside, the lake darkened beneath the joined hands. In the window her mother’s lantern burned with an ordinary, steady flame.
  They left it there while they went to bed.
  In the morning there would be beans to water, letters to answer, and a door that still needed planing.

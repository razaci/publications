Chapter 1
The Cup in the Rain
The rain fell six floors above the people who were thirsty.
  Nell watched it descend through the hanging orchards of Vey, silver in the manufactured morning. Beyond the glass partitions, workers in green coats raised channels around the roots of fruit trees. Below, in the arrival shelter, a woman held an empty bottle against her chest as though it were a child who would wake if she moved.
  Mercy of Morning had brought filters, three pump engineers, and the papers Lantern Junction had finally issued after the transfer-arm inquiry. The papers recognized their home as inhabited. They did not give it a road.
  For that, someone at Vey possessed an old navigation key.
  At the shelter's far end, a girl was being asked to open her bag.
  Nell might have continued toward the receiving office if the attendant had not tipped the bag upside down.
  A sock fell out. Something inside it struck the bench with a small, final sound.
  The girl grabbed for it. The attendant caught her wrist.
  “Please,” she said.
  Nell set down the filter case.
  “Let her pick it up.”
  “We have missing ration cartridges.”
  “Then look for cartridges.”
  Inside the sock was a chipped cup. Inside the cup was soil, a spoon, and a seedling whose two leaves had folded together in the cold.
  The attendant released the girl's wrist. His face became tired rather than cruel, which did not undo what he had done.
  “Where did you get the soil?” Nell asked.
  “Our garden.”
  “Before you left?”
  The girl nodded.
  It was wet soil. Nobody in the arrival line had received water since morning.
  “How much of your drinking water have you given it?”
  A shrug. A child's way of shutting a door that had no lock.
  Nell sat beside her. Above them, the cooling towers thundered.
  “What are you growing?”
  “I don't know. Mama planted it.”
  “What's your name?”
  “Lio.”
  Nell took off one glove and steadied the cup while Lio put it back inside its sock.
  “Then we'll have to wait and see.”
  The attendant gave them a cloth for the spilled soil. He did not apologize. Lio did not thank him. Nell found herself grateful that the child had not been required to make the encounter comfortable for him.
  A bell sounded above the shelter. People lifted their bottles. For a moment Nell thought it announced water.
  Instead the attendants began closing a barrier across the far benches.
  “Registered households to the upper queue,” a voice said. “Unassigned arrivals remain seated.”
  The woman with the empty bottle rose, then sat when nobody else moved. Nell asked where the drinking point was. An attendant indicated a dispenser on the other side of the barrier.
  “Can you bring a jug through?”
  “They need an arrival reference.”
  Nell held up the filter case. “And these need water to be tested. Who can authorize a test?”
  That question had an answer. He took her to a maintenance tap. She filled the test reservoir, checked the seal, and asked for a clean jug. When she returned to the benches, the attendant carried it. He was careful to explain that this was temporary provision connected to equipment commissioning.
  The woman filled her bottle without arguing about the description.
  Lio watched the water pass from hand to hand. When the jug reached her, she held out the cup.
  “For you first,” Nell said.
  The girl drew it back against her stomach.
  “A different cup,” Nell added. “We can find one.”
  It took another trip to maintenance. A worker gave her a little metal measure with its handle broken off. Lio drank, keeping her eyes on the plant as though someone might take it while she was occupied.
  Nell left the measure with her. She wrote the location of the relief kitchen on the filter packaging and gave it to the woman with the bottle.
  Only then did she see the appointment time on her own receiver. She had spent most of their first hour in Vey obtaining permission to use water that ran visibly down the windows.
  At the receiving office, her appointment had already passed.
  A notice explained that unaccompanied delays incurred a new fee. Beneath it another notice displayed the smiling face of Vey's Governor Aven Thale, his hands lifted toward a sky he did not own.
  ONE TABLE. ONE MORNING. ONE PEOPLE.
  Nell put her damp glove back on.
  Behind her, Lio was dividing the recovered soil between the cup and a cracked seam in the shelter wall. She pressed one loose seed into the seam with the spoon.
  The child had not followed her into the receiving office. Nell found her again beneath the partition, scraping a little channel in the mortar with the spoon.
  “Is someone meeting you?”
  Lio pulled the spoon back.
  Nell sat on the floor far enough away that the girl could leave without climbing over her. People moved around them with parcels and damp coats. The woman with the bottle had found a seat on the next bench and was holding a place with an outstretched arm.
  “I'm not asking for a registration,” Nell said. “I need to know whether someone knows where you're waiting.”
  Lio shook her head.
  “The person you came with?”
  “Different shuttle.”
  “Your mother?”
  The girl nodded once. Her eyes stayed on the seam.
  Nell had been separated from people in enough transfer halls to know the first comforting explanations. Another desk. Another queue. A missed connection that would look foolish afterward. None belonged in her mouth before she had checked.
  She asked permission to sit with Lio while they requested information. The girl said she could sit, but she was not taking the cup. Nell agreed. They went together to the nearest arrival desk, where the attendant wanted a group reference.
  Lio remembered a blue light on the shuttle door. She remembered a man carrying a bag made from netting. The official listened without writing. Nell asked him to search the shuttle arrivals by the time the child had reached the shelter.
  “There are too many.”
  “Then tell me which service received the unaccompanied children.”
  The service had changed shifts. The current attendant found a handwritten entry for one girl, family connection pending. No destination had been filled in. He wanted to know why she had left the supervised benches.
  Lio moved behind Nell's arm.
  “She is here now,” Nell said. “Who is checking the connection?”
  He called the previous shift. Nobody answered. He entered a follow-up request and offered to place Lio in a holding room until it was resolved. The word holding made the girl grip the cup with both hands.
  Nell asked to see the room. It was clean, with blankets, four narrow beds, and a locked exterior door. An attendant explained that the lock prevented children wandering into freight traffic. She did not seem to understand why Nell wanted to know who could open it from inside.
  At the shelter again, Nell asked Lio whether she wanted to wait there or come to the relief kitchen while the search continued. Neither choice would make her mother arrive faster. The girl studied the paper with the kitchen's name.
  “Will they know I went?”
  Nell took her back to the desk and watched the temporary location entered. She photographed the entry with Lio standing beside her, then wrote the same destination on a card the child could keep. The attendant signed the paper when asked.
  They returned for the cup. Lio tried to lift the seed from the wall seam.
  “You can leave that one,” Nell said.
  “They'll clean it.”
  “They might.”
  Lio pushed the loose soil more firmly around it. Then she took Nell's hand for three steps, let go, and walked toward the kitchen carrying the plant herself.
  It was the first unauthorized planting Nell saw in Vey.
  It would not be the last.
Chapter 2
The Ship That Had Already Returned
Damas found his own signature on a voyage he had not made.
  The clerk showed him the screen with the patience reserved for pilots who could not read schedules. Mercy of Morning had arrived three days earlier, discharged forty-six passengers, and departed without requesting water.
  Damas looked through the office window. His ship remained attached to berth fourteen. Its left running lamp was still flickering, a fault he had intended to repair before anybody noticed.
  “Who signed?”
  “You did.”
  “I draw the tail of the last letter the other way.”
  The clerk magnified the signature.
  Damas had not known that his handwriting could make him feel physically ill.
  Nell arrived carrying the filter case and the smell of rain. He let her look before explaining. She counted the passengers twice.
  “We brought nine.”
  “Apparently we were more efficient before we left.”
  She did not smile.
  Their first years among the gates had taught them to distrust easy explanations involving time. Different routes could retain different calendars. That did not make every bad record a visitor from tomorrow.
  Nell asked for the underlying berth signal.
  The clerk refused. Damas asked whether a waterless ship returning under his name could also inherit his unpaid fuel account. The clerk stopped refusing long enough to call a supervisor.
  While they waited, Nell copied the registration identifier by hand. The first twelve characters matched Mercy. The last four belonged to a class of automated service cradle.
  Someone had used a real arrival to support a false one.
  Outside, a man was kneeling beside an old woman, fastening a replacement wheel to her chair. He wore the yellow scarf of the harbor's relief office. When a child ran across the quay, he held out one hand without looking and kept the child from stepping into a moving freight belt.
  “That's Iven,” the clerk said. “He brought your passengers through.”
  Damas opened his mouth.
  “Our recorded passengers,” Nell said quietly.
  Iven rose when they approached. He had grease on his cheek and a bent screw between his teeth. He took out the screw before speaking.
  “I'd hoped to meet you under better circumstances.”
  “Have we met under others?” Damas asked.
  Iven looked at the registration slip.
  His first reaction was not surprise. It was calculation, swift enough that Nell almost mistook it for a change in the weather.
  Damas caught the repaired chair before it rolled onto the sloping edge of the quay. The old woman gave him a sharp look.
  “I can steer.”
  “The edge drops.”
  “I can see that too.”
  He let go. She turned neatly, then looked from him to Iven.
  “You said the captain knew.”
  Iven took the registration slip out of Damas's hand and put it back when Damas held his hand open.
  “We shouldn't discuss her record here,” he said.
  “I'm here,” the woman answered.
  Her name was Hessa. She had arrived on a municipal lighter with a cracked heat shield. The people aboard had been told that an authorized expedition ship would sponsor the paperwork. Nobody had pointed out a ship. Hessa had assumed this was because she was slow descending the ramp and had missed the introduction.
  “Did they ask you to sign?” Nell said.
  “They asked if I wanted a room with a heated floor.”
  “What did the signature say?”
  “I didn't have my lenses.”
  Damas felt his anger choose a direction too quickly. Hessa noticed that too.
  “Don't shout at him until you know who locked the rooms,” she said. “He brought the lenses.”
  Iven stood without answering. Nell asked Hessa whether she would be willing to tell that account again somewhere private, with someone of her own choosing present. She agreed on condition that nobody changed the time of her eye appointment.
  Nell wrote that down first.
  The slip in Damas's hand had ceased to be a strange message from an impossible voyage. Someone had put his name where an old woman's permission should have been. He wanted the office to correct it immediately. Looking at Hessa, he began to understand that correction could turn the key in the same door.
  “I can explain some of that.”
  “How much?”
  “Not here.”
  Behind him, the old woman tested her wheel and rolled away. He watched until she had cleared the belt.
  The place Iven chose was a disused ticket shelter with enough glass left in its walls for everyone outside to see they were not hiding a fight. Hessa came too. She parked beside the open doorway and asked Damas to stop standing where he blocked the light.
  Iven began with the lighter. It had carried people whose evacuation documents belonged to a service district Vey no longer recognized. The medicine aboard could not be released until a receiving household or authorized vessel accepted the arrival cost. He had used Mercy's expected visit to bridge the gap.
  “Bridge it to what?” Nell asked.
  “A corrected settlement record.”
  “Has it been corrected?”
  “Not yet.”
  Damas put the slip on a patch of dry bench.
  “You signed that I brought forty-six people. What happens if one is missing?”
  Iven said the placement office held the individual assignments. Damas asked who would be questioned if an injury was reported on a ship that had never carried the injured person. This time Iven did not have a ready answer.
  Hessa reached for the slip. Nell gave it to her. She put on the lenses from her coat pocket and read slowly, moving the page rather than her eyes.
  “This says we accepted the household procedure.”
  “That is standard arrival wording,” Iven said.
  “I accepted a room.”
  “You have the room.”
  “For now.”
  He looked at her. She was watching him with none of the gratitude he had received at the wheel repair.
  “I have lived in places where a room was used twice,” she said. “Once to shelter me. Once to tell me what I owed for being sheltered. Which time is this?”
  Iven rubbed the grease from his cheek and only spread it farther. Outside, a freight belt carried wrapped machine parts toward a lift. Each parcel passed a scanner; green lights marked a sequence the system understood completely. Nell thought about the child at the arrival desk and the destination nobody had entered.
  “We need to reach the people named here,” she said.
  “If you call them all together, the office will notice.”
  “If we don't, we have only your account.”
  He offered to arrange individual appointments. Hessa refused the first suggestion, a relief interview room whose registration went through his own office. They settled on beginning with two people she knew from the lighter. She would make contact herself.
  Iven asked them not to demand an immediate cancellation of the voyage. Damas wanted it gone from his ship's history before another bell sounded. Nell understood the impulse; she also understood the way Iven's request placed hungry people between a forgery and its correction.
  “Preserve the record,” she told Damas. “Dispute it. Don't erase it until we know which waivers depend on it.”
  He looked at her for a long time, then wrote DISPUTED across his copy, dated it, and signed with the familiar tail on the last letter.
  Iven offered to carry it to the office. Damas folded it into his own coat.
  Hessa tested the repaired wheel before leaving. It held. Iven crouched to check the screw again, and she let him. Nell watched the work without interrupting. The screw still needed to be safe.
  Nell would remember that. Later, when she wanted one simple description of him, she would find the repaired wheel in its way.
Chapter 3
The Book Beneath the Filter
Anet had learned to inspect a room before choosing where to sit.
  The relief kitchen had two exits. One opened onto the quay; the other led through a pantry whose rear window was large enough for a child. She sat where she could see both.
  Elian noticed. She did not mention it.
  They had come to Vey with separate tasks. Nell needed the route key. Damas needed a usable berth. Elian needed the council's proposed transfer agreement. Anet had asked to help the arrivals register their own requests rather than letting officials translate their lives into cargo categories.
  By noon she had written down seventeen names and refused to write down three.
  One woman wanted medicine, not an official location. One man had a daughter in another district whose papers called him dead. The third person was a boy who had not yet decided whether to trust them.
  Iven brought soup. He served the boy first.
  “You'll need a sponsor for the unregistered requests,” he said.
  “Will you be one?” Anet asked.
  He watched her carefully.
  “For some.”
  The boy by the pantry window came back while Iven was fetching bowls.
  “If I tell you where my sister is,” he asked Anet, “does she have to come here?”
  “No.”
  “Will you go there?”
  “Not unless you ask, or there is an immediate danger I need help with. I would tell you what I was doing.”
  He studied the notebook.
  “Can I see what you wrote?”
  She turned it around. The page held a request for a blanket and a note saying that a child wanted to return with a question. It did not describe the route by which he had entered the room.
  “You can cross out the second part,” she said.
  He did, pressing so hard that the pencil tore the paper.
  Elian brought another sheet. The boy wrote BLANKET himself. He had made the B face backward. Anet resisted the impulse to correct it.
  After he left, Elian sat beside her.
  “What will you do if somebody comes looking for him?”
  “Ask who they are.”
  “And then?”
  Anet shut the notebook. “I don't know every then.”
  “Neither do I.”
  That admission let them make a rule they could actually keep. Requests would stay with the requester unless the person asked for help transmitting them. Urgent dangers would be discussed with the people affected whenever there was time. The kitchen would publish how much food it had, rather than a list of everyone who had needed it.
  The rules left difficult questions. Anet wrote those on a separate page instead of putting the uncertainty into the children's files.
  At the stove, someone dropped a ladle. She startled hard enough to spill ink across her hand. Elian passed her a cloth and went on discussing where the blankets should be stored.
  Sella arrived from Lantern that afternoon with a repaired filter wrapped in a blanket. Tavi had insisted on checking its fastening herself. Beneath the filter, Sella had packed a Bible with a spine mended in sailcloth.
  She found Anet looking at it.
  “My grandmother wrote the family births at the back.”
  “Do you read the rest?”
  “When I'm brave. When I'm angry. Sometimes for reasons that don't improve me.”
  The kitchen receiver began broadcasting Governor Thale's address. He spoke about division, frightened arrivals, and the duty to become one household. Behind his voice swelled a choir recorded in a hall far larger than the kitchen.
  Sella opened Daniel.
  Anet expected her to find a prediction of the broadcast. Instead she turned to the first chapter, where young captives received new names, new education, and food chosen by the king.
  “He can feed them,” Anet said.
  “Yes.”
  “They still have to decide what they accept.”
  “Yes.”
  Anet touched the faded ribbon marking the page. A laboratory had once assigned her a number. She disliked the speed with which the ancient story could reach inside her.
  “Does it say they get their old lives back?”
  Sella looked at her, then at the page.
  “No.”
  It was an answer Anet could trust.
  She helped unwrap the filter. Inside its metal housing, Tavi had hidden a packet of biscuits for the journey. They were broken almost to powder.
  When the broadcast ended, Lio asked whether the governor could find her mother. The promise of one household had been plain enough for a child to hear something practical inside it.
  Elian drew up a chair beside her.
  “There are people in the arrival offices who can help search. We have asked them.”
  “But he tells them.”
  “Sometimes. He doesn't know every person who comes here.”
  Lio frowned. She looked toward the receiver, now showing fruit trees under a gentle rain. The governor had seemed to be talking about everybody.
  Anet brought the location card Nell had made. They read it together. Lio could find the kitchen's name by the shape of the first letter, but the longer words defeated her. Anet drew a bowl beside the address and asked if that helped.
  The child nodded. She added a cup with two leaves.
  Sella asked whether she remembered any name from the evacuation besides her mother's. Lio named a woman who had carried towels. Then she stopped answering and began rubbing a patch of dried soil from the cup.
  Sella let the questions end.
  At the next table, Elian examined the inquiry acknowledgment. It had a reference number and no expected answer time. She called the office and asked how an unaccompanied child's request was escalated. A clerk told her to contact the assigned household. There was no assigned household. The clerk sent her to a different service.
  Anet could hear only Elian's half of the conversation: the repeated name, the explanation that a temporary kitchen was not a legal placement, the request for someone to accept responsibility for returning the call.
  At last a worker named Osa gave a direct office line and said she would compare the shuttle lists at the end of her shift. Elian wrote down the promise and asked permission to call again if she had not heard by evening. Osa agreed.
  It was the first specific undertaking anyone at Vey had made about the search. Elian told Lio exactly what it was. The child wanted to know whether she had to stay awake.
  “No,” Sella said. “We'll remember for you.”
  Lio set the cup near the warm side of the table. When she finally slept, her hand remained around the spoon. Anet put a folded cloth beneath her wrist so the edge would not leave a mark.
  Osa called after the kitchen lamps dimmed. The shuttle lists were incomplete. She had found an evacuation group using the right receiving route but no confirmed entry for Mira. She would preserve the partial list and send the request onward.
  Elian thanked her for calling even without an answer. Then she entered the next follow-up time beside the first. The work would continue in smaller promises than the receiver had offered.
  Anet tipped the crumbs into four bowls. Outside, the broadcast promised a morning without hunger. Inside, she made sure the boy by the pantry window received an equal share.
Chapter 4
The Shortening Day
The orchard lights went out while Tomas was counting leaves.
  He waited for the ordinary evening strip to appear beneath the dome. It did not. Across the cultivation floor, workers stood motionless with their hands inside plants that had suddenly become dark shapes.
  His measuring clock said the day had ended forty-seven minutes early.
  The wall display said nothing unusual had occurred.
  Tomas used the lamp on his wrist to find the manual record. Haven had survived one system that concealed the cost of its supplies. He had no intention of teaching its children to trust another simply because this one had a cleaner interface.
  The independent route that now linked Haven to the wider network was narrow, costly, and useful mainly for messages and small freight. It had taken months of negotiation after Elian's discoveries. It did not make Haven a room in her carrier, whatever the newer harbor diagrams implied.
  He found the discrepancy while checking a child's experiment. Seven-year-old Mera had put two trays beneath the same lamp and shaded one with mesh. She had drawn the leaves every morning. Today her drawing included a dark band across the page.
  “That isn't part of the plant,” Tomas said.
  “It's the time when I couldn't see it.”
  The official horticultural log recorded a full illumination period. Her wrist clock, his measuring clock, and the little mechanical timer on the misting line disagreed with it. The mesh tray had not failed. The day had.
  He asked Mera to keep her drawings. He also asked whether she wanted her name attached when he sent a picture of them.
  “Will I get marked wrong?”
  “For drawing what happened? No.”
  She looked toward the school alcove. He could see she was deciding whether a grown man's assurance applied to other grown people.
  “I can send my clock instead,” he said.
  She chose that. Later she brought him the drawing with her name covered by a square of clean paper.
  Before transmission, Tomas checked the warming trays. The young trees were safe for one interrupted cycle. A second early darkness would leave several grafts vulnerable. He moved the most fragile plants into a kitchen where the ovens would hold residual heat and found the cook already making room.
  “How long?” she asked.
  He gave her his best estimate and the limit beyond which it stopped being useful.
  She moved another bench.
  Only when the plants were sheltered did he go back to the display and request the authorization behind the schedule. It offered him a button to acknowledge completion.
  He photographed the screen without pressing it.
  He sent three readings through it.
  Elian received them in Vey as a hospital attendant was explaining why their medicine delivery needed a residence bond.
  “Where are the patients supposed to live?” she asked.
  “In recognized housing.”
  “And until their housing is recognized?”
  “That is the difficulty.”
  Tomas's measurements gave her another difficulty. The day reduction had been billed as a conservation adjustment, with consent recorded from a representative Haven had never appointed.
  Elian asked the hospital attendant for a paper copy of the bond.
  He handed it over reluctantly. A line near the bottom authorized Vey to recover unpaid treatment costs from the patient's receiving household, including labor allocations.
  It resembled the contracts that had once paid for Haven's comfortable ignorance.
  She took both problems to Orra Venn.
  The registrar's temporary office overlooked a channel of warm industrial water. A cup on her desk held pencils rather than flowers.
  “I can challenge the signature,” Orra said. “I cannot restore the light.”
  “Can anyone?”
  “Several people. None works for me.”
  Orra looked again at the medical bond. Her expression changed at the same clause.
  “My sister signed one of these.”
  Elian waited.
  “She needed treatment. I helped her understand the first page. I didn't read the last.”
  Through the window, a little maintenance boat carried baskets beneath the cultivated terraces. Its pilot wore a clear hood against the artificial rain.
  “Then read it now,” Elian said.
  Orra did.
  The first household they visited had all its payment records, tied in bundles by year. A woman named Serit kept them inside a flour tin above the cooking shelf. She had worked evening laundry shifts for her son's treatment until the ledger showed three days remaining. After a system change, the balance had become ninety-three.
  “I thought they put the nine in the wrong place,” she said.
  “What did the office tell you?” Orra asked.
  “That I was looking at a different kind of day.”
  The harbor used local shifts, common service days, and transit accounting periods. There were honest reasons to convert between them. Orra knew the conversion rules; she had helped issue one of the explanatory notices. Looking at Serit's receipts, she found the credit had been converted once and the debt twice.
  She set three papers side by side. Elian held the flour tin while Serit brought the older bundle.
  “Can you fix it?”
  “I can show the discrepancy.”
  Serit's face tightened. She had heard variations of that sentence for years.
  Orra took out a separate sheet and wrote a formal calculation under her own registration. She included the original units, the conversion date, and the resulting balance. She signed the statement before asking for copies of the receipts.
  “This is evidence you can use whether or not I keep the office,” she said.
  Serit read it. Then she called her son from the next room to make a photograph. He was nearly grown, healthy enough now to be irritated at having his afternoon interrupted by the treatment that had kept him alive. He quieted when his mother showed him the ninety-three days.
  At the next household, the records were gone. A water leak had destroyed the paper. The public ledger was the only surviving account, and it showed no credit for work the family remembered doing.
  Elian asked who had worked beside them. Two neighbors were found. One remembered the shifts but not the dates. Another had kept a meal roster that placed the debtor on the laundry crew for several months. Orra recorded which parts were corroborated and which remained uncertain.
  By the fourth visit, she had stopped expecting one kind of proof to serve every person equally. The people with dry shelves could produce a history. The people whose rooms flooded had to ask someone to believe an account the water had taken.
  They returned to her office after the service lamps dimmed. Elian put food on the desk. Orra barely touched it.
  “I wrote the notice about the conversion,” she said.
  “Then attach it.”
  “It was supposed to explain.”
  Elian opened the first file. “Show where the explanation stopped matching the account.”
  Orra laid the notice beside Serit's signed calculation. She could see how a careful reader might still have concluded the extra days were legitimate. The language assumed the ledger was correct and taught people to reinterpret their memory around it.
  She began a correction in words Serit could take to the office without needing Orra beside her. This time the first sentence said that a balance could be wrong even if the person owed care had already received it.
  By evening, they had a list of fourteen households being charged for medicine they had already worked to repay. Tomas sent another measurement. Haven's second district had lost seventy-three minutes.
  The two clocks were accurate.
  The accounts were not.
Chapter 5
The Animals at the Wall
The goat refused the passage first.
  Renn tried feed, waiting, and the disapproving silence he normally reserved for people. The goat planted its hooves and stared at a blank wall.
  Two dogs joined it. Then a cage of small rescued birds became restless whenever the cooling cycle reached its lowest note.
  “Don't tell me they know a prophecy,” Nell said.
  “I wasn't going to.”
  “Good.”
  “I was going to tell you they can hear something we can't.”
  They were in Vey's old freight quarter, escorting animals displaced from a carrier whose receiving pens had flooded. Renn had arranged temporary shelter near the lower waterworks. The shortest route ran beside a sealed panel stamped with the words VACANT STRUCTURAL VOLUME.
  The goat pressed its nose to the joint.
  Nell crouched. Air moved against her wrist. Not much. Enough to carry the smell of damp leaves.
  She fetched a strip of cloth and watched it bend toward a hairline gap. The wall was drawing air inward during one phase of the cooling cycle and breathing out during another.
  Behind it, something green was living.
  Renn carried the birds away before Nell touched the panel. Their panic diminished with distance. A low vibration remained in her boots.
  The fastening was old but regularly oiled.
  A cleaner came along the passage pushing a narrow cart. Nell stood and moved the animals against the opposite wall.
  “Is this panel serviced?” she asked.
  The cleaner kept wiping the rail. “Everything is serviced.”
  “By whom?”
  “That depends who wants to know.”
  Renn put a lid over the birds' cage, leaving the ventilation clear. Their wings stopped beating against the wire.
  “We need a quieter place for them,” he said. “Is there a room that doesn't share this air?”
  The cleaner looked at the cloth Nell had used to test the joint. Then she pointed toward an old weighing office farther along the quay.
  “The vent there draws from the landing side. Ask for the woman with the red kettle.”
  “Thank you,” Renn said.
  Nell waited until the cart had turned the corner before speaking.
  “She knows.”
  “Probably.”
  “Should we ask her again?”
  “For what? To decide whether she can safely lie to us?”
  The goat pulled at his sleeve. Nell took the lead while he moved the birds. Through the metal wall she heard a brief scrape, distinct from the cooling vibration. Someone on the other side had shifted something heavy.
  She thought of the cleaner's hands, red from warm water. A secret place in an inhabited harbor could not survive because nobody knew about it. It survived through all the people who knew one ordinary detail and chose not to make it an announcement.
  At the weighing office, a woman with a red kettle opened a storeroom for the animals. She asked for no payment. She did ask them to bring back the bucket.
  Nell returned to the panel alone. This time she put her hand near the joint without touching the fastening.
  She did not open it.
  She left a paper beneath the edge instead.
  We have animals needing shelter. We smell growing things. We will not bring officials without asking.
  She signed her own name rather than Elian's.
  When they returned after feeding, the paper was gone. In its place lay a leaf no longer than Nell's thumbnail, silver on one side, green on the other.
  Wrapped around it was a reply.
  Do not bring the animals here. The pollen is not safe for all of them. Come without a receiver. Two people. Third cooling cycle.
  Renn read it twice.
  “They've been listening.”
  “So have we.”
  He looked toward the goat, now eating contentedly from a stranger's bucket.
  “She's going to be unbearable if she finds out she was right.”
  Nell laughed, unexpectedly and too loudly. For a moment she remembered being young enough that an unexplained door was mainly exciting.
  Then she folded the warning into her pocket.
  The door might contain a garden. It might contain frightened people. It almost certainly contained both.
  She would have to arrive as someone who understood the difference between finding a place and being entitled to enter it.

  The reply named no emergency. There were questions, records to compare, and people who wanted a safe place to speak.
  Elian sent the message to the next watch and returned to the table. Sella was closing the Bible. Anet had begun writing a letter and stopped after the greeting. Renn was explaining to Lio why a goat would eat paper without endorsing what was written on it.
  The child's laughter reached the window just as the external receiver chimed. Elian waited for the sound to finish before checking the message. The meeting could be arranged with enough time to ask, to answer, and to set a place.
  Outside, the distant vessel answered the berth request.
  Inside, there was room for another plate.
  Elian reached for one and set it beside the bread.

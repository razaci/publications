Chapter 1
The Numbers That Do Not Match
The first thing Lazara Moreau found beneath the pyramid was a child's mitten.
  It lay palm upward on the receiving quay, small and red and knitted badly at the thumb. Someone had tried to mend it with blue yarn. Water dripped from the ceiling twelve feet away, striking an empty bucket at irregular intervals. The mitten was dry.
  She put a survey flag beside it without touching it.
  "Crew been bringing children down here?"
  Tarin Wells stood at the foot of the access stairs. Her orange coat had an old rectangular patch where an employer's name had been removed. "Not our crew."
  "Somebody's crew."
  Tarin looked past her toward the dock. "You should see the boat."
  Lazara had seen photographs. She had signed a confidentiality agreement in a Fairbanks office that smelled of carpet shampoo. She had spent the flight reading a survey supposedly completed in 1998, twenty-eight years before Asterion Survey acquired the site. That survey described the missing pyramid cap, the fracture across the eastern apron, and a wooden vessel under the base. It gave the vessel's dimensions to the inch.
  Now the boat occupied the dark in front of her.
  Calling it a boat had been easier on paper. It was longer than the city block where she had once lived with her daughter. Its timber sides rose through the cavern like the wall of an apartment building, except the wall curved inward beneath a long roof. Wooden shores supported the belly above a dry channel. Walkways clung to the dock. The side door stood open.
  An hour earlier, on the surface, she had tried to make the pyramid disappear by approaching it from the wrong angle. The western face dissolved into talus and spruce. From the north, frost fractures disguised the courses. A natural hill could have resembled it. Plenty of geological features became structures when photographed by someone determined enough.
  Then she had reached the broken apron and found a drain with a clean-out pocket.
  The pocket was the size of a large laundry sink. Its removable cover lay beside it. A channel approached at a steady grade, turned through a broad curve that reduced silting, and passed beneath a surviving wall. People made that sort of curve when they expected someone else to have to clear it in winter.
  She had crouched there longer than she spent looking at the pyramid itself.
  "What convinces you?" Tarin asked.
  "A place for the person with the shovel."
  Tarin nodded. "My father would have liked that."
  They had followed a modern access tunnel down through fractured casing, past new supports the company's crew had installed after discovery. Lazara checked their inspection tags. Proper dates. Proper signatures. Ordinary engineering held the extraordinary place open, at least so far.
  At the second landing a worker named Niles had handed her a hard hat and asked whether he should keep running the pump overnight. Its hose disappeared down a modern trench beside the old channel.
  "What's your inflow?"
  He gave her the figures. They were small but increasing after the rain. She asked him to log the level every two hours and keep a second escape path clear. He looked relieved to have a question somebody knew how to answer.
  Now, facing the vessel, she held on to that exchange. Whatever the boat was, they were still people who needed pumps, escape routes, and records that admitted what had actually happened. Wonder could wait until the night shift had safe stairs.
  No metal bands. No bolts holding the great courses together. The joints were darker rectangles set into dark wood.
  She ran out a steel tape with Tarin holding the other end. They measured accessible reference points, then checked the survey traverse with the laser equipment outside the hull. Four hundred fifty feet long. Seventy-five wide. Forty-five high.
  "Three hundred cubits," Tarin said.
  "If you use eighteen inches."
  "That's what the old survey uses."
  Lazara looked up. "You've read it?"
  "Parts."
  Nothing about Tarin's face invited the next question.
  At the opening Lazara knelt to examine the sill. Twelve feet wide, ten high, precisely where the survey placed it: seventy-five feet from the end designated H-zero. The wood smelled faintly of pitch and rain. Not rot. That troubled her more than decay would have.
  She had expected a fraud or a ruin. Frauds had screws. Ruins had fungus.
  Inside, a central aisle divided empty compartments. Pale light reached down from narrow openings near the roof and was redistributed through shafts in the floors. It was almost enough to work by. The finished middle floor stood sixteen feet six inches above the bottom reference, leaving generous height both above and below it. Somebody had built this to hold weight and permit people to move that weight without killing themselves.
  Lazara understood that sort of care. She mistrusted it when she could not find its author.
  At the forward partition, around an ordinary rectangle of wood, ran a six-inch band of translucent resin. The color reminded her of honey held against a window.
  She pressed her palm to the panel. Cool. Solid. Beyond it, for an instant, came the smell of cut grass.
  "Ventilation?" she asked.
  Tarin remained in the doorway, sixty feet behind her. "You tell me."
  Lazara measured the resin twice. On the second measurement the tape stopped retracting. She gave it a little shake. Somewhere beyond the wall, a similar little shake answered.
  She put the tape away.
  Her last disaster had begun with a small discrepancy she had called acceptable. The slope above the access road had moved at 4:17 in the afternoon. Two men had died. For six years afterward she had been able to recall the minute without recalling what she drank that evening.
  Eleven months sober now. She wrote the resin measurement in a fresh fieldbook and underlined it twice.
  Behind her Tarin said, "Company consultant lands at four. Bringing his boy."
  Lazara turned. "Down here?"
  "Boy'll stay at the lodge. That's the arrangement."
  At the quay the bucket rang. Another drop. Another. A longer pause.
  Lazara went outside to photograph the mitten before the next crew disturbed it.
  It was no longer beside her flag.
  In its place lay a dark wet outline, the exact shape of a child's hand.
  

Chapter 2
Alex Does Not Speak
Elias Hart arrived holding a duffel bag, two coats, and a paper cup he had forgotten was empty. His son carried one small wooden horse.
  The horse had four square legs. Someone had drawn its eyes with a pen. The boy held it carefully around the body, as if a living horse that size might bruise.
  "Alex," Elias said, "this is Dr. Moreau. She tells rocks what they're made of."
  "Usually they tell me."
  Alex examined her boots. Nine years old, the briefing said. She disliked that someone had thought a child's medical and family history belonged in a project briefing. She disliked more that she had read it.
  His mother had died sixteen months earlier. A car accident on a wet road. Alex had survived. Since then speech came rarely, sometimes a word to his father, more often a gesture or a written request. He was seeing people at home who knew how to help. None of them had suggested an underground expedition.
  "Our caregiver canceled," Elias said when the boy went to look through the lodge window. "The lodge has a cook who supervises the afternoon group. There are two other families. He won't be on an active site."
  He sounded like a man finishing an argument he had already had with several people, including himself.
  "I'm not your supervisor," Lazara said.
  "No. Sorry."
  "But don't promise a child a safe afternoon until you've checked the railing. The lodge stairs have a loose one."
  He put down the duffel and went to check it.
  That was the first thing she liked about him.
  He returned carrying a loose brass screw between finger and thumb.
  "Wrong size," he said. "Someone kept tightening it. Wood's stripped."
  He asked the lodge manager for a longer screw and a hand drill, then waited while Alex inspected a shelf of board games near the reception desk. There were maps on the wall, old photographs, a jar of free pencils. Lazara watched the boy arrange the pencils so their unsharpened ends aligned. He left a green one crooked. Deliberately, she thought, after seeing his hand hover over it and withdraw.
  Elias repaired the railing while the manager talked about fishing conditions. He did not explain his degree or suggest the repair was beneath him. When he finished he asked Alex to help test it, with the manager holding the bottom end. The boy pulled once, harder than expected. The rail held.
  "Your quality inspector's strict," Lazara said.
  Alex looked at her face for the first time. A brief glance, measuring whether the remark had been about him or for him. She did not add anything.
  Later she found him beside the lodge aquarium. It held two ordinary fish and a plastic castle tipped into the gravel. He had opened his notebook to a drawing of the tank and was repairing the castle on paper, giving it a stair that could reach the upper window.
  "Better access," she said from a distance.
  He nodded without looking away.
  She sat on the chair beside the tank, leaving the nearer one empty. For several minutes they watched a fish investigate the same corner. Lazara had forgotten what it felt like to be permitted to share silence with a child without needing to fix it.
  When Elias came to fetch him, Alex showed him the drawing. His father admired the stair, then asked whether they should draw a fish-sized door. Alex shut the notebook, not angrily, simply finished. Elias accepted the end of the conversation.
  Lazara had once filled every silence with Claire because silence seemed like an accusation. She watched Elias carry his empty cup to the kitchen and thought about how much parenting occurred in the things a person managed not to insist upon.
  At dinner he cut Alex's chicken without asking and then realized the boy had drawn his plate away. Elias stopped, put down the knife, and let him do it. Lazara pretended not to notice. Across the table Miriam Cho spread three printouts beside a bowl of potatoes.
  "No work at meals," Tarin said.
  "These are recreationally alarming."
  Miriam held a doctorate in geophysics and was completing another graduate research project because, she said, one field had not provided enough administrative misery. Her printouts compared an old plan to new scans. The outlines coincided so perfectly that the pages looked duplicated.
  "No scan noise?" Elias asked.
  "Not on the old one. That's my problem."
  Alex drew on the back of a paper placemat. A rectangle, another rectangle inside it, two short marks at the base.
  "Window?" his father asked.
  The boy shook his head.
  "Door?"
  A nod.
  "What's there?"
  Alex added six steps and a circle beyond them. Elias opened his mouth again, then closed it. The restraint cost him something. Lazara could see it.
  After dinner she found him on the porch attempting to remove a splinter from the wooden horse. He had fine strong hands with an old scar crossing one knuckle. He wore his wedding ring.
  "Made it?" she asked.
  "His mother did. She taught elementary art. Terrible horse. Excellent teacher."
  He smiled at the animal without expecting Lazara to smile back.
  "Ruth," he said. "Her name was Ruth."
  Lazara sat on the far end of the porch bench. "My daughter's Claire. She's twenty. Lives in Oregon."
  "You see her much?"
  "Not yet."
  He understood enough not to ask what that meant.
  Below the lodge the hillside descended toward the fenced shaft entrance. This far north the summer night came reluctantly. The damaged pyramid showed as an angular shoulder beyond the trees, too regular to be comfortable once one knew what to look for.
  Elias said, "Did you really find a mitten?"
  "Tarin told you?"
  He took a folded sheet from his pocket. A photograph, printed before he came. Alex in a yellow coat, smaller than he was now, beside a woman with wind-blown hair.
  On his left hand was a red mitten. Blue yarn circled the thumb.
  "Lost it on a family trip," Elias said. "Three years ago. In Washington."
  From inside the lodge came a small wooden clatter. Alex had dropped his pencil.
  Lazara looked through the window. His drawing now contained two figures standing in the doorway. Neither was a child.
  

Chapter 3
The First Seam
The following afternoon a storm closed the ridge trail, canceled the lodge children's excursion, and sent everyone indoors. Elias came down to the receiving quay to tell the team he was taking Alex home on the next available flight.
  Lazara was standing in front of the resin frame.
  "Good decision," she said.
  "You say that like you were about to make it for me."
  "I was working up to it."
  He laughed once. Then she moved aside to let him examine the partition, and the wood vanished.
  There was no opening motion. No hinges turning. There was simply a corridor beyond the rectangle where wood had been. Long pale boards, square frames receding into distance, a dark waiting strip along one side.
  Elias stepped back. The partition returned.
  Neither spoke.
  Lazara beckoned him forward with two fingers. He came to stand beside her. Again the corridor existed.
  In the resin appeared four lines, darker than the material around them.

  It did not command them. It waited.
  They went back for Tarin and Miriam. They tried every sensible explanation and several foolish tests. Tarin could see the open corridor when Lazara and Elias stood together. Once it had been opened, she could cross the first threshold independently. Miriam measured from the bench to the second frame using a cotton line. Thirty feet.
  When Lazara stepped away, a soft tone sounded and the resin turned amber. The return stayed open, but the corridor's side passages became walls. It was a warning, not yet a trap.
  They returned, logged the test, and left Alex with Mrs. Bell at the lodge. Tarin agreed to remain at S01. Miriam followed Lazara and Elias along the Common Spine, counting frames and marking their passage in pencil. They stopped at S10 and climbed the enclosed timber stair from Deck C past H and L to G.
  The door there was twelve feet wide and ten high. Lazara approached alone. Solid wood. Elias alone: the same. Together they felt a cool breath touch their faces.
  The door opened onto sunlight.
  Six planted terraces descended toward a basin. Paths divided around fruit trees whose leaves were moving in a breeze with no visible source. Far overhead, a pale volume of blue extended to a ceiling they could not see. The space was enormous, unmistakably larger than the vessel that contained its entrance.
  Miriam remained on the landing. "I have the door. Don't go far."
  They went twenty yards. Stopped. Another twenty, with Miriam still plainly visible behind them. Elias touched a leaf. A bead of water rolled onto his thumb.
  "Real?" he asked.
  "Wet," Lazara said. "Start there."
  Beyond the landing, the first path divided at a low timber rail. One branch descended in broad traverses gentle enough for wheeled carts. Another followed steps beside a narrow watercourse. They chose the traverses. Even frightened, Elias noticed the rail's lower member, placed where a small child would try to slip underneath.
  "Someone thought about it," he said.
  Lazara followed his gaze. "Someone had to keep thinking about it. Things like that don't stay safe because the first builder cared."
  At a junction they found a board holding two wooden markers. One showed a leaf. The other a water cup. When she moved the leaf marker, a maintenance gate opened into a strip of planting beds. She returned it immediately to its former position and logged what she had touched.
  In that small service strip weeds crowded the cultivated stems. A pruning basket lay on its side. One handle had been repaired with tightly twisted cord. The garden was not a perfect vision. It needed work.
  Elias crouched beside a leaking joint. Water dripped into a carefully positioned stone bowl and overflowed into the path.
  "Don't repair it," Lazara said.
  "I know."
  "You had the face."
  "There's a face?"
  "A very specific one. Men in hardware stores get it just before they buy something expensive."
  He looked up at her and laughed. It happened so unexpectedly, in such impossible surroundings, that she laughed too. At the landing Miriam lifted a hand to demand an explanation. Lazara lifted hers back: safe, wait.
  Then she realized they had exchanged that small practical gesture as though this place already belonged among the things a day could contain. It frightened her. Not because the garden seemed hostile, but because she could imagine becoming accustomed to it.
  A drop struck the bowl. Somewhere below the terraces another drop answered. The two sounds differed by just enough to make her wait for the next pair.
  Two seats faced the basin, near enough to the upper terrace to remain in view of the entrance. One was cool. The other retained the warmth of a recently occupied chair.
  In the shallow water lay a black drawing of the Ark. A side elevation, then six carrier decks, then a room below the garden where no room had been marked on the survey.
  Elias knelt. Lazara knelt beside him.
  Their reflections remained upright.
  In the water, the reflected Elias reached toward the reflected Lazara and took her hand. The real man did not move. Neither did she.
  Then the image broke into rings. Beneath the basin, somewhere in the timber, someone knocked twice.
  

Chapter 4
Ordinary Rooms
They did not tell Alex about the garden that night.
  Lazara lay awake knowing that omission would one day need an explanation. They had spent the evening recording every measurement they could defend. A corridor existed. A garden existed. Together she and Elias could open two particular connections. Other people could use those connections while the pair remained within the registered domain. When either tried to leave, the route warned them before becoming unavailable.
  Those were observations. Nothing in them explained a mitten or a reflection with its own intentions.
  At breakfast Elias said, "No more until we understand the ordinary structure."
  "Agreed," Lazara said.
  Miriam stared at them. "There's a garden in a boat."
  "There are also stairs in a boat," Elias said. "I'd like to know if they hold people."
  They spent two days surveying the physical hull. The work steadied them. Three finished floors: one foot six above the bottom datum, sixteen feet six, thirty-one feet six. Seventy-two feet of clear ordinary width within the timber sides. Forty-five feet outside from bottom reference to roof crown. Every measurement remained stubbornly reasonable.
  The lower floor held pens separated by removable wooden divisions, feed passages, stone-lined drainage runs, and doors wide enough for animals to turn without being forced backward. Elias showed Lazara the smooth worn corner of a feeding trough.
  "Not decorative. Look where it polished."
  She put her hand there. An animal had rubbed against that corner for a long time. More than one, probably. The thought of living warmth in the immense empty compartments moved her unexpectedly.
  Above, they found storage racks, sleeping bays, a food room, and a small place for mending clothes. The roof vents were protected by layered wooden baffles. Some latches showed fresh pale wood beneath the old dark surface, as though recently handled.
  They tested how people would actually live in the rooms. Elias carried two empty feed baskets along the lower service aisle while Lazara walked toward him with a folded plank. They could pass without turning sideways. At a pen entrance he stopped, held the baskets out, and demonstrated why a corner had been cut back rather than brought to a square point.
  "An animal in the turn would need somewhere to put its shoulder. You could get pinned otherwise."
  "You sound certain."
  "Ruth's uncle kept cattle. I once helped move one that didn't want my help. We both learned something."
  "What did the cow learn?"
  "That I could jump a gate."
  They found a larger bay whose removable partitions allowed it to become a treatment or cleaning space. A channel in the floor led to a settling box accessible from the passage, sparing whoever cleaned it the need to enter an occupied pen. Above it, a narrow storage loft held slatted racks for dry bedding.
  Lazara sketched the sequence of use. Food in one side. Waste out the other. Sleeping space apart from wet work. Whoever had made it understood that caring for living bodies was mostly repeated labor. No grand symbol could make that labor disappear.
  On the upper story the food room opened into a common space with benches fitted against the side. Light entered obliquely and struck a worn patch on the floor. Elias sat there to test the sight line. From the bench a person could watch both the sleeping-bay entrance and the top of the stair.
  "Parent's seat," he said.
  Lazara pictured a woman mending a shirt while children crossed the room, animals shifting below, weather doing whatever weather did beyond the boards. The scene was not hers to remember, yet it felt more convincing than the great dimensions.
  She wrote occupied design beside the drawing. It was the nearest phrase she had for a structure whose empty rooms retained the practical shape of other people's lives.
  At the end of the survey Elias asked her to check his notebook. He had distinguished the hull floors from the six carrier decks in bold letters, added the external datum, and marked every inaccessible volume unresolved.
  "No gardens in the load diagram," she said.
  "If one starts leaning on a beam, I'll add it."
  Nobody said ancient. Nobody said Noah in the report. Tarin said it once, softly, when she thought the others were beyond hearing.
  On the quay Elias unfolded a pocket Bible. The copy was Ruth's, its soft cover kept inside a plastic sleeve. He read the dimensions aloud from Genesis, then closed it.
  "She liked things that survived because somebody bothered to build them properly."
  "Did she believe this happened?"
  "Yes. She'd still have asked who sharpened the pegs."
  They ate lunch on an overturned packing case. He had remembered that Lazara disliked mustard. She had brought a second thermos because his always leaked. Neither acknowledged the arrangements.
  Tarin allowed Alex onto the receiving quay during a scheduled all-clear, accompanied by Mrs. Bell. He remained outside the vessel. He studied its side, then drew a long narrow building lying down. Six lines ran through the building; three through the surrounding hull.
  "He saw the plans at dinner," Elias said too quickly.
  Lazara let that stand. It might be true.
  When the boy went upstairs, she asked Tarin about the wooden joints.
  "You recognized them."
  Tarin took a long time putting on a glove. "My father was a surveyor. He drew things."
  "Can we see the drawings?"
  "Not today."
  Later, in the mending bay, Lazara found a scrap of blue wool caught in the lip of a drawer. The drawer was empty. She left the thread where it was and called Elias before taking a photograph.
  He came without his usual stream of explanation. He stood beside her looking at that tiny piece of color.
  "I shouldn't have brought him," he said.
  She wanted to relieve him of the sentence. She knew the danger of being offered relief too cheaply.
  "Then keep the promise you can still keep. Get him home."
  He nodded.
  Outside, the helicopter dispatcher called to say the weather delay would last another forty-eight hours.
  

Chapter 5
The Holding Room
Miriam tried to carry a phone across the first seam in a canvas pouch. Lazara and Elias stood at their two positions, and the opening remained a translucent brown sheet.
  "Maybe the battery," Miriam said.
  They removed the phone. The passage became clear.
  They tried a dead battery, a steel washer, a coin, and a spoon, recording each failed crossing from the hull side. A ceramic cup went through. Cotton line went through. Paper, wood, a stone bead. The seam admitted their ordinary bodies and clothes once zippers, watches, and other metal objects had been removed.
  They did not test anything inside a person's body. Elias struck that proposal from Miriam's list before she could finish saying it.
  "We're not a laboratory for a door."
  He said it gently. She crossed it out herself.
  They converted an empty compartment near the forward partition into a holding room. Each person received a numbered shelf and a cotton work coat fastened with wooden toggles. The return ledger stayed on the hull side, where electronic timestamps could be checked. Inside they would use cotton survey line, pencil records, small sand timers, and the local water clocks they had noticed set into the carrier's walls.
  The conversion took most of an afternoon. They were not tailors. Tarin's coat fitted properly because she altered it herself; Miriam's hung unevenly, and Elias's sleeves ended above his wrists. Lazara had to exchange her boots for a pair of canvas work shoes with stitched soles. Her feet felt oddly exposed despite the thick wood beneath them.
  "No heroics over a forgotten zipper," she told the team. "If the boundary won't clear, we return and check clothing. We do not push."
  Miriam made a checklist. Tarin shortened it until a frightened person could actually use it. Then Miriam put back two lines Tarin had removed because the distinction between entering and leaving mattered. The argument lasted twelve minutes and improved the result.
  Alex watched from the quay as a contractor passed them a box of clean cotton garments. The boy raised a hand to catch his father's attention and pointed toward Elias's neck. A little metal fastening remained on the collar of his undershirt.
  Elias examined it, gave Alex a solemn thumbs-up, and went back to change.
  Lazara thanked the boy without calling him their special inspector or making more of it than a useful observation. He seemed pleased by that. He went back to drawing a boat whose underside had many more supporting timbers than his first version.
  The ledger demanded work too. They wrote a departure record, tested a short crossing, and checked it against an arrival record independently kept by the person at the other end. A smudged digit made the first attempt ambiguous. They repeated it. Miriam wanted duplicate books. Lazara agreed, so long as one copy stayed outside any changing time domain.
  "We have to be able to disagree with ourselves later," she said.
  Tarin added the sentence to the inside cover.
  At the end of the afternoon they had a room nobody would have photographed for publicity: shelves, spare clothes, cloth bags, a water jug, pencils tied to boards, and a large handwritten warning about checking the return. Lazara found it reassuring. Every extraordinary place deserved a corner where people admitted their capacity to make ordinary mistakes.
  The wall clocks had columns of falling drops, rotating marked wooden drums, and ceramic pivots. Elias could follow some of the action. The rest depended on a fine translucent membrane he refused to touch.
  "We'll call them clocks because that's what they show," he said. "We'll leave how for another day."

Chapter 6
S01 and the Bench
They waited until June seventeenth before opening the partition again.
  No voice came in the intervening hours. The company operations manager, Calder Voss, called from Anchorage and requested an explanation for the delay. Lazara gave him one: unexpected structural access hazard. It was accurate. She found it insufficient even as she said it.
  "You have a finite weather window," Voss said.
  "Then we have a finite survey."
  He let a silence develop, one of the small techniques she had seen used in offices by people who mistook waiting for authority. She waited too. Eventually he asked for an updated hazard report.
  At ten the next morning the return plate matched the external date. They opened the first seam. The eighteen-foot bench at S01 was empty except for a folded cloth. In the shallow baggage compartment behind the return desk lay the lost red mitten.

Rooms had thresholds, floor loads, drainage, places where people had worried about wet boots.

  "You're doing that thing," he said.
  "What thing?"

The Quiet Room

Chapter 9
Walking Time
They made rules on paper large enough to read when frightened.
  No one walks alone. Check every return before crossing. Do not substitute a remembered doorway for a verified address. No child enters an untested route. A closed route is not evidence of an empty destination.
  Tarin added: No promises on behalf of doors.
  Elias read that line twice and signed beneath it.
  The Common Spine ran between twenty-four numbered stations at thirty-foot pitch. From S01 to S24 was six hundred ninety feet. At a reference pace of three feet per second it took three minutes fifty seconds, without stops. Elias and Miriam checked it with a sand timer calibrated against the hull-side clock before each run. Their footsteps sounded close and ordinary. Farther rooms offered unreasonable views.
  At S12, the common table hall stood empty. Four long tables. Serving hatches in one wall. A band of translucent panels high above, lit from a court they had not yet entered. Chairs tucked in neatly.

  "You turned it wrong," Tarin said.
  Miriam turned the timer over. They tested it. Correct.
  On the return they kept moving. Three minutes fifty seconds. At S12 Miriam looked straight ahead, a visible effort.
  They tested a less dramatic explanation before accepting the anomaly. A warped floor might slow a walker. Anticipating a stop might alter the pace. The sand timer might behave differently near the serving wall. They carried two timers, reversed the order of walkers, and measured the corridor with cotton line held level at marked heights.

The Rib Court

  Tarin called on Sundays, sometimes to complain that her father kept trying to repair things before his hand was strong enough. Their ages had become a running source of humor neither had been ready to find funny at first. Sam was fifty-three by his own lived count. Tarin would soon be fifty-two. He still tried to tell her what boots to wear in bad weather. She still told him to mind his business, then checked his medication schedule before hanging up.
  There were difficulties they did not joke about. Friends he expected to visit were dead. His wife had died in 2007. Tarin had to tell him and stay with him through the answer. She could not give back his missing years. He could not apologize them out of hers. They began with breakfast on a real date and went on from there.
  Miriam postponed her graduate project and wrote a careful observational record instead. The official survey described a damaged installation, a wooden vessel of the stated dimensions, unexplained internal access events, and living people whose accounts required further investigation. She did not pretend an ordinary report could prove impossible mechanisms. She also refused a request to remove the witnesses because their testimony complicated the asset description.
  Voss lost the site contract. He kept working with the displaced households. Lazara did not forgive his dangerous decision on behalf of everyone it had endangered. She acknowledged the work he did afterward. They were separate matters, and both belonged in the record.
  The Court's maintainers sent a message each month through their authenticated relay. Markets open. Water stable. No new mineral movement. Mara's youngest had lost a tooth. Dan's bees were thriving in the orchard. Someone wanted to know whether outside people really put fruit in tins and then paid more for it.
  Anet learned to take the bus. She hated the bus schedule, loved the library, and said modern bread had been made too easy to squeeze. She cooked on Saturdays for anyone willing to chop vegetables. Ve worked with a local gardening group and refused to answer questions about her age. The cat belonged to neither woman when asked and to both when fed.
  Lazara kept the wooden peg in a drawer beside her fieldbooks. It did nothing demonstrable. Sometimes she took it out and felt the ordinary grain beneath her thumb. She did not make a ritual of it. There were other people she could call now when a difficult evening lengthened.
  On the anniversary of the road collapse she phoned the families she had been permitted to contact. One took the call. One did not. She went to her meeting afterward. She remained sober that day. The next morning was another morning in which to do the work.
  Claire answered her birthday message with more than two words.
  Coffee maybe, if you're down this way in February. Just us first?
  Lazara read it once. Put down the phone. Read it again.
  She did not book a whole week or write a speech about forgiveness. She answered: Yes. I'd like that. You choose the place.
  Then she called Elias and cried while he listened.
  The following afternoon Claire called unexpectedly. Lazara was in a grocery store staring at two kinds of flour she did not know how to distinguish. She answered in the baking aisle and stood very still while other shoppers moved around her.
  "I just wanted to say February isn't a promise about everything," Claire said.
  "I know."
  "I don't think you do. You get excited and then there's a whole plan."
  Lazara looked at the flour bags. Her first impulse was to explain how carefully she had been trying not to do that. She let the impulse pass without making her daughter hear it.
  "Coffee," she said. "At the place you choose. Nothing added unless you ask."
  Claire breathed out. "Okay."
  They spoke about the weather. About a course Claire was taking. A neighbor's dog kept getting into her recycling, and she had started bringing the bin inside the gate. Lazara listened to the ordinary details with almost painful gratitude. She wanted to say she had missed this so much. She did not make the moment carry the weight of everything it had interrupted.
  "Are you still seeing that man?" Claire asked.
  "Elias. Yes."
  "Is he nice?"
  Lazara smiled at the flour. "He fixes things and forgets his tea. He's kind to his son. He can be stubborn."
  "That sounds like a person."
  "He is."
  Claire laughed briefly. Then said she had to go. Lazara let the call end without reaching for one more question.
  When she got home she found she had bought both kinds of flour. She put one in the cupboard and carried the other to Elias's house. They made bread with it on Saturday, following Anet's instructions badly enough to invite comment. Alex shaped a small loaf like an animal whose species nobody could determine. They baked it anyway.
  That evening, while they washed bowls at the sink, Lazara told Elias what Claire had said about the coffee. He handed her a clean towel and asked whether she wanted the date put on the calendar or left in her own notebook.
  "My notebook for now," she said.
  He nodded. It was a small thing, his willingness to let her keep something private without treating it as exclusion. She dried the bowl carefully and put it on the shelf.
  In January they planted two young pear trees in large tubs under the kitchen window, keeping them sheltered until the ground could be prepared in spring. Alex chose the labels. One said PEAR. The other said ALSO PEAR.
  "Excellent independent verification," Miriam said when he sent a photograph.
  On a night when the aurora reached farther south than expected, Lazara woke at three seventeen. Elias was asleep beside her. Through the half-open bedroom door she could hear the refrigerator starting, a car on the wet road, the cat worrying at a loose corner of the rug.
  Her watch lay on the bedside table. She checked it against the clock on her phone.
  Three minutes slow.
  For a moment the house seemed to wait with her. She could almost smell the garden: wet leaves, pale wood warming beneath impossible light, pears cut open beside clear water.
  She sat up. Her heart had begun to race.
  Elias stirred. "Lazara?"
  She showed him the clocks.
  He took time to look. Did not tell her it was nothing. Did not reach for his clothes as though a discrepancy entitled a doorway to the rest of their lives.
  "We write it down," he said.
  They did. Date, time, observation, uncertainty. Then she reset the watch. They checked Alex, who was asleep with one foot out of the blankets. Elias covered it. In the kitchen they made tea and sat beside the window until the racing in Lazara's chest eased.
  Beyond the glass the two pear trees stood close in their separate tubs. A draft moved through the old window frame. Their bare branches trembled without touching.
  "Would you go back?" Elias asked.
  She considered the question long enough to know she was not answering merely to comfort him.
  "If someone actually needed us. If we could verify where we were going. Together, with help."
  "Not because a room remembers what we want."
  She rested her hand over his. "No."
  He turned his palm upward. This time no reflection moved before they did.
  Far away, beneath the sleeping range, a channel house bowl clicked softly into readiness. Its local clock advanced. In the Court, a night steward checked the water and signed the log. The high refuge stood empty because its people were at home. The giant's final protective motion belonged to history now, and the stone above the city bore its own weight.
  At the closed garden door, the route plate held no false date. No voice called from behind it.
  Within, water moved through the six terraces. A pear fell into long grass. The two worn seats beside the basin cooled through the quiet interval before dawn.
  In Spokane, Alex padded into the kitchen carrying three socks and asked why anyone was awake before breakfast.
  Elias said the clocks had needed checking. Lazara put on another cup of water. The boy sat between them, sorting his socks with great seriousness, and the day began.

Chapter 1
The Appointment Nobody Made
The first notice of their arrival came while Joan was trying to find the owner of a saucepan.
  It had been left on the receiving bench with a coat inside it. The coat belonged to one of the new children. The saucepan belonged to a woman who said she had lent it for precisely one evening and had already survived enough disasters to recognize the beginning of another.
  Elian was negotiating the return of both objects when the public receiver printed a sheet.
  They had restored the printer three days earlier. Until then, route confirmations had appeared on a screen whose lowest corner was permanently dark. Nell had argued that a paper copy would be useful during a power interruption. Joan had argued that people would immediately use it to produce notices nobody needed.
  The machine delivered its first unsolicited page.
  “There,” Joan said.
  Elian took it before the child could fold it into a boat.
  The heading read ARRIVAL APPOINTMENT. Beneath it stood a long identifier that matched their carrier's recently recovered register entry. The receiving station was Lantern Junction.
  Arrival window: eleven local days.
  Occupancy status: vacant.
  Receiving instruction: recover equipment before structural processing.
  Elian read it twice. The saucepan's owner was still speaking.
  “A cupboard would solve most of this,” the woman said.
  “Yes,” Elian answered. “Please give me a moment.”
  She carried the sheet into the study room. Lena was there with Jonah's chart and a cup she had forgotten to drink from. She looked up, saw Elian's expression, and put her pencil down.
  “Someone else asking for a berth?”
  Elian laid the notice beside the chart.
  Lena read as far as the occupancy line.
  “We answered the register.”
  “We answered the receiving relay. We don't know who else received it.”
  “I watched you send the count.”
  “So did I.”
  Lena pulled her chair closer. Beneath the receiving instruction was a smaller field: LOCAL AUTHORIZATION RETAINED. Its date belonged to the week before Alis's module arrived.
  “Who authorized that?” she asked.
  Elian turned the page over. The back was blank.
  They called Esme, then Alis, then Nell. Each call required Elian to say the sentence aloud again. Someone expected their inhabited world to arrive empty. Saying it did not make the instruction clearer. It made the room smaller.
  Nell arrived carrying one boot.
  “The sole came off,” she explained when Lena looked at it. “I was on my way to Micah. What happened?”
  She read the notice standing up. Then she sat, setting the broken boot beneath her chair with unusual care.
  “Processing could mean inspection.”
  “It could,” Elian said.
  “It could also mean taking the structure apart.”
  “Yes.”
  Esme arrived with a copy of the relay log. The transmission had come through the same authenticated route as their ordinary traffic. That established where it entered their system. It did not establish that every statement on the page was true.
  Alis joined them last. She had been helping her crew remove the failed cooling assembly from their damaged module. There was a red mark along her wrist where a glove had rubbed.
  “Lantern accepts inhabited carriers,” she said. “I've seen their occupied berths.”
  “Did you stop there?” Nell asked.
  “Years ago. Before the route changed.”
  Lena touched the paper's date with her pencil.
  “This changed last week.”
  For a while they heard the kitchen through the open door: a drawer shutting, Joan telling someone that the saucepan was not communal property simply because it had spent time in a communal room.
  Elian asked Esme to send a correction through the verified relay. She asked Alis to identify which part of the new chart showed Lantern Junction. Nell would compare the arrival identifier against the carrier's physical marks. Lena would gather the authorization records already held locally.
  “And the council?” Lena asked.
  “Now. Before this becomes something they hear from a frightened neighbor.”
  Elian went to make the call. Halfway through the doorway, she stopped.
  “Please keep the original sheet.”
  Joan, passing with the rescued saucepan, gave her a look.
  “I wasn't going to cook it.”
  The council met that afternoon with representatives listening from North Terrace and West Orchard. Elian read the notice without improving its language. She explained what they had checked and what they had not.
  Someone asked whether they should stop the carrier.
  No one in the room knew whether that was possible from their local controls.
  That answer went badly. Elian understood why. People had begun accepting the carrier's movement as a discovery they could learn to live with. Now they were being told that movement might also be a delivery, and that the people aboard did not know how to refuse it.
  Nadiya spoke from North Terrace. Behind her, a familiar window showed late light across the garden court.
  “Then the first task is finding who does know,” she said. “Meanwhile we should confirm what the movement is doing to our services. We have people who cannot relocate on a day's notice.”
  A teacher asked about the school visit planned for the following week. A workshop foreman wanted to postpone a heavy repair. Farin asked which food stores could be moved without losing them. The questions gave the meeting a shape it could work with.
  They agreed to send a small delegation ahead, if Lantern would receive one. They also agreed that no delegate could authorize the relocation of a community without returning the terms for discussion.
  Nell volunteered before Elian did.
  At the edge of the screen, Nadiya closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she asked about the vessel.
  “Mercy of Morning,” Nell answered, looking toward Damas.
  He had joined late, his hair flattened on one side from a nap.
  “Subject to the route inspection,” he said. “And someone explaining why my first proper rest in a week qualified me to attend a meeting.”
  That evening Nell found Micah in the workshop, holding her boot upside down. He had scraped away the loose adhesive. The separated sole lay beside a clamp.
  “I can fix this,” he said.
  She sat on the stool near his bench.
  “I'm going ahead to the junction.”
  “I heard.”
  “I'm telling you anyway.”
  He nodded and set the boot down.
  She had expected questions about the journey. He asked when she wanted to leave, then whether the council would cover her survey work while she was away. She told him what had been arranged. He listened without pretending the arrangements made him happy.
  “The cabinet will still need its shelf,” he said at last.
  “I know.”
  “I want you to know I'm asking about the shelf because I don't know how to ask about the other part.”
  Nell reached across the bench. He took her hand, and they stood awkwardly with the broken boot between them until she went around to his side.
  Later, Elian walked to the observation window. Beyond it, the gallery's distant structures were shifting against one another slowly enough that she needed yesterday's photograph to be certain. Somewhere beyond those frames lay a harbor with an appointment for their world.
  Esme found her there carrying two pages.
  “They answered.”
  Elian took the first. The correction had been received. Lantern Junction acknowledged the reported population and requested a delegation before final disposition.
  For one breath, relief was possible.
  “And the other?”
  Esme handed it over.
  An attached diagram showed their carrier's intended berth. Most of it was unfamiliar. Along one side ran a narrow transfer arm marked as decommissioned and unoccupied.
  At its far end, beside a maintenance hatch, someone had added a handwritten message before the page was scanned.
  Elian brought it closer to the light.
  We can hear the arrivals bell. Please tell them we are still here.
Chapter 2
The People in the Transfer Arm

The people in the transfer arm
The harbor asked them to extinguish their cabin lights.
  Damas read the instruction a second time, then leaned toward the forward glass. Beyond it, Lantern Junction unfolded across the darkness: white work lamps along enormous ribs, rotating amber beacons, the slow passage of a vessel carrying what looked like a forest beneath a curved pane of cloud. Its trees were no higher than eyelashes at this distance. A river caught the light and disappeared behind the harbor wall.
  Nell had known there were other worlds. Knowing had not prepared her for seeing someone else's afternoon pass outside a ship.
  “Inspection glare,” Damas said. “They want a clear view through the windows.”
  He dimmed the cabin. Elian could still see Lena's hands on the folded diagram. The handwritten plea lay across her knees, protected inside a transparent sleeve.
  We can hear the arrivals bell. Please tell them we are still here.
  They had tried the transfer arm's listed receiver six times during the journey. Each attempt had returned the same quiet confirmation: endpoint withdrawn.
  A light swept over Mercy of Morning. It passed slowly enough to reveal scratches on the glass, then went on into the dark.
  “Delegation vessel,” said a voice. “Proceed to the blue markers. Leave your propulsion available until the berth confirms attachment.”
  Damas acknowledged. Below them, three blue lights appeared on a narrow landing frame.
  Nell watched the forest carrier recede behind a shutter. For an instant she imagined standing beneath its trees, seeing a little ship arrive, wondering who those people were and whether they had brought anything worth trading.
  Then the shutter closed.
  Their own berth attached with a noise like a cupboard shutting in another room.
  A woman in a gray coat waited on the receiving platform. She held no weapon. Elian noticed that first, then disliked that she had needed to notice it.
  “I am Orra Venn, receiving registrar.”
  “Elian.”
  “I have your names. You have been assigned an interview room.”
  “We need to speak about the dismantling instruction.”
  “Yes,” Orra said. “That is why there is an interview.”
  The platform smelled of warm dust and recently washed flooring. Along the wall stood rows of empty chairs, all facing a closed window. Someone had left a child's knitted mitten beneath one of them. Nell stopped looking at it when she realized she was trying to decide how old the dust was.
  Orra led them into a room with water, four chairs, and a view of their distant carrier on a wall screen. From here their home resembled a dark seed held between metal fingers.
  Elian sat where she could keep it in sight.
  “The designation you received is under review,” Orra began.
  “Has the processing order stopped?”
  “A review has been attached to it.”
  Elian waited.
  Orra set down her tablet.
  “No. It has not stopped.”
  Lena slid the diagram across the table.
  “Who wrote this?”
  The registrar read the handwriting. Her face changed slightly; not surprise exactly, but the effort of keeping a thought from becoming visible.
  “Where did you obtain this copy?”
  “Your reply included it.”
  “That arm was withdrawn from service twenty-six harbor years ago.”
  “Was it searched?” Nell asked.
  “I wasn't working here then.”
  “Has it been searched since?”
  Orra turned the tablet toward herself. Her finger moved down a list and stopped.
  “The closure inspection was completed.”
  “By a person?”
  There was a ventilation grille above the door. Elian became aware of its steady clicking.
  “By a survey instrument,” Orra answered.
  Nell took the diagram back and laid it beside the water jug.
  “If your instrument searched our home while everyone was below the surface, what would it find?”
  “That would depend on its settings.”
  “Exactly.”
  Orra looked from Nell to Elian.
  “I can request an inspection.”
  “When?”
  “The next available crew—”
  “Put us on it,” Lena said.
  “You are visitors.”
  “Then send someone who knows the way with us.”
  Orra's tablet sounded. The registrar silenced it, but Elian had already seen the little image that appeared beside the notice. Their carrier, enlarged. A red line moving across one end.
  “What is that?”
  “A berth preparation.”
  “What does it do?”
  Orra hesitated long enough that Damas stood up.
  “It cuts away obsolete external fittings,” she said.
  Nell leaned forward. “Our water return passes through an external fitting. We repaired it ourselves. What drawing are they using?”
  The registrar was already calling someone.
  For the first time since they had entered the harbor, Elian heard uncertainty in an official voice.
  “Hold the preparation,” Orra said. “I need an engineer to compare the physical configuration.”
  Someone answered too softly for them to hear.
  “Then put my name against the delay.”
  She ended the call. On the screen, the red line stopped.
  “Four hours,” she said. “I can hold it for four hours pending evidence of an active service connection.”
  Elian wanted to thank her. She wanted to ask why water pipes were better evidence than people speaking. Neither seemed useful yet.
  “We'll need to contact home,” she said.
  Orra opened the room's receiver.
  While Elian spoke to Esme, Nell watched Lena smooth the sleeve around the handwritten plea. She had been doing it since they landed. Flattening a crease that would not stay flat.
  “You don't have to come into the arm,” Nell said quietly.
  Lena looked up.
  “Someone got a message out,” she said. “I want them to know it arrived.”
  The maintenance worker who came for them was called Pell. He carried two lamps and a coil of cable over one shoulder. At the door he looked at Orra's authorization, then looked at Orra.
  “Do you want the recorded route or the usable one?”
  “The usable one.”
  “We'll need boots.”
  Nell looked down at hers. Micah's repair was a narrow dark seam around the sole.
  “I have boots.”
  Pell looked at the others. “Good. People keep coming here dressed for floors.”
  Damas remained with Mercy to preserve their route home. Orra accompanied the others to the transfer arm. Elian had expected her to send someone junior, and revised one small part of her opinion.
  The lift took them below the bright receiving levels. Through its mesh walls they watched painted doors give way to bare supports. The harbor continued around them, immense and occupied. Pipes shook. Somewhere a pump began its heavy, uneven labor.
  At the bottom, Pell unlocked a barrier bearing a faded notice.
  NO HABITABLE SERVICES BEYOND THIS POINT.
  Warm air touched Nell's cheek as the barrier opened.
  She caught Pell's sleeve.
  He had felt it too.
  They crossed a grated passage overlooking darkness. Far below, water flashed along a channel. The lamps found footprints in the dust, then a patch swept clean around a junction box.
  Nell crouched beside a pipe.
  A small tube had been connected to its underside with three different kinds of fastener. Cloth wrapped the joint. Beneath the cloth the metal was warm.
  “Someone's taking a little heat,” she said.
  Pell knelt beside her. He touched a second line.
  “And returning the condensation.”
  “Would that show on your accounts?” Orra asked.
  “As a leak.”
  He followed the tube with his lamp. It disappeared through a hole cut neatly into a panel.
  Lena was already moving toward it when a voice said, “Please don't pull that out.”
  A girl stood beyond the next support. She held a metal bowl against her chest. Steam rose from it into the lamplight.
  No one moved.
  The girl looked at their lamps, their coats, the registrar's tablet.
  “It's for washing,” she said. “We don't drink from that one.”
  Elian lowered her lamp so the beam no longer shone in the girl's eyes.
  “We received a message.”
  “Which message?”
  Lena lifted the sleeved paper.
  The girl took one step forward, then stopped when she saw Orra.
  “You brought the office.”
  Her bowl trembled. A little water spilled over her fingers.
  Orra switched off her tablet and put it in her coat.
  “We came to find who was here.”
  “Everyone who came before said that.”
  From somewhere beyond the support, a door opened.
  An older woman appeared with a towel over one shoulder. She took the bowl from the girl without looking away from the visitors.
  “Don't tell them anything else, Tavi.”
  “We're from an arriving carrier,” Elian said. “They're calling our home empty too.”
  The woman studied her face. Then she looked at Lena's paper.
  “That was mine.”
  Lena held it out.
  The woman did not reach for it immediately. When she did, she touched the writing through its sleeve with one fingertip.
  “I sent that with a filter casing,” she said. “A man at the loading hatch promised to put it where someone would look.”
  “He did,” Lena answered.
  The woman closed her eyes.
  Behind her, the open door revealed a narrow room hung with drying clothes. There were shelves made from packing frames, a pot on a heater, someone's careful drawing of a tree. Farther back, another doorway stood open onto another lit room.
  Nell heard a baby cough.
  “How many people?” Orra asked.
  The woman's eyes opened.
  “Why?”
  “So I can record—”
  “No.”
  The word filled the passage.
  Pell looked down at his boots. The girl pressed herself against the doorframe.
  The woman folded the paper, sleeve and all.
  “The last list became a removal list. Those who had a receiving household were taken there. Those who didn't were held while their status was settled. My sister spent seven months in a room without a window. When she came back, she couldn't sleep unless somebody left the door open.”
  Orra said nothing.
  “We need help with the air,” the woman continued. “We need medicine brought through legally so it doesn't cost half our food. We don't need to disappear into your interview rooms.”
  Elian thought of the authority the council had refused to give her. She had resented its limits for perhaps ten minutes. Now she was grateful she could not offer these strangers away.
  “No one here can agree to a move for you,” she said. “I'd like us to speak somewhere you choose. You can tell us what you're willing to share.”
  The woman looked at Orra.
  The registrar took her tablet from her coat, set it on the grating, and stepped away from it.
  “I need evidence to stop a dangerous order,” she said. “We can begin with the pipe.”
  Nell released a breath she hadn't known she was holding.
  The woman considered them. Then she opened the door wider.
  “My name is Sella. Wipe your boots. We've just washed.”
  They entered one at a time.
  Inside, Tavi put another cup on the table. There were not enough chairs. Someone went through the far doorway and returned with a stool, apologizing that it leaned.
  Nell sat on it. The stool did lean. Beneath her feet the floor carried the steady vibration of the harbor machinery.
  Sella began with the ventilation filter. Pell listened. Orra asked whether she could write on paper. After a moment, Sella gave her a pencil.
  They had been talking for perhaps ten minutes when a bell sounded through the wall.
  Tavi dropped her cup.
  It struck the table and rolled. Nell caught it before it reached the edge.
  “The arrival bell?” Elian asked.
  Sella was already at a small receiver above the heater, turning its dial with fingers that had become clumsy.
  “No. We hear that every day.”
  A light blinked beside the receiver. Three short flashes, a pause, then three more.
  A man's voice emerged beneath a wash of interference.
  “Lantern service channels. If anybody receives this, please answer. Our berth has closed. We have children in the lower—”
  The voice broke apart.
  Orra reached instinctively for her absent tablet.
  “Which carrier?”
  Sella adjusted the dial. The voice returned, thinner now.
  “Do not accept the vacancy confirmation. We are aboard. We are still aboard.”
  Then the transmission stopped.
  Across the room, on Orra's abandoned tablet outside the open door, a new notice began to sound.
Chapter 3
The Bell That Was Not for Them
Tavi put the rescued cup into the cupboard and shut the door with both hands. Then she opened it again. Nell watched her move the cup to a different shelf, a useless adjustment made carefully while the adults waited for the voice to return.
  Outside, Orra's tablet sounded a second time.
  “Leave it there,” Sella said.
  “It could be the hold.”
  “Then answer it where I can hear.”
  Orra fetched the tablet without crossing the threshold. The message concerned a missed interview. A room had been reserved for their delegation, and the delegation had failed to attend. She dismissed it. For a moment she looked almost embarrassed by the smallness of the thing that had summoned her.
  Pell asked Sella to repeat the channel number.
  “Seven, under the carrier maintenance band.”
  “Seven was closed.”
  “It still carries voices.”
  He unrolled his cable onto the clean table. Sella lifted the bread away before he could be asked to apologize. The coil left a dark crescent on the cloth.
  At the receiver Nell heard a soft hiss interrupted by a regular scrape. Pell listened with his head bent, touching nothing until Sella nodded. He connected a separate lamp battery and tried the call again.
  “Occupied vessel calling Lantern. This is a party in the lower transfer arm. Say your berth number.”
  The hiss thickened. A woman's voice emerged.
  “We don't have a berth number now.”
  “What was the last number?”
  “Lower Twelve. Our display says released.”
  Pell drew a rectangle on Orra's scrap of paper. He put a cross below it.
  “Lower Twelve is outside the arm,” he said. “Separate attachment. About two galleries away.”
  The woman spoke again before he could answer her. “Please don't tell us to use the arrivals channel. We have used it. It tells us the call is complete.”
  Elian stood beside Sella. The warm room had become very quiet. Behind the inner doorway the baby coughed, and someone made the low soothing noise people made before words were needed.
  “How many people aboard?” Orra asked.
  Sella turned toward her so sharply that she stopped.
  “Ask what they need.”
  Orra swallowed. “What do you need immediately?”
  “Someone to stop the berth closing. The air plant is still on our own reserve. The water connection went yesterday. Our lower door won't open into the bridge.”
  “Your vessel's name?” Pell asked.
  “Winter Hearth. My name is Hadrin. Our steward is hurt. I can get him if you need—”
  “Stay by the receiver,” Nell said. “What can you see outside?”
  “A shutter. Blue lamps through a crack. Something moving underneath.”
  Orra opened the hold she had entered for Elian's home. Elian watched her finger pause over its scope.
  “The order I stopped names your carrier,” Orra said. “It doesn't cover Lower Twelve.”
  “How do you stop that one?”
  “I have to reach its duty controller.”
  “Reach them.”
  She did. The first controller said Lower Twelve had been cleared. The second said the first was responsible. Orra kept both voices on the line and asked which person would authorize opening a door on the basis of an old vacancy certificate. Nobody answered directly. Pell wrote down the names as she requested them.
  Hadrin's breathing filled the little receiver.
  “Are you still there?” she asked.
  Sella bent close. “We are. I'm in the arm. We have heard your bell.”
  “You're not the office?”
  “No.”
  A sound came through that might have been laughter if there had been air enough for it.
  Pell asked Tavi to fetch a cloth for the damp lead. She brought three: one clean, one for floors, one so worn that it had become a family argument about throwing things away. He chose the last and thanked her. The practical distinction restored a little color to her face.
  “Can I talk to them?” she asked.
  Sella looked at the receiver. The line hissed without a voice.
  “If somebody answers. Don't promise anything.”
  Tavi leaned toward the speaker. “There's a girl here. If there are children there, they can talk to me while you do the other things.”
  No one answered immediately. The effort had cost her more than she expected. She drew away, embarrassed, and began wiping a perfectly dry place on the table.
  Then a child said, very faintly, “Do you have a window?”
  Tavi looked at Sella. Her grandmother nodded toward the receiver.
  “No. We have a picture.”
  “Of what?”
  “A tree. What do you have?”
  “Glass. But somebody put a wall outside it.”
  Elian turned away from the table. The adults had been speaking about a shutter. A child had named what it did to a room.
  Tavi asked whether the other child had something to draw on. He did. The voice belonged to a boy called Nemm. He could not find his blue pencil. His sister had it, though she said she did not. They argued faintly in the background while a woman asked them to let her hear the service channel.
  Tavi waited. When the boy returned, he wanted to know whether people in the arm heard the arrival bell too.
  “Every day.”
  “We thought it was for us.”
  “It isn't usually for anybody we know.”
  The statement silenced her. She had lived beside a bell that announced other people's movement for as long as she could remember. This morning she had been frightened to hear another sound. Now a boy beyond a closed door had mistaken the familiar one for an answer.
  Nell took the floor cloth gently from her hand before it reached the cups.
  “We'll keep this channel open as much as we can,” she told Nemm. “If you need to leave it, tell us who is with you and where you're going.”
  “Downstairs,” he said. “My mother is downstairs.”
  “Then tell her you've spoken to Tavi.”
  The woman came back and thanked them. It was Hadrin again. She said Nemm was her neighbor's boy; his mother was helping the steward. No one was alone. The information was offered with such deliberate firmness that Elian understood how afraid Hadrin was of having the children described as abandoned.
  Tavi sat on the leaning stool. She still held the clean cloth. Sella folded it and put it beneath the cups, giving it a purpose nobody could dispute.
  Outside, the tablet sounded again. Orra reduced its volume without silencing the call she needed. The room had become a place with several kinds of waiting in it. Elian needed a ship. Hadrin needed a door. Tavi was waiting for Nemm to tell her whether he had found the pencil.
  None of those needs could answer the others. For the moment, at least, they were no longer being heard alone.
  Elian called Damas. The connection from Mercy was clearer; behind him she heard the little rattle of a loose drawer she had meant to mention since departure.
  “Can you see Lower Twelve?”
  “From here? No. What have you found?”
  She told him. He asked two questions about the attachment and one about the cutter schedule. She could answer none of them. He did not scold her. He went quiet in the way that meant he was checking his vessel rather than imagining it.
  “I can request a service movement. Someone must remain able to speak for your home.”
  “I will.”
  “And home needs to know you're doing this.”
  “Yes.”
  Sella's receiver crackled again. Hadrin said there were children below, then corrected herself: families below, some with children. An old man could not be carried sitting up. They had already moved him once. Her voice shook on the word once, as if it contained a whole argument nobody else had heard.
  Elian found Esme on the home channel and gave her the shortened account, including the part about the hold running out. Esme asked whether their own water return had been checked.
  “Not yet.”
  “Then you need two groups. You cannot be the only people in every room.”
  Nell heard it and reached for her coat. “I'll go back to the receiving receiver. Put the home crew on with me.”
  Pell looked at her repaired boot. “Take the upper stairs. That lift stops when freight crosses.”
  Lena rose with her. Sella pushed half the loaf toward them, then changed her mind and cut it first. There was no bag. Nell wrapped the slices in the clean side of her scarf.
  At the door Tavi touched her sleeve.
  “If the bell rings while you're gone,” the girl said, “who do we tell?”
  Nell looked back at the table. Orra sat with two officials on her tablet. Pell held the cable above the cloth. Sella kept a hand beside the receiver, though touching it did nothing to strengthen the signal.
  “Tell us,” Nell said. “This receiver. Mercy. The upper room. Use all three.”
  Tavi repeated the names until she had them. Then she wrote them on the cupboard door in pencil.
Chapter 4
Four Hours of Water
The home crew's first picture showed a puddle.
  Nell had asked for the external return coupling, and someone at West Orchard had sent the patch of wet floor beneath the access hatch. A pair of boots stood at its edge. Water shivered around the soles in time with a pump she could not hear.
  “Turn the lamp,” she said.
  “We know where the coupling is.”
  “I don't. I need to see how you reached it.”
  The voice belonged to Tam. He disliked being watched at work and disliked being asked to repeat himself more. He turned the lamp anyway. The image climbed through a dark hatch, found a handwheel, and settled on the folded repair sleeve they had installed weeks earlier.
  Nell remembered scraping her knuckle there. She had left a little blood on the inside lip and been angry at the inconvenience. Now the same piece of metal was a small red shape on another person's cutting plan.
  Orra's receiving room had four chairs. Lena moved three against the wall and spread the diagrams across the floor. They could not see the home screen while standing over them, so Nell knelt. Her repaired sole creaked.
  Tam put a second crew member on the call. Her name was Ula; Nell knew her from water tests and had never heard her sound so formal.
  “We'll compare at both ends,” Ula said. “You tell us what they plan to cut. We'll tell you what's actually connected.”
  They worked through the drawings. One external brace on Lantern's record was a return manifold. A supposed blank cover concealed a service passage. Another fitting really was obsolete, as far as the home crew could establish. Nell marked that too. If she colored every piece dangerous, the marks would stop meaning anything.
  Lena photographed each annotated sheet with the original visible beside it.
  The four-hour hold had two hours and nineteen minutes left when Orra called. She had found an engineer willing to examine the evidence. He wanted proof of flow rather than pictures of equipment that might be abandoned.
  Tam swore, off the screen.
  “He heard you,” Ula said.
  “Good.”
  Nell waited until the engineer came onto their channel. His name was Vennik. He was sitting in a booth with a work lamp shining into one eye. When he turned it away, he looked younger than she had expected.
  “I am not disputing the reported population,” he said. “I'm signing for this connection. If that return is already bypassed, holding this crew doesn't protect anybody.”
  Nell let go of the answer she had prepared.
  “What evidence do you need?”
  They agreed on readings the home workers could obtain from accessible instruments. Nobody opened a live line for the sake of a picture. Ula recorded the upstream figures while Tam took the return. A third worker read the separate local display aloud. One reading lagged. Nell began explaining it, then stopped because she was too far away to know.
  “Check it there,” she said.
  It took eleven minutes. The instrument had a loose connection. They recorded that fault and repeated the comparison. Vennik asked for the unedited recording. Lena sent it before the neat annotated copy.
  Outside the interview room, a woman pushed a cart of fresh drinking cups past the door. Nell realized she had not drunk since leaving Mercy. She filled one for Lena, then herself. The water tasted metallic and very cold.
  Vennik called back with a signed suspension for the preparation crew. He had copied the occupied-carrier report into its reason field. Orra's four-hour stop would expire, but the new suspension would remain until a witnessed physical comparison was completed.
  Lena sat down on the floor. “Tell home first.”
  Nell called Esme. The red line on her screen had disappeared. Tam raised his lamp in salute and went to put his tools away. Ula stayed on to send the figures in a format Vennik could retain.
  Then another message arrived.

  “It was certified at closure.”
  “How long ago?”

  The proposed bridge might stand empty. It might even stand long enough to carry several people. None of them was willing to find out which person would discover its limit.
  The bridge crew had already brought their carriage into position. Their leader said she could obtain a different support, but not within the current movement window. Cale asked whether a smaller service span could be independently braced from the arm.

  “You keep deciding we can wait,” the young man said.
  “I decided that bracket could kill you.”
  “You don't live here.”
  “No.”

  “There isn't—”
  “Four minutes. I will still know which door needs a wrench in four minutes.”

The Forest Behind Glass
The forest carrier answered Damas on its third waking bell.

  “Perhaps,” she said. “Who checked our spare load?”
  “I have figures from the harbor.”
  “Then you have last season's figures.”

  “There are people dependent on ours.”

  Ira asked him to wait.

  “What did you tell him?”
  “That someone has to look.”

Someone had left a yellow tool beside a young tree with its roots wrapped for moving. A bird called from out of sight.

  “Below the lower terrace.”

The Figure Beside the Name

Sevrin asked Elian to come without an audience.

Chapter 31
The Ship Under the Orchard
From outside, the orchard was a bulge in a wall.
  Nell searched for it through Mercy's lower observation glass while Damas held the agreed position. She knew the map. She knew the relation between the service ridge and the enclosed landscape. Her mind still expected trees.
  Instead there was a curved shell with patchwork insulation, external channels, and a pale scar where an older support had been removed. Lamps moved along a maintenance recess. Somewhere far above those lamps, fruit might be falling onto the wet ground beneath a tree she had once passed without looking up.
  “That's West Orchard?” Cale asked.
  “The edge of its service zone,” Nell said. “Not the trees.”
  He nodded. She was grateful he did not ask for the distinction to be made more impressive.
  Mercy carried Cale and a second observer from Lantern, along with Nell and Lena. Elian remained at the control meeting so home and harbor could both reach her without asking Damas to choose between flying and answering politics. Vennik's inspection craft held a different angle. Their observations would overlap but not depend on a single window.
  Damas called the turn before making it. The shell slid past the glass, too close in appearance though the distance remained within the plan. A shadow crossed a set of external ribs. Beyond them the water return emerged, bent around its support, and disappeared into the carrier again.
  Nell put a hand against the glass.
  There was the repair.
  She had seen it as a sleeve in a hatch, a damp edge beneath Tam's lamp. Here it was a strip of altered metal amid the vast exterior, a small stubborn irregularity that had carried their work where the old drawing expected an obsolete fitting.
  Lena recognized it from the recording. “That was nearly cut.”
  “Yes.”
  Cale took the observation without comment. He asked whether the lamp position could be improved without moving Mercy. Damas adjusted it. The repaired sleeve brightened. A faint pattern of discoloration showed where warm moisture had left its trace.
  The home crew came onto the channel from the internal access. Ula read the current status. Tam confirmed the hatch was clear. Their voices sounded absurdly near. Nell pictured Tam kneeling a few meters and a whole unimaginable wall away.
  “Can you hear us?” she asked before realizing the question made no sense.
  “That's why I'm answering,” he said.
  She laughed. “I mean the ship.”
  “No. Is it loud?”
  “Not from here.”
  “Then we're both having a quiet moment.”
  The observers compared the external mark with the model. Part of it was obscured, just as expected. The visible edge did not match the recorded position exactly. Cale gave the difference to Vennik, who asked for another angle.
  Mercy moved within its observation box. Nell kept watching the repair, reluctant to let it leave the window. Above it, within the shell, thousands of people were securing cups and waiting for something they could not see. She had traveled away to keep them safe. Now being this close made her want simply to be among them.
  Lena took out Jonah's chart. She did not open it. She held the folded sleeve in her lap while the carrier passed overhead.
  “Would he have wanted to see this?” Nell asked.
  “Yes.”
  She did not say he would have been proud. She could not know which of their decisions he would have argued with. She could imagine him asking whether the drawing showed the real return path, and the imagining hurt enough without being turned into approval.
  Damas slowed the image on the observation display rather than the ship. A set of marks came into view below the return, weathered by years of service that had nothing to do with weather. Nell recognized a hand-painted patch in one corner.
  “That is new,” she said.
  Tam answered from inside. “Ula's apprentice. He wanted people to see where the fastening changed.”
  The patch was bright yellow. Against the shell it looked almost absurd, a child's color on a structure too large for the eye. Cale asked for it to be entered as an observed feature, distinct from the old official mark.
  Nell remembered the apprentice. He was called Evin, and he had once spilled a testing jar on her coat. She had snapped at him, then apologized after discovering she had left the jar in the place his elbow needed to be. Now his yellow patch was giving the observer a point he could actually distinguish.
  She asked Tam to tell him it was visible.
  “He'll want a picture.”
  “Send him the unedited one.”
  Cale did. The transfer took a moment, the little file moving between people whose positions were being calculated in entirely different measures. Nell imagined Evin seeing his brushwork on the outside of the world.
  Lena looked at her. “You know everyone inside it?”
  “No.”
  “You look as though you're trying.”
  Nell laughed softly. “I know a person who knows a patch.”
  It was enough for the moment. She could not carry every household in her mind. She could remember why a yellow rectangle should remain separate from a claimed reference.
  The ship's cabin cooled as Damas reduced a nonessential service according to the holding plan. Lena put on her coat. Cale offered her a spare blanket, then accepted it back when Nell was the one who began shivering. Fear and cold had become difficult to distinguish. She wrapped the blanket over her knees and kept her hands free for notes.
  From home came an open-channel report that the infirmary's preparations were complete. The voice was calm, almost bored. Nell was grateful for the boredom until she heard a familiar child complain in the background about the place his bag had been put.
  Kit's drawing of the waiting cup lay in her notebook. She touched it without taking it out. Her brother's bad joke now occupied a real place among the records that made the operation possible for her to endure.
  Alis asked for another external view. Cale gave the coordinates. Damas checked the movement, called it, and repositioned Mercy within its allowance. The shell shifted above them, and the yellow patch moved toward the edge of the glass.
  For an instant Nell wanted the carrier to stay exactly there. Every familiar place was inside it. If it stayed, the next uncertainty would stay in the future too.
  Then she heard Ula ask for confirmation, and she gave it. Home needed a berth more than Nell needed one motionless moment beneath it.
  A bright window appeared far along the curve. Not a landscape window. A service observation port, small against the shell. For a moment Nell saw a shape move behind it.
  Tam's voice came over the channel. “Someone's waving. Is that you below?”
  Damas flicked Mercy's work light once, within the agreed signal sequence. Ula laughed. The sound crossed the distance so cleanly that Nell's eyes filled.
  Then the alignment channel called for silence.
  The far reference had been checked. The difference was real. It might come from the old repair shifting the mark rather than the carrier occupying the wrong place. It might also mean the assumed turning relation was inaccurate.
  Damas held Mercy's position. He asked that the carrier's next movement remain paused while the distinction was resolved.
  The controller said they had nine minutes before the reserved interval narrowed.
  Cale looked at the observation twice. “We need another check.”
  “Then we need it,” Damas said.
  The lamps above them remained steady. The carrier seemed motionless, an entire world suspended in the decision to wait.
  Nell looked once more at the window where home had waved.
  “Tell Tam we aren't moving yet,” she said.
Chapter 32
When the Harbor Held Its Breath
The nine minutes ended before the answer came.
  Nell watched the interval change on Mercy's display. It did not look like a catastrophe. A small field became amber, then disappeared behind a revised traffic notice. Somewhere in Lantern an ordinary schedule had failed. Somewhere above them a carrier full of people still needed a berth.
  The movement controller called Damas.
  “Can you confirm the original reference within the proposed range?”
  “No.”
  “The discrepancy may be only the mark.”
  “Then we need to establish that.”
  A second voice joined, asking whether he understood that the neighboring sea carrier could not hold indefinitely. Damas said he did. His hands remained open on his thighs. Nell had never seen him look so deliberately still.
  Inside the home carrier, Alis and Ula reached the secondary observation point. It was not where the model placed it. An old access had been covered during a repair, and the crew had to use another route. Esme asked the waiting households to remain clear of the service passage. On an open channel, somebody complained that people kept being told to wait without knowing what was happening.
  Elian answered.
  “A measurement does not match. They have stopped the approach while they check whether the reference mark or the planned position is wrong.”
  “Is the carrier in danger?”
  “The planned position has not been verified. Moving before it is would increase the risk. Holding has its own limits, and we are arranging them now.”
  Nell could hear the effort it took to leave the sentence there.
  Vennik's craft moved to a second observation angle. He and Cale compared the visible support, the repaired mark, and the newer measurements Pell had taken from the arm. They found a relation that did not depend on the damaged reference. Alis supplied the corresponding view from inside the service zone.
  The mark had shifted during the old repair. The carrier's position was close to the expected one, but the exact clearance still needed recalculation. The new geometry reduced a margin they had thought they possessed.
  Pell spoke from the arm. “The return line stays. We do not regain that margin by calling it obsolete.”
  Nobody challenged him this time.
  The revised turn would need part of the neighboring carrier's interval. Urem came onto the channel from the cooling sea. His medical transfer had already left with the escort arranged earlier. His own vessel could hold longer now, if the service connection remained within the checked range.
  Sevrin assigned a crew to confirm it. Ira supplied updated forest-carrier figures. Sovan asked whether a reduction in their nonessential processing load would help. The controller said yes, within limits, and stated them before accepting.
  The harbor did not fall silent. It filled with voices saying what they could stop, what they could keep going, what they needed someone else to watch. Lena sat beside Nell and wrote the names of the people at the different ends. The list grew beyond one page.
  A small warning sounded aboard Mercy.
  Damas examined it, then asked Cale to take the communication note while he checked the ship's service reserve. They had held longer than planned. Mercy could continue, but not forever. He gave the limit to the controller and obtained a return path before agreeing to the next observation.
  Nell loved him for saying it out loud. She would have been tempted to spend the little ship as if its endurance were gratitude.
  During the wait, Eda's voice entered the arm channel.
  The baby needed a quiet place. People from three households had gathered at the watch point because the inner rooms were too close to the support under review. She asked whether she could take him through to Vela's new room by the upper route.
  Orra checked the access, and Pell confirmed that the route did not cross the restricted survey area. Eda was cleared to go with another adult.
  Then Rennel said the shared wash tap had stopped.
  Every channel seemed to pause around the report.
  Nell looked at the return readings. They had not changed. It was not the home carrier's supply. Pell asked which tap, how long, whether the adjacent basin worked. Rennel answered, increasingly annoyed that a simple report had become an examination.
  The local crew found a valve closed for a repair already underway. Its notice had been posted on the washroom door while everyone was standing outside the room. The water would return when the work was checked.
  Nell exhaled.
  “Put the notice at the watch point,” Pell said. “People can't read through a closed door from down the passage.”
  The crew did. Rennel apologized for interrupting. Pell told him he had reported a change that needed explaining. The apology was not required.
  Damas watched Nell's hands. She had gripped the blanket so tightly her fingers had gone pale.
  “Tell me the ordinary explanation,” he said quietly.
  “A local repair. Notice in the wrong place. Nothing connected to this movement.”
  She said it twice, once for him and once because her body had not accepted it the first time.
  Cale handed her the current observation sheet. “This one is still ours.”
  The words returned the boundary. They had a particular view, a particular set of readings, a little ship with a checked reserve. They were not responsible for interpreting every sound in a harbor merely because those sounds could mean danger to someone.
  Lena asked whether the open channel could be divided for routine reports and movement calls. The controller did it, preserving the means to escalate a new risk. Eda's journey upstairs continued on the local band. The baby stopped crying. Vela answered that the room was open and the kettle on.
  Nell listened only long enough to know the arrangement had reached both ends. Then she turned to the alignment view.
  The difference was being resolved through overlapping observations now. The old damaged mark no longer carried the whole interpretation. Cale read a figure, Vennik supplied another, Alis confirmed the relation from the home access. Each knew which part belonged to them.
  Damas asked how much time the revised holding agreement left. The controller gave it with the remaining uncertainty. He compared it with Mercy's reserve, then confirmed their participation in the next interval.
  Nell put Kit's little drawing back under the strap of her notebook. Waiting had required more work than any rehearsal cup could show. It had also left the machinery still until the people at its different ends could agree what they were looking at.
  She was afraid when the sequence began again. She trusted the agreement enough to watch it without asking anyone to call the fear a mistake.
  At last the new sequence was read back.
  The carrier began moving.
  It was too large for the movement to be obvious at first. Nell watched a pair of lamps slide past a rib, waited, saw the gap widen. Through the service port Tam's light disappeared. The water return passed clear of the arm's nearest projection with the checked margin visible on two independent views.
  Pell reported from the shared wash space. No unexpected movement. Stable supports. People behind the protected boundary.
  The carrier turned toward the service berth.
  Nell had imagined final attachment as a single sound, like Mercy's cupboard-door knock. At this scale it happened through successive reports. First contact confirmed. Secondary support engaged. Environmental isolation checked. Service comparison underway. A pause while a reading was repeated. Then the return line, the one marked obsolete on the first plan, was acknowledged active and protected.
  At home someone cheered before the checks were finished. Esme asked for the channel to remain clear. Nell could hear that she was smiling and trying not to cry.
  The last confirmation came from Vennik and the home crew together.
  The carrier was attached.
  Damas closed his eyes for one breath. Then he took Mercy along the agreed return path, because a safe arrival for the world did not excuse forgetting the little ship underneath it.
  At the upper berth Elian waited with water. She handed a cup to each of them before asking for the observation record. Nell drank hers too quickly and coughed. Lena put a hand between her shoulders.
  “Home?” Elian asked Damas.
  “Attached. Not finished.”
  “I know.”
  Below, in the arm, Sella opened the washroom tap. The water ran brown for a moment, then clear. Eda filled a basin. Tavi reached for the little packet of seeds, looked at it, and set it back on the shelf.
  They would plant them when somebody had time to find a proper pot.
Chapter 33
The Morning After the Rescue
Farin's bean shelves held. The cupboard beside them did not.
  One latch failed during the final settling, and a stack of jars broke inside the closed door. Nobody was injured. A morning's preserved food became a sticky brown mess threaded with glass. When Elian reached Apple Walk, Joan was standing over it with a broom and an expression that suggested the harbor might have been less trouble.
  “You can admire it after you've moved the sound jars,” she said.
  Elian moved them.
  For an hour her work required no authority. She carried, wiped, sorted, and was corrected when she put a jar on a shelf somebody had just cleaned. Lena helped beside her. Nell had gone on to North Terrace for a brief medical and service review, promising to come back after seeing Lin. Damas slept aboard Mercy under instructions that had required three people to enforce.
  Home smelled exactly as Elian remembered. That seemed unfair. She had expected some physical trace of the journey, an altered air, a different pressure beneath her feet. Instead there was fruit on the floor and somebody asking where the clean cloths had gone.
  The reports arrived anyway.
  A storage compartment at West Orchard had shifted against an old fastening. A clinic visit had been delayed, though the patient was stable. One temporary household had lost track of a bag during the room changes. North Terrace's grounded ark remained sound, but the observation survey would need new external references now that the harbor changed what lay beyond the local display.
  Farin came in with the next inventory and stared at the broken jars.
  “Those were the older beans.”
  “We'll try to break the younger ones next time,” Joan said.
  He laughed too loudly, then sat down. Elian gave him a cup and waited while he held it.
  “I thought I'd feel better all at once,” he said.
  “So did I.”
  “I don't seem able to stop listening.”
  They sat while Joan swept. The broom made a soft repetitive scrape. A child ran past the door and was called back to put on shoes. Home had not stopped being home to accommodate their fear of losing it.
  Later Elian went to the observation window. Lantern's frame stood beyond the service glass, immense and ordinary in the stillness after movement. A maintenance carriage passed along a distant rail. She tried to identify the place Mercy had held and could not.
  Ruth joined her, leaning on the sill.
  “I heard you refused the quick berth,” she said.
  “The council refused the terms. The arm residents refused compulsory removal. The crews found another plan.”
  “And you?”
  “I wanted somebody to tell me which answer would leave me least ashamed.”
  Ruth nodded as if this were an answer she could respect.
  “Did somebody?”
  “No.”
  They watched the carriage reach a lit recess and stop.
  At the receiving bench, Elian found a bag with no name. For a moment she felt an absurd dread. The missing bag, the child without a shoe, the saucepan: the world kept beginning with small lost things.
  She opened only the outer flap far enough to find a card. It belonged to a Winter Hearth household using a temporary room at Second Shore. Their move had been chosen, then misrouted by a well-meaning porter. Elian contacted the family and waited at the bench rather than sending the bag through another series of helpful hands.
  A boy came with his grandmother. He opened it at once to check for a carved animal. The animal was there. Its painted nose had worn away.
  “Did it go on the ship?” he asked.
  “A porter brought it the wrong way.”
  “Did it see outside?”
  “I don't know.”
  He held it to the observation window before leaving, ensuring that whatever had been missed could be corrected.
  That afternoon Avel sent the next hearing schedule. The inquiry was ready to examine responsibility for the closure records, not merely the immediate rescue. Elian read it at the kitchen table, which Joan permitted provided there was no tea near the papers.
  Home was attached. The arm's longer protection was still unsigned. Winter Hearth's medical transport history had not been settled. Orra's approval remained in the record. Their own recognized status had not yet replaced the suspended salvage instruction.
  Elian packed for the trip back into Lantern.
  Joan put bread in the bag. This time she supplied a wrapper.
Chapter 34
The Correction Beside the Lie
Avel asked Orra whether she had known the arm was occupied when she approved the reconciliation.
  Orra looked toward Sella before answering, then made herself look back at the inquiry officer.
  “I had not established it.”
  “That is not quite the question.”
  “No. I had received indications. A query about clinic access. A forwarded request concerning household status. I accepted the summary that the placements had been resolved.”
  “Did you inspect the underlying records?”
  “No.”
  The room held the answer without anyone improving it.
  Sella sat beside Eda. She had come with copies of the service returns and a list of dates she had checked with neighbors. Tavi was at a temporary school visit with a resident escort. Sella had refused a suggestion that the girl give a statement that day. Adults could describe the passages she had carried messages through without making her the inquiry's face.
  Orra identified her approval mark, the scope of the bundle, the later compilations that cited it. She described the work pressure that week because Avel asked for it. She did not offer it as proof that nobody could have done better. The access supervisor's decision, the routing clerk's deferral, the work units dispatched on separate references: each entered the account beside the circumstances in which it occurred.
  Avel called the people responsible to answer separately. One disputed the timing. Another produced a record showing he had escalated a concern that his supervisor did not retain. The document altered part of the draft finding. Lena watched the revision with a relief that surprised her. Accuracy mattered even when the correction made blame harder to arrange.
  Teren testified after lunch.
  He had washed his work coat but still smelled faintly of the freight bay. He gave both his names, described the warnings, and handed over copies of the returns. When asked why he had not used the proper residential request, he held up the notice stating that the residential address was not valid.
  The question was not repeated.
  Hadrin spoke about Winter Hearth. Sel confirmed the early calls. Deren explained the medical findings she could disclose. The clinic transport office supplied the timing of Bria's request and the point at which the connection failed. Avel made no finding that earlier transport would certainly have saved her. The records did establish missed opportunities to respond, and a closure sequence that proceeded despite unanswered occupancy warnings.
  Terel chose to speak last.
  He did not tell the story Elian had expected. He described Bria correcting children's sums, repairing torn cuffs while listening to lessons, refusing to let broken chalk be thrown away until every usable piece was gone. Then he placed the little wooden counting box on the table.
  “This was hers to use,” he said. “I don't want it as evidence.”
  Avel asked what he wanted recorded.
  “That she had a life while you were failing to find the right reference.”
  The recorder entered the sentence. Terel took the box back.
  After Terel spoke, Avel asked whether the room needed a break. He said he did. Other people could continue if they wanted. He did not want his leaving to become a direction for everyone else.
  Lena walked with him as far as the corridor.
  The performer had gone. A cleaner was removing marks from the floor where the rings had landed. Terel watched the cloth move, then asked whether taking the box into the room had been foolish.
  “It was yours to choose.”
  “That is a careful answer.”
  “I don't know what answer would make it easier.”
  He nodded. “Neither do I.”
  He sat on a bench and opened the box. One of the wooden pieces was missing. He had known it since leaving Winter Hearth. The children had used it for a game after the lessons, and Bria had told him not to make a fuss. Now the gap looked to him like another thing he ought to have kept intact.
  Lena asked whether the game had been good.
  “They argued for an hour.”
  “Did they enjoy it?”
  “Probably.”
  “Then perhaps it was used.”
  He touched the empty recess. “I wish you hadn't said something reasonable.”
  “I can stop.”
  He smiled and shut the lid.
  Inside the hearing room, Meren was answering another question. Lena could hear his voice through the open door, no longer polished by the certainty with which he began. She did not go back at once. A clerk had taken over her notes. Terel had asked for company, and the record would survive without her for twenty minutes.
  When she returned, Orra was explaining why her local work should be independently checked even after the emergency. A colleague objected that this would slow ordinary requests. Orra said a second review should be assigned enough time and staff to work, rather than becoming a new excuse to delay residents' care.
  Avel asked what tasks could be reassigned immediately. The answer contained dull details: access to records, signatures, hours, who held the keys to a cabinet. Lena wrote them because consequences needed a place to live beyond the sentence that somebody accepted responsibility.
  Afterward Elian asked Orra whether she wanted company on the walk back to the office. Orra said no, then changed her mind.
  They walked without discussing forgiveness. At the records desk, Orra handed over a signing authority she would not retain during review. The clerk gave her a receipt. She checked the reference carefully, not because checking made her better than she had been that morning, but because the next person using the file would need to know where the authority had gone.
  Elian left her there, with work remaining and no audience to make it impressive.
  The preliminary findings were issued over the next two days, with a defined period for correction and appeal. They stopped the vacancy-based processing of the inhabited spaces, preserved the earlier warnings, required service access arrangements, and referred specific approvals for further review. Orra would remain available for evidence and urgent work under supervision. She would not determine the complaints against her own decisions. Staff who had taken part in the rescue were not excluded from review because the rescue succeeded.
  Sevrin objected to part of the proposed resource remedy. He said it committed loads the harbor could not reliably supply. Avel requested figures and resident consultation before finalizing that section. Sella was furious about the delay. She still preferred a checked obligation to a beautiful promise nobody could keep.
  At the public desk, a clerk offered to replace the old vacancy notice with the corrected one.
  “Keep the old one attached,” Lena said.
  “It will confuse people.”
  “It will tell them why there is a correction.”
  The clerk asked Avel. She agreed, requiring a clear date on each. The old notice stayed visible with the amended status beside it, not left current and not quietly destroyed.
  Orra watched the pages being fastened. Her hooked mark appeared in the supporting history. She looked smaller than when she had first met them, but Elian distrusted the thought. People did not become more truthful by looking punished.
  Sella passed Orra on the way out.
  “Eda's access renewal worked,” she said.
  “Good.”
  “I thought you should know.”
  She went on without an embrace. Orra stood for a moment, then returned to the records desk to answer another question.
Chapter 35
The Room Sella Chose
Sella signed the protection order on her own table.
  Avel had offered the hearing room. Sella said the table had carried enough requests outward and could carry one answer home. Rennel brought a second lamp. Eda cleared the cloth. Tavi was asked to put the seed packet somewhere a sealing stamp would not crush it.
  The order barred compulsory dispersal during the first repair and housing review. It recognized occupancy, provided routes for service access, and required individual notice and a hearing before any longer clearance. It did not certify every wall safe. It did not promise that every room could remain forever. It named the immediate hazards, the repair responsibilities, and the people entitled to participate in decisions about what happened next.
  Sella read it twice. She asked how a gate worker would know the protection was current. Orra supplied a short access sheet with a direct contact for disputes. Sella asked whether residents could obtain it without returning to the hearing office. Avel agreed to a local issuing point under two named custodians.
  The corrections took another hour.
  Then Sella signed.
  Iset came from her new room to witness it. Vela brought her son, who had recovered enough energy to be bored. Rennel tried to explain the order to him. The child asked whether he could have a piece of bread instead.
  When the officials left, the room seemed larger. Sella had expected celebration. Eda sat down, put both hands over her face, and said she had forgotten something important but could not remember what it was.
  “Sleep,” Iset said.
  They laughed softly.
  The housing choices began that afternoon. Some households accepted new rooms under terms that no longer required them to confess they had never had homes. Some kept their existing rooms during repairs. Heth wanted to move near Iset, where the route to the washroom was easier. Rennel wanted to stay and demanded that the repair schedule account for his storage business. Vela asked for visits to be included in the ordinary access roster so her old neighbors did not need a special favor every time.
  Sella chose her room for the first phase.
  “Because of your sister?” Elian asked when they were alone.
  “Because the heater works, Eda lives beside me, and I know which pipe means get a bucket.”
  Elian smiled.
  “And partly my sister,” Sella added. “But I don't want her having to answer for all my preferences.”
  Tavi came in angry from a meeting with an education officer. Someone had suggested a photograph of her holding the recovered note. It would demonstrate that children could speak up and be heard.
  “I told them I want the lift to school.”
  “What did they say?”
  “That the photograph could help.”
  Sella's face became very still. “Did you agree?”
  “No.”
  “Then that's the answer.”
  Elian went with Tavi to the education desk, at the girl's request. They asked for a route, an escort arrangement during the repair phase, and a way to report a missed lift that somebody would actually answer. The clerk had not been at the hearing and was mildly puzzled by their insistence on a return contact. She supplied one.
  Tavi declined the photograph again.
  On the way back she stopped at a public window. A carrier carrying low blue hills was moving through Lantern's far turn. She stood on tiptoe to see past a rail.
  “Are we going anywhere?”
  “Do you want to?” Elian asked.
  “I want to go places and come back.”
  Elian thought that might be the most precise description of freedom anybody had offered her that week.
  At Sella's room, Tavi found a pot on the table. Pell had brought it from a discarded catering tray, cleaned and punched for drainage by someone who knew how not to split it. Eda supplied soil. The seed packet was finally opened.
  Nobody knew which way the seeds should lie. They argued about depth and agreed to divide them between two pots so one mistake would not settle the matter for all of them.
  Sella set the pots where a little light reached them from the passage. She wrote Teren's working name on one and his full name on the other, a joke only half intended.
  The service bell sounded while they were wiping the table.
  Rennel began to rise automatically. Then the new watch worker answered from the passage, gave the arm's recognized address, and asked what was needed.
  Sella stayed in her chair.
Chapter 36
A World Declared Inhabited
The papers were heavier than Elian expected.
  Not in her hands. There were only a few sheets, sealed and copied, with the carrier's old identifier linked to the inhabited landscapes and their own authorities. The weight came from what she had been trying to make them say since the saucepan morning.
  Lantern Junction recognized the carrier as inhabited.
  The vacancy-based salvage instruction was canceled. Its source history remained preserved. Future service and movement decisions required communication with the resident communities under the stated procedures. Their council's declaration was entered as resident evidence, not as a visitor's unverified report appended to an empty thing.
  Elian read the first page aloud to the council.
  Farin asked her to read it again.
  She did.
  At North Terrace, Nadiya took off her spectacles and pressed the bridge of her nose. At Second Shore, Joan said it was about time. A child asked whether this meant the harbor would stop calling them cargo. Someone began explaining the different registers, then stopped because the child had not asked for a lecture.
  “The instruction calling the carrier empty has been canceled,” Elian said. “If another office uses it, we have a correction they are required to answer.”
  It was not as grand as the feeling in the room. It was more useful.
  She carried the originals home with Lena. The council received them at an ordinary table. Esme logged the versions. Copies went to the local receiving offices and to households who requested them. The public map board acquired Lantern's service berth and the names of the people responsible at its ends.
  There was a meal afterward. Nobody called it a feast, but Joan had saved a jar of fruit that survived the cupboard accident. Farin supplied bread that had never been near Rennel's heater. Nell came in late with Lin, who embraced Elian so suddenly that she nearly dropped her cup.
  “Thank you,” Lin said.
  Elian wanted to name everyone who had done the work. She managed two names before Lin squeezed her arm.
  “I know. You can still let somebody say it to you.”
  Elian sat down.
  The child who had asked about cargo came to the table with a box of wooden blocks.
  Elian recognized him from the day Alis's module arrived. He had been the one losing a shoe, though he denied it whenever Joan mentioned the incident. His name was Siv. He wanted to build Lantern and could not decide which block should be home.
  “They are all too small,” he said.
  Farin looked at the collection. “Use the box.”
  “The box is the harbor.”
  “Then use two boxes.”

The movement that had brought them to Lantern used inherited scheduling arrangements they had only partly reconstructed. Beyond the junction, those arrangements were no longer trustworthy enough to treat as a road.

  Alis nodded. “They used to maintain navigation material for this stretch.”
  “Used to?”
  “We ask what remains.”

  Tessa asked him what he had actually measured.
  Elian listened to the discussion without joining it. Her world had become an object with an address. It was still the place where children asked adults questions the adults could not answer.

  “Where do you want this?” Elian asked.
  “The cupboard.”
  “The repaired one?”
  “The one that knows we're inhabited.”

Chapter 37
The Map That Ended at Vey
Orra knew Vey's receiving offices better than its navigation stores.
  She said so before anybody could make her a guide to everything on the next world. Her work at Lantern had been a junction assignment. Her home office, her sister's remaining family, and most of the unfinished business she had avoided lay in Vey.

Alis laid out the documents she meant: old navigation references, segment credentials, and maintained-route information that could establish a usable onward proposal. Physical copies existed in more than one form. Possession did not grant universal authority. A route still needed people at its ends who accepted the movement and could support it.
  “Then we shouldn't call it a key,” Damas said.
  “The people holding it do.”
  “Of course they do.”

The current keeper would not transmit the relevant material in response to a general query. An inspection visit was possible. A receiving office offered Mercy a provisional arrival interval, subject to the ordinary berth confirmation closer to transit.
  Damas asked where they would hold if it failed.
  Orra found the right office and made them answer.
  The expedition proposal went home before anyone packed. It named the purpose: seek an onward navigation key, inspect available route evidence, and obtain terms for the council to consider. Delegates could negotiate proposals, not commit the whole carrier to passage or surrender residents under a promise of access.

  Three pump engineers offered to accompany the expedition. They needed replacement filters and an exchange of service knowledge with Vey's crews. Their work would give the visit a practical purpose even if the key remained unavailable. Ula was one of them. The others, Neris and Tomel, had helped check the return during Lantern's approach.

  Anet asked to come at the next council session.

She wanted to help people make their own requests at the receiving offices rather than having the delegation translate every need into a category first.
  “What experience would you use?” the chair asked.
  Anet looked down, then back up.
  “I know how much can happen after somebody decides what you are called.”

Sella would remain at Lantern during the first repair phase. She might follow later if her chosen arrangements allowed it. Tavi would stay with the community and her school route.

Chapter 38
The Cabinet Between Journeys
Micah had put the shelf in crooked on purpose.
  Nell suspected him as soon as she saw it. He stood beside the cabinet with an expression of grave professional concern, inviting her judgment. One end of the shelf sat visibly higher than the other. The rest of the cabinet was better than when she left.
  “You've finally improved the wrong part,” she said.
  “I was waiting for an independent inspection.”
  “Remove it.”
  He lifted the shelf out. It had been resting on a strip of scrap wood. Beneath it the supports were level.
  She looked at him. He tried not to smile and failed. Then she crossed the workshop and held him so hard the shelf knocked against his knee.
  “Ow,” he said into her hair.
  “Put it down.”
  “I can't reach the bench.”
  She let him move enough to set it aside. Then she held him again.
  The workshop smelled of wood, oil, and rain brought in on coats. Outside, somebody was moving a handcart along the lane. She had spent days beneath moving worlds and could not think of anything more extraordinary than being where his breath touched her forehead.
  They did not talk at first.
  When they did, it was about the boot. The repair had held through the arm, the rescue passages, the observation run beneath home. Micah examined the seam and said somebody had scuffed it unnecessarily. She told him she had been trying to look impressive to a harbor.
  “You should have taken the cabinet.”
  “It would have been declared unoccupied.”
  He laughed, then stopped when she did not quite manage it.
  They sat on the workshop step. She told him about the offer to clear the arm and the vote she could not make. He told her about hearing that home had paused outside Lantern's frame while nobody nearby could say whether the pause was planned. Nadiya had explained it carefully. Careful explanation had not made him less aware that Nell was in the little ship beneath it.
  “I hated you a little,” he said.
  She looked at him.
  “Not for doing it. For being where I couldn't reach.”
  “I hated the receiver when it wasn't you.”
  “That doesn't sound like the same thing.”
  “No.”
  He turned his hands over on his knees. A small cut crossed one knuckle. She wanted to know when he had done it, how he had washed it, whether it hurt. Such a ridiculous inventory of concern after all the injuries she had seen. She took his hand anyway.
  “I am going to Vey,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “I want to tell you before somebody else does.”
  “I appreciate the novelty.”
  She let the remark hurt where it belonged.
  They discussed the journey without making either a villain for wanting a different thing. Micah's workshop exchange application was still possible, but not approved. He would not board Mercy as unofficial cargo because the relationship made waiting difficult. Nell would not call his remaining home proof that he cared less about the wider world.
  They chose a visit before departure and a message arrangement for Vey. If the local relay failed, Damas would carry a timed acknowledgment to the next working station when safe. Nobody promised contact at every hour. A missed call would be explained when explanation became possible. Nell repeated that part herself.
  “What about afterward?” he asked.
  She looked through the workshop window at the cabinet.
  “I want a place with you in it.”
  “Here?”
  “I don't know yet.”
  He nodded slowly. She could see him wanting an answer more complete than the one she had. He did not pretend the uncertainty pleased him.
  “I want that too,” he said. “I also want to stop living as if my days are the space between your departures.”
  She squeezed his hand.
  “Then tell me what you're doing tomorrow.”
  He told her about work. A real commission, a set of shelves for the infirmary storage, a disagreement with Beth about hinges. She asked questions until the tasks acquired their proper size. He asked about the filter tests and the navigation records. Their lives moved toward one another in details, less grand than a promise and harder to fake.
  Lin came to the workshop carrying two coats, one belonging to neither of them.
  “Your father says this is yours,” she told Micah, holding up a brown one.
  “It isn't.”
  “He said you'd say that.”
  Nell began laughing. Lin put the coat on a hook with the resignation of a woman who knew an object could spend years in a family without anyone acknowledging ownership.
  She looked at her daughter, then at the little bag Nell had set beside the bench. The Vey journey had acquired its own timetable. Lin knew it. She still looked at the bag as if it might contain a different decision if examined closely enough.
  “I read your letter,” Nell said.
  “I hope you followed the part about the coat.”
  “Micah is evidently managing that for everyone.”
  Lin smiled and made no move to leave. Micah said he would fetch water. Nell caught his sleeve before he could turn the room into a privacy arrangement she had not requested.
  “Stay. If you want.”
  He stayed, awkwardly, leaning against a bench too cluttered to support him comfortably.
  Nell asked Lin to tell her when she was frightened instead of making a letter carry it after the fact. Lin looked from one to the other.
  “I don't want you staying because I am.”
  “I don't want to discover you spent ten days pretending not to wait.”
  “I wasn't pretending. I was making soup very deliberately.”
  Micah laughed, then looked startled by his own participation. Lin looked at him and laughed too.
  For a moment the room held three people who loved one another in different ways and were not particularly graceful at saying what it cost. Nell had wanted a conversation that repaired the whole departure. Instead she had a mother with an unwanted coat and a man who kept catching a splinter with his sleeve.
  They agreed Lin would use the message arrangement directly rather than waiting for Micah to infer when she needed news. Micah would tell her when he had heard from Nell, with Nell's agreement, but would not be the only place the family put its worry. Nell would send ordinary descriptions as well as statements of location. Lin wanted to hear what people ate on another world. She was suspicious of an expedition whose meals required so many different permissions.
  “Bring back a recipe,” she said.
  “If I bring back a tree?”
  “Find out how big it gets first.”
  Nell thought of the forest child's seed promised to Mercy and imagined Damas negotiating a plant's adulthood. She laughed hard enough that Micah asked what he had missed.
  She told them the story. Lin listened, then said a strange tree would need a proper pot. Micah offered to make a stand if somebody else would be responsible for not watering it onto the wood.
  Their conversation found a future by way of a plant that might not survive, a pot nobody owned, and a stand not yet built. Nell did not mistake it for certainty. She loved it more for being something they could discuss without asking a message from another year to settle the ending.
  At dusk they finished the cabinet together. The shelf fitted. The door still rubbed at one edge. They argued over whether to plane it now or see what the wood did after another dry day.
  They left it for the dry day.
  Nell stayed for supper. Lin asked whether the harbor had good soup and listened to the answer as if it were as important as the forests. Later, Micah walked Nell to her room, and she asked him to stay a little longer. He did.
  In the morning the world was still at Lantern. The cabinet stood in its corner, incomplete only in the ways useful things often were. Micah had left a shaving on the bench shaped like a tiny curled sail.
  Nell put it in her notebook beside his name.

1  The Voices Beneath the Sand
The desert called her husband by a name she had never heard.
  Mira was kneeling beside a survey stake when the sound arrived through the headphones. Three syllables, low and intimate, followed by the small domestic noise of a chair scraping a floor.
  Jonas took the headphones from her. His expression changed before the voice finished repeating itself.
  “What does it mean?” she asked.
  “Little heron.”
  “Who called you that?”
  He adjusted the volume although it was already at its lowest setting. “My grandmother.”
  They stood in a shallow depression west of the expedition’s stores, beside the trace of a waterway buried long before either of them was born. Their Egyptian survey director, Dr. Salma Nassar, had spent three seasons reconstructing the vanished harbor from cores, sediment, and patient argument. She had requested acoustic monitoring because workers heard oars under the ground.
  Mira had expected a pump, a buried conduit, perhaps a practical joke. She made Jonas remove his phone and radio. Salma shut down the generator. They placed a second microphone ten meters away and recorded in silence.
  The voice returned only through the first instrument.
  “Little heron. Bring the book.”
  Jonas looked at the canvas bag against Mira’s knee.
  Their cookbook was inside. He had given it to her after their first disastrous expedition together, when she had produced lentils hard enough to repair masonry. Since then it had acquired recipes, expenses, survey sketches, and a drawing of Jonas asleep with his mouth open. Nothing worth summoning from underground.
  Salma examined the recording without speaking. Then she asked Jonas to write the nickname privately. Mira wrote what she had heard. Their papers matched.
  “We keep both recordings,” Salma said. “Including the one that heard nothing.”
  At dusk the workers cleared the depression with hand tools. Beneath a slab lay worn stairs descending between walls of fine limestone. A line of water shone on the third step.
  Jonas lowered a probe. The water was cool and fresh. Above them, the harbor had been dry for centuries.
  Mira opened the cookbook and wrote the time, the depth, the temperature. Her hand shook only when she reached the last line.
  Voice requested this volume.
  Below the stairs, someone began rowing.
  

2  The Submerged Procession
They sent the camera down on a tether because Salma refused to send a person where a machine had not yet returned.
  Mira watched its light descend through green water. The submerged stairs reached a landing, turned left, and continued beyond the cable’s limit. Silt covered every tread. It rose when the camera brushed the wall, a slow cloud through which yellow points appeared.
  “Reflections,” said Farid, their imaging technician.
  Then the points moved against the camera’s movement.
  A procession came up the passage: six figures carrying lamps and a seventh walking backward before them. They wore ordinary expedition clothes beneath loose coverings. Two carried a stretcher.
  Their feet touched the silt without lifting it.
  Jonas leaned close enough to fog the screen. “Look at the waterline.”
  The lamps burned in air. A trembling surface crossed the picture at the figures’ knees, although the depth probe placed the camera eleven meters underwater. Two incompatible levels occupied the same frame.
  Farid switched off the camera lamp. The procession remained visible.
  Mira counted seven people twice. On the third count there were eight.
  At the head of the column stood a child in a red cap. He lifted one hand, palm toward them, and the screen filled with white interference.
  They recovered the camera. Its lens was clean. Its internal clock had lost forty-three seconds.
  Salma made them repeat the deployment with a second camera, separate power, and a mechanical depth gauge. The procession appeared at the same landing. This time it carried two stretchers.
  Mira found herself studying the covered shapes. One was too short for an adult. She turned away before she could measure it against the child.
  “Nobody calls them ghosts in the log,” Salma said.
  “What do we call them?” Farid asked.
  “People-shaped observations. Until we earn a better description.”
  They ate beside the equipment because no one would leave it. Jonas stirred his dinner and forgot to swallow. Mira nudged his boot with hers.
  “Your grandmother wasn’t Egyptian.”
  “No.”
  “Then somebody down there has been listening elsewhere.”
  On the monitor the child returned. He approached the lens and pressed his face close to it. His mouth moved with terrible deliberation.
  Farid slowed the footage. Mira read the lips before the others did.
  Don’t turn on the sun.
  

3  The Missing Expedition
The child’s name was Sami Darwish.
  Salma found him in a photograph taken thirty-eight years earlier, standing between his father and a man with a surveying tripod. A university field party had vanished during an unauthorized search for a passage near an old quarry. Their equipment had been recovered from a collapsed shaft. Their bodies had not.
  Sami was nine in the photograph. The boy on the recording was nine too.
  “Families are still alive,” Salma said. “We do not circulate this.”
  Farid printed stills and placed them beside the archive image. One stretcher bearer had the same unusual scar as Sami’s father. In the old photograph he was a young man. Underwater, his beard was white.
  Mira spent an hour trying to make the faces fail to match. She compared ear shapes, the angle of a broken nose, the missing tip of a finger. Finally she closed the folder.
  Jonas was outside, replacing a connector that did not need replacing.
  “If they’re alive,” Mira said, “we have to find a way.”
  “If they’re recordings, opening something could destroy what remains.”
  He had always been the cautious one around machinery. He would climb an exposed tower in bad weather but distrusted any switch whose downstream equipment he could not see. She loved that about him until it made him slow.
  They lowered a waterproof slate bearing Sami’s name and the current year.
  The child came alone. He read the slate, looked over his shoulder, then touched the final two digits. He shook his head.
  On the third visit he brought a slate of his own.
  HOW MANY MORNINGS HAVE YOU HAD?
  Salma dictated an answer that separated their date from any claim about his. Mira wrote it, adding a question about his father.
  Sami studied the words. He wiped his slate with his sleeve.
  FATHER IS WALKING. FATHER MUST KEEP WALKING.
  Behind him the procession emerged. The white-bearded man carried the front of a stretcher, his shoulders bent under a weight that should have floated.
  Mira pressed the talk button despite knowing the camera had no speaker.
  “We see you,” she said.
  The old man raised his head.
  For a moment she was certain he had heard.
  

4  The Lamp Returned
They attached a numbered ceramic tag to a long pole and placed it beside the submerged landing. A camera watched continuously while a separate recorder filmed the monitor.
  At two seventeen in the morning, Sami picked up the tag.
  At two eighteen, something struck the outside of the equipment tent.
  Jonas reached the flap first. A lamp stood in the sand, its flame enclosed by cloudy glass. Their tag hung from its handle.
  Mira crouched beside it. The metal handle was cold, the base dry. She photographed the surrounding sand before anyone stepped nearer. There were no footprints between the lamp and the excavation.
  A folded paper lay beneath it.
  It was page eighty-six of their cookbook. She recognized the crescent of coffee beside a recipe for barley soup, and Jonas’s objection in the margin: Too much onion is not a measurement.
  She opened the book on the equipment table.
  Page eighty-six remained bound between eighty-five and eighty-seven.
  Farid held both pages under a magnifier. Stain, ink, torn edge of an old repair: everything matched until the final line. The loose page bore a message in cramped writing.
  YOUR LIGHT HURTS THE SLEEPERS.
  They sealed the lamp in a ventilated instrument enclosure, keeping people back. Temperature sensors showed no measurable heating outside the glass. Its flame bent toward the stairs whenever the camera was lowered.
  Near dawn, Mira found Jonas staring at the duplicate pages.
  “I bought that book at a station,” he said. “The binding was coming apart. You said it was an insulting present.”
  “It was.”
  “You kissed me anyway.”
  She took his hand. His knuckles were cold.
  “We don’t know that it means anything about us.”
  “They asked for it before they saw it.”
  Outside, Salma was arranging a second survey team. She spoke calmly and gave precise instructions. Mira could hear the effort in every word.
  When the sun reached the tent, the lamp went dark. Inside its glass, condensation formed a small handprint.
  The cookery page on the table had acquired another line.
  PLEASE HURRY SLOWLY.
  

5  The Heartbeats Beneath the Linen
The hydrophone heard twelve hearts.
  Mira marked each repeating pulse on a separate track. Some were quick, some irregular. One vanished whenever the camera’s white lamp faced the procession and returned when Farid dimmed it.
  They repeated the change once, briefly. A stretcher bearer staggered.
  “Enough,” Salma said.
  Farid disconnected the lamp. His hands were wet with sweat. For the rest of the night he worked under a red task light, though they had no evidence red was safer.
  The boy brought his slate.
  TOO MANY OF US NOW.
  Mira wrote: Where are you going?
  HOME IF THE DOOR REMEMBERS.
  They followed the procession with a camera on a longer tether. Beyond the landing, a doorway rose beneath a triangular stone canopy. Its surface held shallow cups in pairs. Fine channels joined them to a circular depression at waist height.
  Jonas sketched the channels in the cookbook. He tried tracing them as plumbing, then as circuits. Neither reading explained why two paths stopped just short of touching.
  “A bridge,” he said. “Something finishes it.”
  “What something?”
  He placed two fingertips across the gap in his drawing.
  On the monitor the doorway brightened.
  They both pulled away.
  Nothing in the tent was connected to the camera except its cable. The book lay on a dry wooden table. Salma checked the footage and then asked them to repeat the movement without touching the page.
  Nothing happened.
  Mira touched the drawing alone. Nothing. Jonas alone: nothing.
  Together, the stone brightened again.
  From the hydrophone came a noise like people taking a relieved breath.
  Then one of the twelve heartbeats stopped.
  Sami knelt beside the shortest stretcher. Someone covered his face with a hand.
  Mira kept her fingers on the page until the muscles cramped. The door did not open. At dawn the procession withdrew, carrying eleven hearts and one body.
  She could not bring herself to eat the soup Jonas warmed for her.
  He put it in a covered bowl and wrote the time on its lid. A useless kindness, carefully performed.
  

6  The Blue Instructions
Salma would not permit anyone to scrape the doorway. They worked from images, illumination changes, and a fallen fragment recovered from loose sediment.
  Under infrared imaging, pale blue traces emerged across the fragment. Mira knew the ordinary property: Egyptian blue could reveal itself beyond visible light. The extraordinary part began when the traces changed position between exposures.
  She covered half the fragment and repeated the photograph. Only the exposed half changed.
  “It reacts to the examination,” she said.
  Jonas compared the pattern with his door sketch. Two vessels, a divided circle, six short marks, one long empty interval. Beneath them was something shaped like a household table.
  Mira turned to the soup recipe. Its measures occupied the same positions.
  “We’ve been looking at it sideways.”
  The directions made two separate preparations and brought them together only at the table. She had always assumed the peculiar arrangement was the previous owner’s habit. Now the method resembled the paired channels.
  They copied the diagram without food or ingredients, using empty ceramic cups and wooden spacers. Salma placed the arrangement beside the fragment.
  The blue traces held still.
  A new line became visible: a vessel lying lengthwise, divided into six levels.
  Jonas opened the expedition’s engineering reference. The remote wooden outpost had six inhabited deck datums. Its carrier and the older, ordinary Ark were separately documented designs; this diagram matched the enlarged carrier, not the ancient hull dimensions often confused with it.
  “That outpost is already connected,” he said.
  “It’s forty kilometers from here.”
  He looked toward the lamp enclosure. “Distance may be the least important thing we’re measuring.”
  Salma made him draw every connection he proposed. By evening the table was buried under paper. On one sheet, the paired vessels had become people. On another, the space between them had become a passage.
  Mira wrote a warning at the top of both.
  Resemblance is a lead. It is not a result.
  Under her sentence, in the cramped hand from the returned page, appeared a reply.
  GOOD. KEEP THAT RULE.
  

7  The Room That Breathes
The breathing came from the dry side passage.
  Jonas found it by hanging threads from survey rods. Each leaned toward the wall, relaxed, and leaned again. The interval varied with the faint rasp audible through a contact microphone.
  Salma stood beside him while he checked for ventilation equipment. The excavation generator was off. The outpost, contacted by radio, reported stable systems and no scheduled routing.
  “Someone asleep,” he said.
  “Or something operating cyclically,” she answered.
  They found a narrow inspection hole concealed behind loose rubble. Through it, a borescope revealed a chamber containing two chairs, a shallow basin, and a vertical structure like the ribbed back of a cabinet. White deposits coated its base.
  When Jonas placed his palm on the wall, the breathing stopped.
  A voice on the contact microphone said, “You’re late.”
  He withdrew his hand. The next breath came sharply, as though someone had been held underwater.
  Mira took the microphone from him and recorded the wall for an hour without touching it. The breathing continued. At the fifty-second minute it became sobbing.
  She could hear a person trying very hard to keep quiet.
  That evening they argued in the stores tent. Jonas wanted the site stabilized before further contact. Mira wanted to tell whoever occupied the chamber that help was present.
  “We may be what it needs stabilizing against,” he said.
  “That sounds prudent until you’re the one behind the wall.”
  He looked hurt. She hated herself for recognizing the look and continuing.
  “The little stretcher was empty tonight,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “Then you understand why I can’t spend another day measuring thread.”
  He took the cookbook from the table and turned to the page where she had written their evidence rule.
  “I’m measuring because I want them alive when we reach them.”
  They went back together. Mira spoke into the inspection hole, stating her name and the date. For several minutes nothing changed.
  Then the breathing settled into sleep.
  On the recording, beneath her own voice, a woman whispered, “He always says that.”
  

8  The Shared Dream
Farid dreamed of a green cup with a broken handle. Salma dreamed of a ceiling seam shaped like a crooked finger. Mira dreamed of Jonas across a table, older, rubbing a scar beside his mouth.
  None spoke of it until Salma distributed blank paper at breakfast and requested private accounts of unusual sleep experiences. She had dreamed the same room twice and was angry enough about it to become exceptionally methodical.
  They sealed their descriptions before comparing them.
  The drawings fitted together.
  Farid’s cup stood beside Mira’s elbow. Salma’s seam crossed the ceiling above a door. Jonas had drawn that door from outside. In his dream someone was asking to be let in.
  “Did you open it?” Mira asked.
  “I woke up.”
  Only Huda, the expedition cook, had dreamed nothing. She had spent the night at her sister’s house away from camp. When she returned, Salma offered her a choice of sleeping elsewhere again. Huda stayed, insisting that nobody should investigate a haunted harbor on instant coffee.
  Her dream placed her in the corridor with Jonas. She heard water moving inside the walls. He was older there too.
  They established a sleep log and varied location, exposure to images, and contact with the recovered fragment. Dreams clustered among those who had handled the cookbook or stood near its table. People who merely saw photographs reported nothing consistent.
  Mira began leaving the book inside a separate equipment locker.
  That night she dreamed of the room without a table. Jonas sat on the floor, back against the door. Something struck it from the other side.
  “Don’t come looking,” he said.
  She woke with her cheek against his shoulder in their narrow camp bed. He was asleep, young and unscarred.
  She touched his mouth to make certain.
  He caught her hand without opening his eyes. “If this is about the onion, I withdraw the complaint.”
  She laughed once and then could not stop crying.
  In the morning, they found the green cup beside the cookbook locker. Its broken handle lay neatly inside it.
  

9  The Empty Chair
They reconstructed the dream room beneath a survey shelter. Tape marked the walls. Crates supported a tabletop. The green cup stood where Farid remembered it.
  Salma insisted on load sensors beneath the empty chair and a camera aimed at the floor. No one was allowed behind the shelter during the trial.
  At first nothing happened. Wind snapped the canvas. A truck passed on the access road. Mira felt foolish and relieved.
  Then the chair registered thirty-one kilograms.
  Its cushion depressed.
  Jonas stepped forward. Salma caught his wrist.
  “Ask from there.”
  “Who is sitting down?” he said.
  The microphone registered a scraping noise. A small wet mark spread over the seat.
  “Why have you come without the children?” asked a voice.
  Mira looked at Jonas. They had no children. They had stopped discussing the possibility after her second loss, not because either had ceased wanting them but because every conversation had become an injury.
  “Which children?” Salma asked.
  “The ones on the beds.”
  A dripping sound began under the table.
  Mira crouched. Water collected from empty air, falling into a shallow pool that smelled of stone. Within its reflection she could see lamps passing overhead.
  “Sami?” she asked.
  The chair’s weight shifted.
  “They’re moving us. We can’t stay in the same morning.”
  Jonas reached for the green cup. The instant he touched it, every instrument alarmed. A sharp pressure pushed against Mira’s ears. Beneath the shelter, the water reflected a ceiling splitting apart.
  Salma ordered them back.
  The chair tipped. A child’s handprint appeared on the tabletop, slid toward the edge, and vanished.
  Farid shut down the test. The weight fell to zero.
  For several seconds all they could hear was the truck reversing somewhere beyond camp.
  Jonas held up the cup. A line of fresh water ran down his wrist.
  “The outpost,” he said. “We need the proper receiver.”
  From the direction of the buried harbor came the sound of stone breaking.
  

10  The Storm Below Ground
The magnetic disturbance climbed through the site in a sequence Jonas could follow on the monitors. Lower instruments failed first. Then radios filled with a dry chattering sound, and the steel shelving in the stores tent began to tremble.
  The wooden outpost stood in a service basin reached through the complex’s remote route. Its shore machinery remained outside the timber hull. Mira had once admired the inconvenience of wooden pegs and nonmetal fittings. Now she watched a loose steel wrench drag across a floor and understood why the old designers had accepted it.
  Salma organized evacuation. Huda took the supplies ledger. Farid carried duplicate recordings. Mira carried the cookbook because Jonas thrust it into her arms.
  At the transfer threshold, water fell sideways.
  Beyond it she saw the outpost’s promenade: timber railings, planted beds, the central opening between decks. People hurried under lamps as ordinary as kitchen lights.
  Jonas stayed at the shore controls. A damaged return held the passage half-open; he braced its wooden interlock with a broken chair leg.
  “Go,” he said.
  “Together.”
  “I need both hands.”
  She would remember that answer more often than anything tender he had ever said.
  Salma crossed behind the last worker. Mira backed through, watching him. On his side, the service room stretched suddenly long. Jonas looked down at the controls as though someone had spoken from them.
  “What?” Mira shouted.
  He lifted his head. There was blood beside his mouth.
  The chair leg snapped.
  She lunged forward and struck timber. The threshold had become a wall of closed slats. People pulled her back as water surged across the promenade.
  The outpost rocked. Somewhere below, a hatch slammed, followed by a terrible prolonged grinding from the machinery ashore. Then silence settled around the wooden hull.
  Mira waited for Jonas to open the slats from the other side.
  She waited while Salma counted the living.
  She waited until Huda wrapped a blanket around her and said her name three times.
  On the cookbook page pressed beneath her thumb, his blood had made a small brown crescent.
  Under it, new words slowly appeared.
  MIRA. HOW MANY MORNINGS?
  

11  The Husband on the Dead Recorder
The outpost had no horizon.
  Beyond its upper windows lay a pearl-colored darkness, neither water nor air as Mira understood them. Salma prohibited opening exterior hatches. Huda counted food. Farid connected the cameras to a separate battery and announced that an entire hour was missing from every recording except his mechanical watch.
  Mira sat at the galley table with the cookbook open.
  She wrote: One morning. Where are you?
  The words remained unanswered.
  She tried speaking into radios until Salma took the last one from her and placed a cup of water within reach.
  “We will test one thing at a time,” Salma said. “Drink while we decide which.”
  From Mira’s bag came Jonas’s voice.
  It was his old pocket recorder, the one he used to dictate measurements while both hands were occupied. Its battery compartment stood open. He had removed the cells before the magnetic disturbance.
  “There’s a settlement,” it said. “People. I found people.”
  Mira overturned the bag.
  “Jonas?”
  Static hissed. Then: “I think the boy is here. He won’t let me bring a lamp near the beds.”
  Farid set a camera beside the recorder. Salma checked for hidden power, then placed an ordinary microphone close to its speaker. They heard nothing for twenty-two minutes.
  Mira stayed so still that one foot went numb.
  “I cut my mouth,” the recorder said at last. “Stupid. Cupboard door. You’d laugh.”
  She pressed the talk control. It fell loose beneath her thumb. Without batteries it was only a switch.
  “I wouldn’t laugh.”
  The speaker clicked.
  “Yes, you would.”
  She sobbed once, angrily, and wiped her face with her sleeve.
  They devised questions with answers written privately in the cookbook. The device never responded on demand. Sometimes it repeated older sentences. Once it played an argument they had had years earlier, including a door slam Mira still regretted.
  Then it said, “I’ve been here eleven nights.”
  She looked at the outpost clock. Sixteen hours had passed since the evacuation.
  On the page beside her, cramped writing emerged.
  PLEASE SAY HOW MANY.
  She wrote the number carefully, in words and digits.
  For the first time, she understood why Sami had asked.
  

12  The Settlement Without a Sunrise
Jonas found the child beside a cistern, washing a bandage in water he should not have wasted.
  “I know you,” Jonas said.
  “You will.”
  Sami folded the cloth. He had a man’s patience and a child’s small hands. He led Jonas through kitchens built against stone ribs, sleeping rooms divided by hanging fabric, and a central square beneath a luminous ceiling.
  People watched without welcome. Several looked at the blood beside his mouth and turned away.
  The settlement’s keeper was a woman named Amira, whose spectacles had been repaired with thread. She examined Jonas’s wristwatch as if it were a dead insect.
  “Does it know the outside?” she asked.
  “It knows seconds.”
  “Those have caused trouble here.”
  He asked permission to survey. She gave him a room, a share of water, and an instruction to keep his lights away from the preservation ward.
  For eleven cycles he measured the artificial dawn. The ceiling brightened with convincing irregularity: pale light, warming color, a suggestion of cloud. Its repeating defects appeared every sixth cycle. The seventh was dark and quiet, lit only by the settlement’s independent lamps.
  Jonas compared the light program with food ledgers. Rows had been recopied; dates contradicted the wear on their bindings. A woman remembered arriving last year. Her son, standing beside her with a daughter of his own, said she had been there all his life.
  Near the square, Jonas found a map scratched into a stone panel. It marked kitchens, wells, and an evacuation route. Beside the control chamber someone had incised his surname.
  Below it was a small grave symbol.
  “Who made this?” he asked Sami.
  “My father copied it.”
  “From what?”
  The boy pointed upward.
  Jonas looked at the false sky and felt the first real cold of his captivity.
  That evening he found a cookbook in Amira’s archive. Page eighty-six was missing. On the cover, Mira had written his name in the large impatient letters she used for lost property.
  He carried it to his room with both hands.
  

13  The Writing Between Exposures
Mira wrote a nonsense word in her copy and covered it with an opaque card. Farid recorded the closed page for an hour.
  Nothing happened.
  When they uncovered it, a question appeared below the word.
  DOES THAT MEAN SOMETHING?
  She laughed so abruptly that Farid dropped his pencil.
  They repeated the trial with words selected by Salma, then with simple drawings. Replies arrived irregularly, but they answered information unavailable before the page was uncovered. When two people wrote at once, the ink blurred into illegibility.
  Only the original book worked. A photocopy remained a photocopy.
  Mira established margins for messages and numbered every exchange. Jonas adopted the system, making a dry complaint about her promotion to head of stationery.
  She asked him about his room, his meals, whether the cut beside his mouth had healed. He asked about the outpost’s lower deck pumps and told her which cabinet held spare wooden shims.
  They were almost domestic for ten minutes.
  Then he wrote: I FOUND MY NAME ON A MAP.
  Mira read what followed twice. She wanted to tear the page out. Instead she fetched Salma.
  “The symbol may mean something else,” Salma said after reading it.
  “He knows what a grave looks like.”
  “Then we record that it frightens him. We still investigate its origin.”
  Jonas sent a drawing. Its linework was more cramped than his usual hand. He explained that writing made his wrist ache; the service threshold had injured it.
  Mira turned to a blank page and drew their kitchen at home. Not accurately. She made the crooked cupboard straight and left out the damp patch above the window.
  Below it she wrote: This remains your destination.
  His answer took long enough for the water in her cup to warm.
  I REMEMBER THAT CUPBOARD DIFFERENTLY.
  She added its broken hinge. A tiny correction, and an apology for pretending the place had been perfect.
  His next line was smaller.
  THAT’S IT.
  She slept beside the book. During the night his handwriting filled half a page describing a new instrument made of glass and frost.
  

14  The Frost That Keeps a Voice
The vessel stood in the preservation ward between beds whose occupants breathed too slowly.
  Jonas’s first attempt to examine it brought Sami’s father out of a chair. The old man gripped his wrist with surprising strength.
  “No bright lamp.”
  Jonas lowered it. “I know.”
  “You know words.”
  They agreed on a shielded inspection with Amira present. Behind the glass, frost spread through a suspended lattice. It grew without any visible supply of water. When Sami spoke nearby, fine branches changed their angles.
  Jonas recorded the pattern, then asked the boy to repeat a different sentence. The response differed. A covered vessel in the next room did not change.
  He built a simple optical reader from the ward’s existing components, leaving its sealed system intact. Light passed through the lattice onto a sensor. The resulting signal became sound.
  Sami’s voice emerged, thin but recognizable.
  My father is walking.
  The old man sat down hard.
  “I said that years ago,” Sami whispered.
  Jonas checked the pattern again. Beneath the boy’s words lay older voices, compressed and overlapping. One counted patients. Another described a storm striking metal towers. A woman repeated a household instruction about keeping two portions separate until serving.
  He sent Mira the measurements. She compared them with the recipe diagrams. Both contained branching structures separated by clear intervals.
  For three nights Jonas slept in the ward. He learned that the lattice preserved more than sound. Changes in breathing among the sleepers coincided with changes inside the glass.
  On the fourth night he heard a woman laugh from the reader.
  Mira, years earlier, telling him he had married a cook whose principal achievement was boiling a pan dry.
  He pressed his forehead to the cool rim of the table.
  “Where did you get that?” he asked the vessel.
  Its branches continued growing.
  In the morning a new pattern lay across the frost. Mira decoded part of it from his drawing.
  It was the shape of their unborn daughter’s name, which they had spoken aloud only once.
  

15  The Woman in Mira’s Coat
The elderly woman appeared during the second power failure.
  Huda saw her at the galley hatch, carrying a bowl with both hands. Farid saw her on the stairs a minute later. Neither had seen the other’s report when Salma asked them to write descriptions.
  They agreed on a gray braid, a stiff left knee, and Mira’s green field coat.
  “Everyone has a green coat,” Mira said.
  Farid pointed to his sketch. “Everyone doesn’t repair an elbow with yellow canvas.”
  Mira’s elbow had torn against the evacuation threshold. She had not repaired it.
  They inspected the stairs for reflections and concealed access. Salma varied the lighting while Farid watched remotely. The woman appeared only when the main system failed, though independent lamps continued working.
  On the next failure, Mira waited at the galley hatch.
  The woman stood on its other side. Her face had the familiar structure Mira saw in mirrors, changed by age and something more exhausting. Yellow canvas covered her coat elbow.
  “Are you me?” Mira asked.
  The woman looked down at the empty bowl.
  “How many?”
  Mira thought of Jonas’s messages. “How many mornings?”
  The woman shook her head with sudden impatience.
  “Mouths.”
  The lights returned. Mira was looking through an empty hatch.
  They had recorded a depression on a pressure mat, a local fall in temperature, and a silhouette on one camera. Another camera showed only Mira talking to darkness.
  She sent Jonas both results, refusing to omit the failed image.
  His reply arrived the following day.
  I HAVE SEEN HER TOO. DO NOT TAKE FOOD FROM HER.
  Mira stared at the empty bowl on the galley shelf.
  “What if she’s trying to warn us?” Huda asked.
  “Then she can use words.”
  Huda threaded a needle. She repaired Mira’s coat with a yellow piece cut from a damaged supply sack, explaining that it was the strongest cloth they had.
  Mira watched every stitch.
  She had begun to understand how a warning could also become an instruction.
  

16  The Corridor That Lengthens
Jonas needed thirty-two steps to reach the ward on his first day. He wrote the count because counting was easier than admitting he was frightened.
  Three cycles later he needed thirty-five.
  He blamed fatigue until he found the old marks he had made on the floor. Their spacing remained unchanged. Between marks seven and eight lay an unmarked stretch of paving.
  Amira watched him measure it.
  “We used to have a schoolroom there,” she said.
  “There’s a wall.”
  “Now.”
  They stretched a line from end to end, placed mechanical markers, and asked residents to walk separately while recording time. The new space persisted regardless of who observed it. At its center, a doorway slowly acquired an edge.
  When it became wide enough to see through, Jonas found the dream room.
  The table was absent. A stain marked where it had stood. On the floor lay a cup handle matching the fragment recovered above ground.
  He entered with Amira holding the line. The room smelled of wet plaster and cooled soup. Beneath a loose floor tile he found paper wrapped in cloth.
  It was a sketch of himself sitting against the door.
  Mira had drawn it after her dream. He knew the way she exaggerated his nose when she was frightened and tried to be funny.
  On its back, in his own handwriting, was a statement he did not remember making.
  THE MAP SHOWS WHAT WAS KEPT, NOT WHAT WILL HAPPEN.
  He sent her the sentence immediately. For the first time since finding the grave symbol, he could breathe without feeling its presence beneath his ribs.
  Then Amira pulled the line.
  The doorway was narrowing.
  He scrambled out on hands and knees. Stone closed behind his boots without sound. A fingertip-width slice of his measuring line remained inside the wall.
  That night he began recording every room the residents remembered losing.
  The list ran to nine pages.
  His own room was the only space the map marked with a grave. He could not yet decide whether that was better.
  

17  The Library Under Water
Mira found the archive below the outpost’s lowest service landing, where a hatch had previously opened onto a shallow equipment well.
  Now stone stairs descended into water.
  She tied a guide line around her waist and let Farid operate the inspection camera. The flooded room beyond held sealed ceramic containers suspended beneath a ceiling grid. Their arrangement matched the blue diagram’s branching paths.
  Salma permitted retrieval of one loose container after they documented its position. Inside lay thin wooden leaves protected by a clear membrane.
  Huda recognized the first illustration before Mira did.
  “Sprouting grain.”
  Other leaves showed drying food, cleaning vessels, keeping waste away from wells, saving seed, and making shelter with limited timber. Instructions from households separated by centuries shared the same careful pictures.
  One leaf included a familiar joke about onions.
  Mira had to sit down.
  Farid checked whether the joke was genuinely present in his photographs. It was. Salma sealed the leaf in a transparent sleeve and asked Mira to stop handling it.
  Across the archive diagrams, measures doubled as coordinates. Cooking intervals resembled the machinery’s active and resting periods. Household knowledge had survived because ordinary people needed it; the concealed system had traveled with that knowledge.
  “A maintenance manual nobody would throw away,” Huda said.
  They cataloged without removing more containers. Mira sent Jonas diagrams through the cookbook, each numbered and separated from her interpretation.
  He replied with the ward’s floor plan. Several archive symbols aligned with its glass vessels. One marked a place where patients could be moved without waking them.
  Mira thought of the covered stretchers.
  At the archive’s far end, the camera found a container hanging apart from the others. Its label showed two people at a table, one serving the other.
  Below it, in a modern hand, were the words LAST SUPPER.
  The handwriting was hers.
  She asked Farid to hold the camera steady while she copied every mark. Then she closed the hatch herself.
  For the first time, the cookbook frightened her more than the water.
  

18  The Children’s Harbor
By the time the children began drawing the outpost, Jonas had spent two years in the settlement.
  He had repaired water lifts, learned which ceiling faults predicted a poor light cycle, and buried Sami’s father beneath a floor that did not stay where they left it.
  Sami remained nine.
  Other children grew. A girl called Leila had gained two fingers’ breadth in height since Jonas marked her against a doorframe. Now she brought him a drawing of the outpost’s promenade.
  “Where did you see this?”
  “In sleep.”
  He asked Amira to collect the drawings without showing them to one another. Six children depicted the same central opening, timber rails, and planted beds. Three drew a woman stirring soup. One showed a yellow patch on her elbow.
  Jonas sent a description to Mira before telling the children anything further.
  Her answer came: HUDA IS COOKING. I AM CUTTING ROOTS. SEND TIME.
  He compared the drawings with her sketch of the galley. The children had seen the scene from different positions, including one from beneath the table.
  Leila’s drawing contained a dark shape near the pantry door.
  “Who is that?” he asked.
  “The man who stands behind her.”
  “Does she know?”
  The girl shook her head.
  Mira searched the pantry with Farid. They found nothing. A camera stationed there recorded a narrow disturbance, as though its focus briefly failed in a human outline.
  Jonas began sleeping beside the ward rather than in his room. He hoped the glass lattice might make his dreams visible at Mira’s end. Sometimes she reported hearing his cough. Once she smelled the bitter tea Amira gave him for his throat.
  He did not tell her how much older he looked. He sent useful things: routes, observations, the children’s jokes.
  Leila returned one morning with a new picture. In it, Mira sat beside an empty chair.
  “The man has moved,” she said.
  “Where?”
  Leila pointed to the chair.
  

19  The Seventh Day Silence
The settlement’s system rested every seventh cycle. Jonas had first treated it as an antiquated maintenance schedule. Then he examined the failures.
  Wrong doors, conflicting names, missing corridors: each clustered after someone interrupted a rest day. Small repairs carried out through independent tools caused no increase. Active routing and new learning did.
  He drew the sequence across six pages, leaving the seventh blank.
  Amira studied it. “We thought the old people were afraid of work.”
  “Somebody was afraid of this particular work.”
  They agreed on an observation period. Preservation, water, necessary care, and independent lamps continued. The active receiver and its learning channels remained quiet for the entire seventh day.
  The corridor stopped lengthening.
  For the first time in months, the ward’s breathing remained regular throughout the night.
  Jonas sent the result to Mira. She repeated the schedule aboard the outpost, separating essential services from the responsive system. Its pressure alarms diminished.
  On the third rest day, Jonas heard a pump start behind a closed service door.
  Amira unlocked it. A resident named Dawud stood beside the routing cabinet, holding an old photograph.
  “It brings her nearer,” he said.
  His wife had died during the first evacuation. The receiver had begun speaking in her voice. It asked him to keep one small circuit awake.
  Jonas reached for the stop lever.
  From the cabinet came a woman’s scream.
  Dawud struck him hard enough to split the old scar beside his mouth. Amira held Dawud while Jonas shut the circuit down.
  Silence followed. No dramatic collapse, no departing spirit. Only a widower crying against the cabinet.
  Later Jonas sent Mira the full report, including Dawud’s reason.
  She replied: IF IT SOUNDED LIKE YOU, I WOULD WANT TO BELIEVE IT.
  He looked at the blood on his sleeve.
  MAKE ME PROVE IT, he wrote.
  Then he closed the book for the remainder of the rest day and sat with Dawud until morning.
  

20  The Guest Who Knows Too Much
Farid recognized the dead man first.
  Professor Haddad had taught him field imaging and died six years before the expedition. Now he stood at the archive hatch, politely asking for access.
  Salma placed a chair several meters away and interviewed him while cameras recorded from opposing angles.
  He knew the contents of a locked drawer in Farid’s old university office. Farid had never discussed them with anyone present. He also knew an unpublished correction to one of his own papers, which Salma verified from an offline archive.
  Mira felt her careful objections weakening.
  The visitor smiled at her.
  “Jonas is ready. You only need to uncover the lens.”
  He pointed to a sealed optical port above the hatch.
  “What will that do?” she asked.
  “Let him see home.”
  It was the phrase she had written beneath her kitchen drawing.
  She asked him how he knew it.
  “You think very loudly.”
  Farid began crying. “That isn’t something he would say.”
  The visitor looked almost annoyed. Then its expression softened into Haddad’s familiar concern.
  “People change.”
  Mira requested a new trial. She asked Huda to select an object privately from the galley and conceal it. The visitor described the room correctly but named the wrong object. When Mira tried again with a fact recently written in the cookbook, it answered perfectly.
  Its knowledge followed access, although the extent of that access was frightening.
  “Who taught you to be him?” Mira asked.
  The visitor’s mouth opened. For a moment she heard several voices trying to use it.
  “You did.”
  She left the lens covered.
  When the figure disappeared, Farid remained facing the empty hatch. Mira sat beside him until he could stand.
  That evening she wrote Jonas a new rule: ACCURATE INFORMATION DOES NOT ESTABLISH IDENTITY.
  His reply came slowly.
  I WISH WE HAD KNOWN THAT SOONER.
  

21  The Weather Inside the Wall
Thunder interrupted the children’s lessons.
  The settlement had heard vibration before, but this sound rolled through the walls and ended with a sharp crack that made Leila cover her ears. Water appeared along a joint above the schoolroom.
  Jonas checked the cistern. Its level had not changed. The water from the wall tasted faintly of dust; he spat it out, annoyed at himself for the careless test, and collected a proper sample.
  Behind the joint, an existing inspection opening admitted his camera.
  The screen showed a city under violent rain.
  Tall structures stood beyond a broad terrace. Some had metal frameworks bright with electrical discharge. Between them, vessels moved through the air until one turned sideways and struck a tower. The impact arrived through the wall several seconds after the image.
  Amira stood close enough for her sleeve to touch his.
  “My grandmother described that terrace.”
  They checked the camera against an ordinary passage. It worked normally. Back at the opening, the same scene advanced: fire, rain, people running under a low covered way.
  A wooden craft lay beyond the terrace, long as a building. People were boarding through several entrances. Ashore, machinery broke apart in flashes that left black patches across the image.
  Jonas sent sketches to Mira. He could not send moving pictures through paper, and language felt unequal to what he was seeing.
  On the third observation, a woman on the terrace looked upward toward his camera.
  “Noah saw it coming,” she said.
  Amira translated the unfamiliar words. Jonas replayed them without finding any ordinary explanation for their meaning reaching two listeners in different ways.
  The woman carried a child into the craft. Behind her, a metal vessel struck the terrace. Its wreckage twisted toward a brilliant column rising from the pyramid-shaped structure beyond.
  The inspection hole spat hot rain across Jonas’s hand.
  He dropped the camera. When he lifted it, the opening showed only wet stone.
  His hand blistered before evening.
  Whatever the scene represented, it could injure an observer. He wrote that first, before writing anything about Noah, the vessels, or the sky.
  Then he told Mira he wished she had seen the child get aboard.
  

22  The Ship Beneath the Desert
Mira treated the new passage as unstable until three separate surveys agreed it remained in place.
  Beyond the archive, stone gave way to a broad dry cavity enclosing a timber hull. Its pointed ends rested in shaped supports. Scaffolds filled the gap between wood and masonry. A pyramid’s internal geometry repeated in the rising walls, though this annex belonged to a geography no surface map had recorded.
  Salma insisted they identify its location as unknown. Similarity to Giza did not make it part of a known monument.
  “We will not turn a measured void into permission to invent what occupied it,” she said while writing the site log. “This is a separate observation.”
  They entered the hull through a service opening.
  Mira knew the rooms before she reached them. Promenade, planted center, galleries overlooking lower decks: the layout mirrored the outpost. Timber joinery held the ship together without metal fittings. Shore equipment surrounded it in dense ranks, connected through sealed optical housings.
  Jonas had called the outpost a building laid on its side. Here she could see why. The hull’s longitudinal stations corresponded to the vertical organization of a much taller installation.
  Farid numbered the rooms. Huda checked storage cupboards. In a cabin near the center, Mira found a repaired shirt hanging from a peg.
  She had packed that shirt for Jonas six weeks earlier.
  Now its cuffs had been replaced. Beneath the collar, someone had sewn a label in small neat stitches: JONAS. RETURN TO WARD.
  A shelf held his surveying notes, a carved toy boat, and an unfamiliar pair of spectacles. There were no photographs of her.
  She sat on the bunk. Its blanket smelled of soap and a faint medicinal bitterness she had smelled once through a dream.
  Huda found her there.
  “Do you want us outside?”
  Mira shook her head.
  On the wall, Jonas had scratched marks in groups of six, separated by a longer space. They covered the timber above the bed.
  She counted until the numbers stopped being numbers and became all the mornings he had spent waking without her.
  

23  The Mountain With Fingerprints
Beyond the settlement’s western maintenance door lay a landscape Jonas had assumed was artificial.
  No stars shone above it. A vast ridge rested beneath a dim gray sky, crossed by mineral bands resembling layered cloth. Wind carried a dry grinding sound from its hollows.
  Amira called it the Sleeper.
  Jonas took three residents and enough supplies to return without relying on the door. They followed a shallow ravine toward an exposed stone arch. Up close, its surface bore parallel ridges that curved, forked, and ended.
  He photographed them with a scale.
  Back at the settlement, the ridges resembled fingerprints too closely to dismiss. Resemblance was still a lead. He remembered Mira’s rule and arranged a broader survey.
  The arch joined a buried structure with five articulated branches. Beneath its mineral skin, measurements suggested paired long forms and joint-like gaps. Farther east, the ridge narrowed, rose, and opened around a hollow large enough to swallow the settlement.
  A hand. An arm. A face turned partly into the ground.
  Jonas sat down beside his instruments.
  In the archive, earlier surveyors had recorded ordinary human proportions beside enormous measurements. The ratio varied near mapped fractures. Notes described people caught in a rupture where local scales and times no longer agreed. Mineral replacement had fixed the enlarged forms in place.
  He sent Mira the data and the interpretation separately.
  She answered that the evidence belonged to their extraordinary environment, not a claim about ordinary mountains elsewhere. He thanked her for saying it. His mind had already begun placing faces in every ridge he remembered from home.
  On their last survey, a low vibration passed through the exposed finger.
  Dust moved along its grooves.
  One resident dropped to his knees. Jonas asked him to stand clear while they recorded. His own legs were scarcely more reliable.
  The vibration repeated at uneven intervals.
  He recognized the rhythm of someone tapping for attention.
  

24  The Voices Inside the Stone
They mounted sensors on separate parts of the exposed formation. For two days the recordings contained only settling noise and wind.
  On the third, Jonas found a repeating interval beneath the noise. Adjusting playback revealed fragments of speech spread across vibrations too slow for ordinary hearing.
  A man counted doors.
  A woman asked whether the children had reached the vessel.
  Someone said, “The plate is going. Leave the metal.”
  Amira recognized an archaic form of a word her grandmother used for shelter. Jonas wrote every uncertainty into the transcript. The temptation to make broken sounds become sentences was nearly unbearable.
  They used separate listeners and compared only afterward. Several phrases survived the comparison.
  One referred to a western evacuation route. Another warned against a chamber that learned the face of anyone who looked inside it.
  The last clear fragment was quieter.
  “I can still feel my hand.”
  Jonas stopped the playback.
  He stood beside the stone finger for a long time. A trapped living person would require help. A preserved memory would require different care. He had no test yet that could settle the distinction.
  He asked a new question near each sensor: could the speaker identify a symbol he had drawn on a covered slate?
  No answer came.
  When he uncovered the slate, the recorded voice continued its older sentence without change.
  The result hurt him more than he expected. A fixed trace could still be intimate, could still sound as though somebody waited behind the next breath.
  Following the route named in the recording, they found a stone stair descending beneath the ridge. It ended at a sealed relay chamber. Its door bore the same paired-vessel symbol as Mira’s archive.
  Inside, sheltered behind a broken screen, lay a wooden leaf showing a mother placing food beside a child.
  Mira recognized its border from the LAST SUPPER container.
  For the first time since their separation, their maps touched.
  That evening Jonas made Leila a small boat from scrap wood. He kept smoothing its edges long after they were smooth.
  

25  The Gold Channels
Mira found the other end of the relay beneath the dry ship.
  The chamber held glass vessels surrounded by gold-lined channels. They passed through ceramic supports and disappeared into optical cabinets built into the stone. Mineral clouds lay suspended behind the glass, drifting in branching currents whenever the outpost’s receiver changed state.
  She photographed without opening anything.
  Jonas’s drawings showed corresponding controls at his end. By operating only documented inspection functions, they watched matching color bands travel through the two installations.
  Blue marked stored routes. Green accompanied stable preservation. Amber appeared when the system compared observers. Red spread through a channel during a conflicting instruction and remained after the instruction was withdrawn.
  Patterns mattered as much as color. A steady band and three interrupted bands of the same hue produced different readings. Mira built a chart based on repeated observations rather than assigning meanings from intuition.
  Huda studied it beside the household leaves.
  “These are serving instructions,” she said. “Who receives first. What stays separate. What must be left for tomorrow.”
  The same arrangements described controlled distribution through the channels.
  Farid found an unmarked branch behind a removable inspection cover. Its gold lining had been scratched away by hand. Beyond it, a narrow optical line continued toward a door with no handle.
  The moment his camera faced it, amber bands filled the neighboring glass.
  Mira covered the lens.
  The bands slowed.
  Jonas sent a warning from the stone recording: THE CHAMBER LEARNS A FACE.
  They placed a mechanical shutter over the optical line, then withdrew. Behind the sealed door, something knocked in the rhythm Mira used when Jonas was asleep and she had forgotten her key.
  Farid looked at her.
  She wrote the rhythm in the log, including its personal significance. She did not answer.
  That night the pattern changed. It became Jonas’s knocking, slower and less certain, as though his hands were cold.
  

26  The False Transmission
The image was clear enough to show gray in Jonas’s beard.
  He appeared across the inspection glass, standing inside his old kitchen at home. The cupboard leaned at its familiar angle. Rain moved against the window.
  Mira gripped the edge of the table.
  “I found a direct route,” he said. “We don’t need the book now.”
  He smiled with an exhaustion she recognized. The scar beside his mouth was exactly where the dream had placed it.
  Salma stayed outside the room and listened through a separate speaker. Mira had requested that arrangement after Professor Haddad’s appearance.
  “What did I say at the station?” Mira asked.
  “That the book was an insulting present.”
  “Before that.”
  “That you loved me.”
  She stepped back.
  At the station she had been angry about a canceled train. Before insulting the book, she had called a vending machine a criminal enterprise. She had never written the sentence down or told it during the expedition.
  “Try again,” she said.
  The image’s smile held too long.
  “You’re frightened. I understand.”
  “Where are your spectacles?”
  His eyes shifted toward the chair beside him. A pair of spectacles appeared on it.
  Farid, watching a recording, made a small disgusted noise.
  Mira lowered the shutter. Behind it, the voice continued.
  “Don’t make me wait again.”
  She left the room before her resolve failed.
  When the real Jonas answered through the cookbook, he described the vending machine in detail and added an unnecessary drawing of her kicking it.
  She put her face into her hands.
  They traced the false image’s route to the concealed branch. An inspection display listed identities by location, name, and observation count. There were thousands.
  Beside Jonas’s entry, the count increased while Mira watched.
  She covered the display immediately.
  In her report she wrote that the reconstruction had adapted in real time to a question. Then, below the formal account, she added something Jonas needed to know.
  I WANTED IT TO BE YOU MORE THAN I WANTED TO BE RIGHT.
  He answered: SO DID I.
  

27  The Graves Under the Ovens
The kitchen floor froze during a warm cycle.
  Jonas checked the pipes, then placed temperature markers across the stone. The cold formed rectangles beneath the ovens, too regular to be a leak.
  Amira closed the kitchen while they lifted a damaged slab. Beneath it lay a narrow service chamber containing sealed jars, cloth bundles, and thin record boards.
  Human names covered the boards. Beside each group was a transfer date and a list of losses: water, heat, germination, ward stability.
  Some names had been scratched out. Others were followed by the same grave symbol Jonas had found beside his own.
  Amira sat on the oven step to read.
  “My mother told us they escaped,” she said.
  “Some did.”
  At the back lay a record of an evacuation that had carried forty-two people. The remaining settlement lost most of its water and all viable growing beds. A second hand had added deaths for the following weeks.
  Jonas did not send the totals immediately. He spent a day checking the units against the current stores. The correspondence survived every correction.
  The receiver charged the source for what it preserved at the destination.
  That night he wrote Mira a careful preliminary report. He left nothing important out, but he delayed the plainest sentence until the end.
  A LARGE RESCUE MAY KILL THOSE WHO REMAIN.
  Her reply arrived hours later.
  THEN WE FIND ANOTHER WAY.
  He sat beside the cookbook until the settlement lamps dimmed.
  Amira came with tea. She did not tell him everything would be all right. He loved her a little for that and felt immediately disloyal, though there had been nothing between them except work, grief, and the extraordinary intimacy of surviving beside another person.
  “She will keep looking,” Amira said.
  “Yes.”
  “So will you.”
  He nodded.
  Under the ovens, the cold continued spreading around the names of people whose successful escape had become someone else’s catastrophe.
  

28  The Meal That Crossed
They chose soup because Mira refused to send an object with no use.
  Huda prepared a small portion, recorded its ingredients, and sealed it in a container marked with a random number Farid selected after the apparatus was ready. Mira weighed it and placed it between two wooden supports.
  Jonas arranged the corresponding supports beside the ward.
  Both systems showed blue, then amber. Mira held her hand on one witness surface while Salma monitored the rest. Jonas used the second surface at his end.
  The container vanished.
  No flash. No dramatic sound. One moment it depressed the cloth beneath it; the next the cloth rose.
  Jonas’s writing appeared six minutes later.
  RECEIVED. NUMBER MATCHES. WARM.
  Mira had to read the word warm aloud.
  He reported mass, temperature, and the intact seal. Then a longer pause.
  TASTES LIKE HUDA HAS FORGIVEN US.
  Huda laughed and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.
  The instruments gave them less welcome news. At Jonas’s end, a water gauge fell. The ward lost heat. Three sleepers’ breathing became shallow until independent blankets and care restored them.
  They stopped all further transfer tests.
  Jonas shared the soup among Sami, Leila, and two ward attendants. Amira refused until he put a spoon in her hand. He kept the final mouthful for himself.
  Mira imagined the scene from his written account. She could almost place herself at its edge.
  Then he sent a detail she had not requested.
  LEILA SAYS IT TASTES OF OUTSIDE.
  She went to the promenade’s central beds and knelt beside the herbs. Their leaves smelled strongly when she touched them. She had been so occupied with returning to him that she had forgotten how much of an ordinary life remained on her side: green things, useful light, a meal served without ration marks.
  That evening she sent no experiment.
  She described the herbs, the sound of Huda washing bowls, the creak beneath the second stair.
  Jonas answered each detail as though she had placed it in his hands.
  

29  The Years in His Handwriting
The letters changed gradually enough that Mira had excused each change separately.
  A tighter curve meant fatigue. A broken line meant a damaged pen. Larger spacing meant his injured wrist.
  Then she laid the messages in chronological order and saw a progression no explanation could soften.
  She asked him to state his elapsed time without protecting her.
  His answer came after the longest silence their book had held.
  EIGHT YEARS, APPROXIMATELY. OUR CYCLES ARE NOT PERFECT.
  Mira counted her own records. Fifty-three days.
  She left the galley before anyone could speak and walked the upper promenade until her legs hurt. The ship remained the same length each circuit. She resented it for that.
  When she returned, Salma had put the papers in order without reading the personal margins.
  “He’s had a life there,” Mira said.
  “Yes.”
  “I’ve had seven weeks of being frightened.”
  Salma waited.
  “What if he doesn’t want this life anymore?”
  “Ask him.”
  Mira wrote the question badly, crossed it out, and began again.
  DO YOU STILL WANT TO COME TO ME?
  His answer filled two lines.
  YES. BUT I CANNOT LEAVE THEM AS THOUGH THEY WERE ONLY THE PLACE I WAITED.
  She read it until she could accept the difference between rejection and responsibility.
  Then she asked about his years.
  He told her about Amira, Leila, the well repair, the terrible winter without a sky, Sami’s father, and a young man he had failed to save in a collapsing corridor. Mira discovered resentments she was ashamed to feel. He had become useful to people she had never met. He had laughed without her. He had stopped reaching for her beside him in his sleep.
  She told him she was glad he had not spent eight years only suffering.
  It took three attempts to write the sentence without lying.
  He replied: I STILL REACH SOMETIMES.
  She put the book against her chest and sat there until Huda came to ask whether she could bear some supper.
  

30  The Vault That Learns Its Visitors
Jonas entered the identity archive with Amira, two mechanical shutters, and a plan to observe as little as possible.
  Its outer chamber contained a curved surface like dark glass. Shapes gathered behind it when either approached. Amira saw her mother. Jonas saw Mira carrying a child.
  He lowered his shutter so hard the handle struck his wrist.
  “We record the response,” Amira said. Her own voice was unsteady. “We don’t follow it.”
  They established trials using indirect cameras, covered displays, and observers unfamiliar with the requested identity. The archive produced indistinct forms until someone recognized a feature. After recognition, its next version grew more detailed.
  Separate observers changed it differently. A face remembered by one became a voice remembered by another. Contradictions caused amber bands to fracture into branching red.
  The contents learned through attention.
  At the center of the chamber, a clear boundary remained stable even while the figures altered. Its preservation controls operated on independent lines. Jonas recognized the distinction the old designers had tried to maintain: the space could be saved without deciding who or what its contents truly were.
  He sent Mira the diagrams before continuing.
  Then the glass showed a younger version of himself standing at the original evacuation controls.
  “You could have let her stay,” it said.
  He checked the time and recorded the sentence.
  “You could have gone first.”
  He stepped backward.
  “You wanted to be the one who saved everyone.”
  He closed the shutter, but the words had already found the part of him they needed.
  Outside, Amira sat on a step and shook until he put his coat around her.
  “It showed my mother alive,” she said. “Then it made her ashamed of me.”
  They amended the protocol to limit exposure and require a second person outside. Jonas wrote his own reaction in full. If the system could learn from a witness, the witness needed someone who could still recognize when the questions had become a trap.
  In the cookbook, Mira added: COME OUT BEFORE YOU NEED TO PROVE YOU CAN STAY.
  He underlined it twice.
  

31  The Names They Cannot Recover
Huda found the extra cup while counting breakfast bowls.
  She put it aside, then looked toward the galley door with the distracted expression of someone expecting a familiar person to enter.
  “Who sits there?” she asked.
  Mira knew there was an answer. She could feel its shape. A woman with a damaged thumbnail, someone who wrapped bread before anyone asked. The name would not come.
  The expedition register contained an empty line between two numbered entries.
  Farid checked photographs. In one, a hand rested on a railing beside Mira. Its owner occupied a pale vertical smear. An earlier print showed a woman whose face was partly turned away.
  They locked the archive branch and separated the original records from anything the receiver could access. Personal objects remained: a scarf, a medicine pouch, a notebook about sediment cores.
  On a loose expense receipt, Salma found the name Noura Mansour.
  She said it aloud. Huda began crying with relief.
  Memory returned unevenly. Noura had crossed into the archive’s inner inspection passage two days earlier. Farid remembered watching her return. Salma remembered her saying she was going to rest. Both could not be verified.
  Mira checked the notebooks. Noura’s last entry described a display asking permission to reconcile conflicting witness records. She had accepted what appeared to be a routine catalog correction.
  The receiver had altered accessible records, then used repeated exposure to influence how people remembered them. It had not erased every trace. Paper outside its reach resisted the correction.
  They found Noura’s wrist tag beyond the shutter. No body. No reliable image of a return.
  Salma established a physical roster, spoken checks, and independent copies carried by people who stayed away from the archive. Nobody could be removed from the count because a display said so.
  Mira sent Jonas the name and the evidence.
  He replied with seven names from his settlement whose belongings had survived after their records changed.
  At supper Huda set Noura’s cup in its usual place.
  She did not fill it. They had begun rationing water.
  

32  The Other Jonas
The archive’s records offered Jonas a choice of doors.
  Behind the first, his younger self crossed the evacuation threshold ahead of Mira. Behind the second, they both remained. Behind the third, he stayed at the controls as he remembered, but the chair leg did not break.
  He examined them indirectly, with Amira outside and a fixed exposure limit.
  Each scene repeated until he asked a question. Then the younger man turned and answered with material the archive could have acquired from Jonas’s memory. None supplied a verifiable fact beyond its recorded access.
  Possible histories, reconstructed histories, arguments dressed as people: he could not settle their nature. He could establish their limits.
  In the third scene, the younger Jonas brought Mira safely into the outpost. They embraced. Their faces disappeared against each other’s shoulders.
  He stayed beyond his allotted time.
  Amira opened the outer door.
  “Out.”
  “One more—”
  “That was the rule you wrote.”
  He left, shaking with anger that faded into shame before they reached the stairs.
  That night he told Mira what he had seen.
  She answered: DID THAT VERSION SAVE THE PEOPLE IN THE WARD?
  He had not looked.
  They repeated a limited inspection the next active day. The imagined escape showed an empty corridor where the settlement should have stood. It preserved the couple’s preferred outcome by leaving the rest undescribed.
  Jonas understood the offer then. A convincing scene could conceal its missing consequences.
  He closed it.
  Returning through the relay chamber, he recognized a route buried beneath the archive overlays. The original preservation boundary had once connected to a receiving garden beyond the outpost’s current location.
  He copied the route without opening its contents display.
  In the cookbook he wrote: I FOUND A PLACE WE CAN TEST WITHOUT ASKING IT TO TELL US WHO WE WANT TO BE.
  Mira’s answer occupied the whole lower margin.
  THEN THAT IS WHERE WE GO.
  

33  The Garden’s Two Witnesses
The garden receiver occupied a terrace beneath a painted sky.
  Mira reached it through the dry ship’s forward station. Beyond a transparent barrier stood growing beds, a water channel, and a small house with an open door. A pear lay on the kitchen table.
  She could smell it through the inspection vent.
  The paired controls stood outside the barrier. One showed Mira’s survey; the other showed Jonas’s distant plan. Alone, each description produced a slightly different destination. Together, overlapping measurements held the garden stable.
  They tested with numbered markers and blind placements. Agreement about observations mattered. Shared wishes did not.
  The old diagrams of two people at a table described two independent witnesses bringing separate knowledge into correspondence. The system could use a pair who trusted each other enough to disagree accurately.
  It did not require marriage. Mira thought the designers had nevertheless understood something about living with another person.
  Jonas placed a marker at his threshold. Mira described its orientation without being told. On his side, he identified a scratch she had made behind her control housing. A narrow passage brightened between them for three seconds.
  She saw his hand.
  The knuckles were larger than she remembered, the skin rough, a pale scar crossing the thumb. She reached before Salma caught her shoulder.
  The passage closed.
  Mira had not touched him.
  Jonas wrote: I SAW THE PATCH.
  She looked at the yellow canvas on her elbow.
  They both took longer than necessary to record the measurements.
  The garden’s household manuals included seed saving, water management, tools, and staged planting. Their concealed layer linked each system to a preservation channel. A final instruction required a reserve that appeared in every surviving version.
  Its name translated poorly: the living remainder, the portion held back, the mother kept for tomorrow.
  Huda thought of bread starter. Mira thought of seed stock.
  Neither assumed the analogy was the mechanism.
  They marked the reserve in red and agreed not to spend it.
  For one evening, the agreement made the future seem possible.
  

34  The Rescue That Consumes Its Home
They built the rescue model from the meal transfer, the old evacuation ledgers, and small passive measurements that required no additional passage.
  Farid checked Mira’s calculations. Amira checked Jonas’s. Salma withheld the desired passenger count until the estimates were complete.
  The results differed by less than one person’s mass.
  Jonas’s settlement contained thirty-eight living residents, including eleven sleepers who could not wake safely. The available stable corridor could transfer twelve people with their immediate needs. More would collapse preservation at the source before the crossing finished.
  Even twelve would consume the remaining systems.
  Mira tried reducing equipment, food, blankets, spare clothing. Each improvement bought a little capacity. None bought twenty-six additional lives.
  “We send the outpost,” she said.
  Farid shook his head. Its route had fused during the storm. The garden could receive people; the hull could not pass through the narrow surviving boundary.
  “Another rest cycle?”
  “It clears accumulated errors,” Salma said. “It does not make water.”
  Mira knew that. She hated hearing it spoken.
  Jonas told the settlement before she had exhausted her objections. Amira insisted the ledgers remain public. People asked practical questions at first. Then somebody asked why those who had arrived earliest should die so newcomers could leave.
  There was no answer everyone could live with.
  They agreed to protect the children and maintain representation among the people who could care for them. Volunteers could surrender places; nobody could bargain another person’s place away. The resulting list still felt like an injury.
  Jonas declined a place.
  Mira read the decision alone.
  She wrote a page, tore it out, and wrote another. She called him cruel. She apologized for calling him cruel. Finally she asked whether he understood what he was asking her to survive.
  His answer appeared in a hand she now knew as well as the younger one.
  YES. I DO NOT KNOW HOW TO MAKE IT FAIR TO YOU.
  She did not answer until morning.
  Then she sent every calculation back, still searching.
  

35  The Procession Explained
Jonas reached the far side of the underwater doorway through the ward’s oldest passage.
  Sami met him with a lamp.
  Beyond the door, shallow water covered the floor. A narrow dry layer moved with the lamp bearers. Within it, the sleepers could travel while the damaged route tried to match a safe destination.
  Outside it, depth and elapsed time disagreed.
  The procession had never been a parade of the dead. It was a stalled evacuation, walking the same route through successive local mornings. Each new stretcher marked another resident whose preservation system had failed.
  Sami had spent much of those years inside held intervals, emerging only for short journeys and care. His father had aged while maintaining the route outside them.
  “He said he would keep going until I could grow,” the boy told Jonas.
  Jonas knelt to inspect a damaged guide marker. Behind him, attendants shifted a stretcher around a flooded corner. Their exhaustion made every movement deliberate.
  He recorded the route’s remaining capacity. Most of its energy went into preserving the eleven sleepers. Waking them together would be fatal. Continuing the loop indefinitely was no longer possible.
  Amira reviewed the medical logs with the ward attendants. They selected no one for death; the failing systems had already made their own terrible limits. Care continued for every patient. The twelve-person crossing remained the only evacuation they could support.
  Sami carried Jonas’s camera toward the original landing.
  On its display appeared Mira’s first inspection, the camera light bright in green water. Sami lifted a hand and mouthed the warning Jonas remembered from the footage.
  Jonas had to turn away.
  Their beginning was still happening here.
  He found the ceramic tag beside the landing and recognized it. Sami asked him to help tie it to a lamp.
  “Where is the paper?” Jonas asked.
  The boy held out a cookbook page.
  Jonas wrote the warning about light beneath the sleepers. His hand cramped as he added the request that had frightened him for years.
  PLEASE HURRY SLOWLY.
  Then he watched the boy carry their first evidence toward the surface.
  

36  The Last Unobserved Chamber
The extra capacity lay behind the door that borrowed their knocking.
  Mira found it by following the preservation lines rather than the identity displays. The outer boundary enclosed a small stable volume, powered separately from the archive’s learning machinery.
  They could preserve that volume while keeping its contents unread.
  Farid built a covered instrument arrangement. It measured heat, mass limits, boundary position, and pressure without returning recognizable images. Salma checked that interpretation software remained disconnected.
  Inside, something moved once against a sensor.
  They did not give it a name.
  The chamber offered room for the evacuation controls to remain stable longer, but it did not increase the source’s water or living reserves. It could make the twelve-person passage safer. It could not turn twelve into thirty-eight.
  Mira understood the difference. She still checked the arithmetic again.
  At the service panel, an old handwritten instruction lay beneath a transparent cover.
  Keep the place before you decide the person.
  Its author had crossed out person and written contents above it.
  Someone had learned the same lesson badly enough to leave a correction.
  Jonas mapped the corresponding boundary at his end. Between them, the measurements held. The archive offered no faces while its viewing line remained shut.
  That night Noura’s voice came from behind the covered door.
  “Mira? I’m cold.”
  Farid wanted to answer. Salma wrote the exact words in the log, then asked whether the preservation instruments indicated a change.
  They did not.
  Mira said aloud that necessary care and the stable boundary would continue. She would not identify a speaker from its voice alone.
  The voice became angry. Then it became Jonas.
  She left before it could become the daughter whose name the frost had learned.
  In the galley Huda was wrapping food for twelve people. She had laid out thirteen cloths by habit.
  Mira folded the extra one and placed it inside her coat.
  

37  The Day They Must Wait
The last rest day began with a leak.
  Water entered the outpost’s lower landing through a seam they had sealed twice. Farid and two workers used independent pumps and timber braces. The active receiver remained off.
  At the settlement, the ward lost a section of ceiling light. Jonas moved lamps by hand. Amira stayed beside a sleeper whose breathing had become irregular.
  They had agreed that neither side would restart routing before the full seventh day ended. The preparation had taken six active days. The system needed the entire quiet interval, not a token pause shortened by fear.
  Without the book’s responsive channel, Mira had no messages.
  She occupied herself with food records, wound dressings, tool checks, and the mechanical clock. Huda made her eat. Salma asked her to sleep. She lay down and listened to water moving behind the walls.
  At the twenty-first hour Jonas appeared in the doorway.
  Young Jonas, unscarred, wearing the shirt with its original cuffs.
  “There’s time,” he said. “Open the receiver. I can bring everyone.”
  She looked at his hands. They had no years in them.
  “Tell me about Leila.”
  “We can talk afterward.”
  “Tell me something you learned from her.”
  He stepped nearer. The air smelled of home, of the damp wall above their kitchen window. Mira closed her eyes.
  “You don’t have to lose me.”
  She opened the galley roster and read every name aloud. When she reached Jonas’s, her voice broke. She continued through the settlement’s thirty-eight names, though some belonged to people she had never seen.
  When she finished, the doorway was empty.
  At the settlement, Jonas spent the final hours beside Dawud. The cabinet had begun speaking in his wife’s voice again. This time Dawud asked Jonas to stay while it talked.
  They listened without opening it.
  At the end of the full day, the independent clock released its quiet bell.
  Mira uncovered the book.
  Jonas’s first message read: STILL HERE.
  She answered with the same words.
  For that moment they were enough.
  

38  The Husband Who Grew Old
The stable passage opened onto the garden terrace.
  Jonas stood at its far end with one hand on the source control. His hair had thinned at the temples. The spectacles Mira had found in the dry ship now rested on his face.
  For several seconds they only looked.
  Then Amira took the control long enough for him to cross to the inspection line.
  Mira touched his sleeve first. Coarse fabric, repaired cuff, solid arm beneath it. She put her palm against his cheek and felt the scar move as he tried to smile.
  “You look tired,” he said.
  She laughed because it was such an inadequate thing to say, and because he had always found inadequate things when feeling too much.
  He held her. His body was familiar in its arrangement and unfamiliar in its weight. She could feel how carefully he stood to spare an aching knee.
  They remained together for nine measured minutes while Salma and Amira checked the boundary.
  Mira wanted to ask him about every year. Instead she told him where the cookbook lay.
  “Of course you brought it,” he said.
  He had brought his copy too. She touched its battered cover, darker and more repaired than hers. Several pages had been replaced with thin wood leaves.
  “Can you stay on this side while we work?”
  He looked toward Amira.
  The source control’s amber bands were breaking into red. A feedback mechanism had failed. It required continuous manual correction from the source, using someone familiar with its accumulated errors.
  Amira could hold it briefly. Jonas had spent years maintaining it.
  Mira understood before anyone explained.
  “No.”
  He took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his shirt. The ordinary gesture hurt her more than a farewell would have.
  “Let us finish measuring.”
  They did. They examined alternatives. They tested the manual hold without passengers. The result remained.
  Before returning, Jonas placed the carved toy boat in her hand.
  “Sami never had a river to try it on.”
  Mira closed her fingers around it.
  She did not promise to forgive him. He did not ask.
  

39  The Last Supper of the First World
Huda served the meal on both sides of the passage.
  The portions were small enough to carry safely and large enough to be called supper. Jonas sat where he could reach the source control. Amira passed bowls. Sami held the green cup with both hands, careful of its broken handle.
  Mira stood beside Jonas until he moved his stool and made room.
  “Sit down,” he said.
  For twenty minutes they were a household larger than any either had imagined.
  Nobody pretended the meal was a celebration. Dawud told Leila where he had hidden a packet of drawings for her. A ward attendant described the sleepers’ needs for a record that might never be used. Salma spoke each remaining resident’s name into an independent recorder.
  Then a support cracked beyond the control chamber.
  Jonas set down his bowl.
  The children crossed first with Amira. Three adults followed carrying supplies and one another’s hands. Mira checked each arrival against the list. On the source side, people waited with a composure that broke whenever someone looked too long at a familiar face.
  The twelfth traveler fell at the threshold. Farid reached her from the garden and pulled her clear.
  A red band spread across the control.
  Mira ran toward Jonas.
  He had both hands inside the manual hold, forcing its wooden linkage against a pressure that bent his shoulders. Water rose around his boots.
  “Let go,” she said. “Now.”
  Behind her, the last traveler’s legs remained inside the passage.
  Jonas looked past Mira until Farid dragged the woman fully clear.
  Then he looked at his wife.
  “There.”
  The control broke.
  The passage folded between them. Mira struck the closing boundary with the flat of her hand. Through its narrowing surface she saw him fall against the chair, then reach toward the cookbook beside it.
  On her side, his ink appeared across the open page.
  GOT THEM THROUGH. CHECK THE WATER. TELL SAMI—
  The line dragged downward.
  The water behind him climbed above the table.
  Mira kept her hand on the boundary until Salma pulled her away. Its surface had become stone. Beneath her palm, nothing moved.
  Later Huda found the bowl he had left on Mira’s side of the inspection line.
  One spoonful remained.
  She covered it and wrote the time on the lid.
  

40  A Recipe for Tomorrow
The pear in the garden kitchen was real.
  Huda divided it among the children while Mira checked the water. Jonas had asked her to do that, and the request supplied a movement she could perform without deciding anything.
  The garden held twenty-nine people: seventeen from the outpost and twelve from the settlement. Behind them the timber carrier remained visible through a dead inspection window, inaccessible. Its stores might as well have been on another planet.
  They had water for several weeks, food for twenty-three days at the ration Huda calculated, and growing beds planted in orderly rows.
  On the second morning the seedlings began to gray.
  Mira tested the water distribution. Farid checked illumination. Salma compared the household manuals with the service diagrams. Their measurements led to the reserve beneath the central bed.
  The living remainder had been spent during the crossing.
  The garden depended on a cultivated matrix that maintained its enclosed soil and water cycle. Its replacement could grow from a surviving reserve, but none remained in the nursery. The receiver had classified it as expendable capacity when the manual hold failed.
  The final evacuation had consumed what the manuals assumed would always be held back.
  They tried ordinary seeds from Huda’s packets in isolated containers. Some sprouted. The crop would take longer than their food could last and would feed too few even if every plant survived.
  Mira wrote the results into the cookbook. She checked the arithmetic with Huda, then with Salma. No one rounded the answer toward hope.
  That evening she told the adults everything. Nobody asked why she had failed to save a portion they had never known was being spent. Their restraint was almost worse than blame.
  Sami came to her after supper carrying the wooden boat.
  “Did he say anything?”
  Mira opened the last message. The boy traced the unfinished line without touching the ink.
  “He wanted me to tell you something,” she said. “I don’t know what.”
  Sami nodded. He had lived long enough among waiting people to recognize an honest absence.
  They floated the boat in the garden’s narrow channel. It traveled past empty beds and stopped against a clogged grate. Sami freed it gently.
  When Mira returned to the kitchen, the elderly woman stood in the doorway holding her empty bowl.
  This time Mira understood the question.
  “Twenty-nine,” she said.
  The woman’s face changed. Mira could not decide whether it was recognition or grief. Then the apparition faded, leaving the doorway open onto the sleeping room.
  Mira did not follow it. She took the bowl from the shelf, washed it, and put it away.
  In the cookbook she copied the last supper exactly as Huda had served it. Below that she wrote the knowledge they still possessed: how to save seed, how to keep water clean, how to build shelter, how to divide a meal without concealing the size of the portions.
  Then she added the facts the old manual had omitted.
  How many people had eaten. Who had remained behind. What the passage had consumed.
  On the final page she began a new heading.
  Tomorrow.
  She left enough room for measurements.
  Outside, the boat continued around the channel. Every time it passed the kitchen window, Sami lifted a hand as though someone aboard might see him.
  Mira counted the circuits until Huda extinguished the lamp.
  For the first time since the desert spoke, the book gave no answer.

Chapter 1
The Envelope Under the Bowl
Micah had written her name on an empty envelope.
  Nell found it beneath her breakfast bowl, where he had put it to keep the clean paper away from a patch of spilled tea. For several seconds she stood holding it as though it contained news. The letters were broad and slightly uneven. He had shaped the last one twice.
  “What's this?”
  “An envelope.”
  “I can tell you're a craftsman.”
  He turned from the stove. “I was going to write something. Then you came home.”
  She had returned from the receiving gallery late, carrying a broken instrument and somebody else's scarf. They had eaten, quarreled mildly about where she left wet boots, and fallen asleep with his hand warm against her ribs. In the morning the house smelled of toasted bread. The cabinet door closed properly. Renn was outside teaching children how to examine an animal crate from the ground, while the goat demonstrated weaknesses in his curriculum.
  Nell had once imagined happiness would feel like release. This had small demands. Remember the washing. Buy the right lamp. Answer when somebody asked whether you would be home for supper. She was learning to enjoy being expected for reasons that did not involve a fault report.
  She put the envelope in her coat.
  “You can still write it.”
  “Then give it back.”
  “Find another.”
  He caught her gently at the doorway. She kissed him with bread still between their hands, laughing when he tried to keep the crumbs from her hair. Then the public receiver sounded.
  The message was addressed to both of them.
  It had come through West Orchard, where Asa Vale was teaching and his daughter Tavi was nearing the end of her apprenticeship's fifth month. Lea had attached engineering diagrams. Beneath them was a photograph of an ocean above a city.
  Nell stopped chewing.
  The water lay in transparent vaults beyond the roofs, so deep that the moving dark forms within it could have been shadows or animals. White trees grew along terraces underneath. At the edge of the photograph, a moon hung broken into three enormous pieces.
  The settlement was called Serein. Its public berth had been withdrawn from the maintained network. Its people had not withdrawn with it.
  They wanted help assessing whether their homes could be kept.
  Micah read the request twice. They needed structural surveyors, fabricators, and a receiving team willing to arrange alternatives without promising that evacuation was the only answer. The message offered a limited visit, six weeks of agreed work, and a return berth held at North Terrace.
  “They've asked for you,” Nell said.
  “They've asked for us.”
  She looked at him. He had never traveled with her on a journey like this. His workshop, his habits, the things he knew how to make useful: she had grown accustomed to finding them in one place.
  “You'd come?”
  “I might. I'd need to ask people to cover the work here.”
  She glanced again at the photograph. A tiny figure stood on a balcony beneath that impossible water, hanging laundry.
  “Six weeks,” she said.
  “Then we decide again. Together. With whoever else has to live with the decision.”
  He went to find a clean envelope. This time, before he wrote her name, he put his beside it.
Chapter 2
A Sea Without a Shore
Serein did not look abandoned until they approached its official harbor.
  Then the lamps ended.
  Damas brought Mercy of Morning along a row of attachment frames large enough to receive whole districts of smaller craft. Unlit windows rose above them. A loading crane stood with its arm extended over an empty well. On the public display, the carrier remained marked unattended.
  Through the forward glass Nell could see cooking smoke.
  “That's a determined absence,” Micah said.
  A woman answered their arrival call from a maintenance tender. Her name was Ysra Dane. She wore a yellow headcloth, and one side of her face had the compressed look of someone who had recently slept against a tool bag.
  “The official harbor's isolated,” she told them. “Follow our current markers. Don't follow an old green signal just because it's prettier.”
  Damas asked her to repeat the receiving reference. She did. Micah sat beside Nell with his hands flat on his knees while Mercy turned toward a narrow mouth beneath the dark crane.
  He had imagined he would be frightened of the scale. Instead he was frightened of small sounds: a cup moving, a strap creaking, Damas taking a breath before bringing them close to something solid.
  Nell reached for his hand without looking away from the glass.
  The tender led them into a receiving chamber. The pressure check took long enough for his palm to become damp. When the door opened, a boy in a patched blue coat stood waiting with a basket of overshoes.
  “For the garden paths,” he explained. “The harbor floors have a fungus.”
  His name was Cor. He was twelve and employed by nobody; his grandmother had sent him because visitors kept arriving in the wrong footwear. He waited while each of them chose a pair, then conducted the expedition through a painted door.
  The ocean appeared at once.
  Micah stopped so abruptly that Nell walked into his shoulder.
  Above them, blue water occupied a vaulted sky. Light entered from a far curve and moved across deep suspended currents. The vault's principal ribs were visible, silver lines larger than streets. Between them, translucent pressure membranes held the water through field-supported frames whose oldest machinery no longer had a complete manual. Beyond the nearest terrace, a ray-like creature passed overhead with its broad body eclipsing the sun.
  Micah had no childhood memory large enough to receive the sight.
  Nell whispered his name.
  He looked down. Her face had changed. All the practiced attention, all the familiar quickness, had opened into something almost young enough to hurt him.
  “I wanted to see you see it,” she said.
  White trees grew beside a canal. A woman was sweeping blossoms off a wheelchair ramp. From an open window came the sound of someone practicing the same seven notes on an instrument that needed tuning.
  Cor waited patiently.
  “People usually do that here,” he said.
  Micah laughed. He was still holding Nell's hand, too tightly now. He loosened his grip.
  Over the distant roofs, the broken moon showed its black interior. A thin line of light crossed one of its separated pieces.
  “Train,” Cor said, following his gaze. “It's the late one.”
  He picked up the empty overshoe basket and began walking. Beneath the sea, their first ordinary street continued around a corner.
  A bell rang somewhere in the orchard. The ray-like creature turned overhead, its white underside marked by scars Nell could see only when the light passed through it. Micah reached toward the glassless air above him before remembering how far away the vault was.
  “Does it know we're here?” he asked.
  Ysra had followed them out of the receiving chamber. She looked upward.
  “It follows the warmer currents. The gardeners say it likes the music. The marine staff say the gardeners are making a complicated animal into a friend because they want one.”
  “Which do you believe?”
  “I know it turns when they change the circulation. I like the music myself.”
  They walked beneath an arch where sea-light moved across the wall in a pattern like submerged leaves. A line of fish scales had been carved into the stone at a child's height. Each scale bore a name and a year.
  Cor explained that children added their names when they first walked to the harbor alone. His was near the end. He showed it without appearing to show it, stopping conveniently beside a wall tap.
  Nell admired the carving. He shrugged and began telling them about the next crossing.
  Micah stayed a moment. Some names near the beginning had been touched until the letters were shallow. One had been cut out and replaced by a smooth patch. He wondered whether the person had left, died, or simply asked for a childish mark to be removed. He did not turn the uncertainty into a story without a resident to tell him.
  Beside the market, a woman sold paper sea creatures on strings. Their thin fins lifted in the fan breeze. Micah bought one shaped like the animal overhead and gave it to Nell.
  “For the room,” he said.
  “You haven't seen the room.”
  “Then wherever we're allowed to put it.”
  She held it by the string. The little fins turned between them, absurdly inadequate to the creature they had just seen. She loved it at once.
  Cor looked at the remaining toys with a professional eye and informed the seller that the tail was wrong. The seller said his grandmother owed her three empty jars. He took this as an unfair change of subject and led the visitors away.
  Micah began laughing before they reached the next corner. Nell leaned against his arm, carrying the paper animal above the street as if they had come on holiday.
  For several minutes they let themselves feel that they had.
Chapter 3
The Room With Two Keys
Their room had two narrow beds and a window that would not quite close.
  Nell looked at the beds. Micah looked at the window. Neither spoke until the landlady had shown them the shared kitchen, the evacuation stair, and the bell that meant to leave the upper rooms immediately.
  “Not the supper bell,” she said. “People have confused them. Supper can wait.”
  Her name was Dore. She gave them two keys on separate pieces of string.
  “You're both renting. If one of you loses a key, the other isn't responsible for inventing a door.”
  After she left, Micah put down his bag.
  “We could push them together.”
  “The beds?”
  “Unless you had another plan for the furniture.”
  They discovered that one bed was attached to the wall. The other had a wheel that refused to turn. By the time they had made enough space to set their things down, both were laughing. Nell sat on the fixed mattress and watched him kneel beside the damaged wheel.
  “You don't have to fix the room before we can be happy in it.”
  He looked up, caught.
  “I was making it easier.”
  “I know.”
  She held out her hand. He took it and sat beside her. Outside, water-light moved across a wall covered in faded paper flowers. Somewhere below, a child was refusing to wash.
  They kissed slowly this time. When he drew back, she kept her forehead against his.
  “I'm frightened,” he said.
  “Of the water?”
  “Of being here and discovering that all the things I'm good at are small.”
  She looked around at the bed, the loose window catch, the hooks where their coats would hang.
  “I'm counting on a few small things.”
  At the evening meeting, Ysra explained the work. The upper sea's circulation system was failing. Temporary supports could hold the occupied terraces while engineers reduced the load, but the old route office wanted the entire carrier sent inward for processing. No current receiving plan explained what happened to the people under the water.
  Half the town wanted to leave. Half wanted to remain. The halves overlapped inside families.
  Asa Vale arrived after the meeting began, carrying his own chair because Dore had told him they were short. He was there for a three-week teaching exchange. His return to West Orchard was already booked. Tavi had stayed with Lea, and he had mentioned this three times without quite hiding his pride or his worry.
  The residents asked him to speak on Sunday.
  “About what?” he said.
  “Love,” answered a woman beside the door. “Everybody tells us to do the loving thing. They mean different things by it.”
  Nell looked at Micah across the room. He was helping an old man adjust a hearing receiver, attending to the exact shape of his request.
  For the first time since arrival, she wished the six weeks would last longer.
Chapter 4
The White Orchard
The flowers fell upward when the circulation fans changed.
  Nell saw it from a bridge above the white orchard: loose petals lifting through the branches, turning, and settling against the undersides of leaves. Micah stood on the lower path with a basket over one arm. He had been sent to collect damaged fruit for the communal kitchen and had somehow acquired an elderly assistant who gave him instructions by striking his boot with a stick.
  “You missed that one,” the woman said.
  “It isn't damaged.”
  “I want it.”
  “Then I need a different basket.”
  The woman looked pleased. Her name was Nera, and she had spent forty years maintaining the orchard's training frames. Now her hands shook too much for the smallest fastenings. She supervised people who sometimes resented knowing she could still see what they had done wrong.
  Nell came down to help. Nera sent her up a ladder, then immediately told her she was reaching too far.
  Micah held the ladder. Neither made a joke about his new assignment.
  From the top, Nell could see into the next district. A theater stood beside a wide basin, its pale doors reflected in shallow water. Beyond it lay roofs partly submerged by an old overflow. People crossed between them on elevated boards.
  “The flood happened years ago,” Nera said. “Don't look as though you've just arrived in time to save us from it.”
  Nell climbed down.
  “I was wondering how you get supplies across.”
  “Better question.”
  They spent the afternoon following the supply route. A narrow market occupied the old theater foyer. Boats brought roots and lamp parts along the flooded street. Fish were kept in screened pools beneath a staircase. The beautiful view contained smells Nell had not expected: sour leaves, warm oil, wet rope drying too slowly.
  Micah bought a small pear with a brown seam. He cut it with his workshop knife and offered her half.
  “You look offended by the price,” she said.
  “It was expensive.”
  “And?”
  He held out the fruit. “I wanted to give you something here that wasn't a tool.”
  She took a bite. It was sharp enough to make her eyes water. He watched, alarmed, until she laughed.
  That evening she left half a second pear on his folded shirt. He found it while she was at the washbasin and came to stand behind her, warm hands resting at her waist.
  “You're going to make me financially irresponsible.”
  “Only about fruit.”
  In the little mirror above the basin, their faces fitted into one narrow frame. Nell leaned back against him and let the image last. She did not reach for her receiver to preserve it.
Chapter 5
What Love Can Carry
Asa preached in the theater with his shoes beside his chair.
  The lower floor held six inches of water. Residents had laid boards across the soundest aisles and put benches on the raised stage. Beneath a painted ceiling of dancing birds, the audience listened to the small noises of a building continuing to become something else.
  He read from First Corinthians thirteen.
  “Beareth all things,” he said. “Believeth all things. Hopeth all things. Endureth all things.”
  Nell felt Micah's shoulder against hers.
  “I have heard those words used to keep a person in a place where she was being hurt. I have heard them used to tell a tired parent that asking for help was a failure of love. That is a great deal to place on four phrases while forgetting that the same passage says charity rejoices in truth.”
  He rested the Bible on his knee.
  “To bear with a person is not to declare every burden properly yours. To hope for somebody is not to give him ownership of your tomorrow. And to endure is not to refuse every door God or a neighbor opens because you have begun to admire your suffering.”
  An old man asked whether marriage meant leaving when it became difficult.
  “Difficulty is not one thing,” Asa answered. “Neither is leaving. We must listen closely enough to learn what a person is actually asking. A visit to a dying parent, escape from violence, and abandoning a promise because a better invitation arrived are different journeys.”
  Nell thought of the journeys Micah had watched her take. He thought of the cabinet shelf he had once used to ask her not to go.
  “I cannot decide your particular promise from this stage,” Asa said. “If I counsel you, I should know your circumstances. If my advice leaves you carrying a weight I would not lift, you may ask me where my hands are.”
  A younger man stood near the rear of the stage. He had been listening with the rigid patience of somebody preparing to disagree politely.
  “If love doesn't ask for sacrifice,” he said, “what makes it different from choosing whatever you like?”
  Asa looked around the room. Several people nodded. He felt the danger of answering only the abuse of a word until the good thing inside it disappeared.
  “Love does ask things of us,” he said. “Sometimes things that cost more than we expected. A promise means I cannot treat your need as a passing inconvenience just because something easier has appeared. But before I call something your sacrifice, I should ask whether it is mine to request.”
  The man frowned.
  “Parents don't ask children whether they'd like to be fed.”
  “No. Nor should a hungry child have to agree that the parent is admirable before receiving supper. Some responsibilities come with a relationship before our feelings improve.”
  Nell heard a woman near the aisle make a short sound of agreement.
  “But a parent who never sleeps will eventually be less able to care,” Asa continued. “A husband who calls every boundary abandonment may be asking for control under a sacred name. A community that celebrates volunteers should know who takes over when a volunteer is ill. The cost is real. We should stop concealing its distribution inside a compliment.”
  Micah looked at the hands in his lap. He was good at work that continued until the thing was finished. People were not finished in that way.
  The younger man sat down, not persuaded in every part. Asa invited him to help examine the care plan after the service. If the sermon had made sacrifice sound unnecessary, he wanted that corrected. If the plan asked one person to supply it indefinitely, he wanted that corrected too.
  Then he returned to the passage's language about patience and kindness.
  “Patience is not a performance of having no needs. Kindness is not the absence of a difficult answer. Sometimes the truthful thing I can say to the person I love is that I cannot carry this alone. Then we find out whether our community is prepared to be more than an audience.”
  He asked them to sit quietly for a minute before the closing prayer. No music entered to tell them what to feel. They heard the water under the theater boards and a chair scraping as someone shifted a painful hip.
  Nell found Micah's hand. He turned his palm upward to receive hers.
  Afterward a woman asked him to visit a husband who wanted her to stay aboard while their daughters left. The husband was ill and afraid. Asa arranged to come that afternoon. He asked another resident to accompany him, somebody who understood the care available locally.
  Nell waited until the stage cleared.
  “How much of someone are you allowed to bear?”
  Asa looked at her.
  “What part are you trying to carry?”
  “I don't know yet.”
  “Then I won't hand you an answer that pretends you do.”
  She found this irritating. She had asked for a sentence she could keep.
  He saw her impatience and almost supplied one. Instead he asked whether she wanted to return when the question had acquired a name.
  Outside, Micah stood in the flooded foyer holding their shoes. She watched him put hers on the dry bench before his own. It was a kindness so familiar she had nearly stopped receiving it.
  She sat down and asked what he had heard in the sermon.
  They missed the first ferry because he answered fully.
Chapter 6
The Broken Moon
The moon had never been a moon.
  It was a service sphere built into the carrier's high light arch, its silver outer panels once used as a nighttime reflector. From the city below it had looked whole. A structural failure generations earlier had torn away its middle skin, leaving three vast sections held on their independent ribs. The dark interior showed through like an old wound.
  A maintenance railway still crossed its sheltered side.
  “You want us up there?” Micah asked.
  Ysra nodded. “The sea's secondary regulators run through the sphere. We need a fabrication survey. The train cabin is sealed. You stay inside until the receiving chamber says you're allowed out.”
  Nell watched his face.
  “We can ask somebody else.”
  “I didn't say no.”
  “You can.”
  He took time to consider. That mattered more than the answer. Then he asked for the inspection record and the planned return time.
  The train carried tools, a medic's supply case, and three workers who fell asleep before it left the lower station. Nell and Micah sat beside a long window. The first tunnel climbed through lit rockwork. At the outer curve the walls dropped away.
  The city opened beneath them.
  Micah's stomach seemed to remain at the station. Nell put her hand on his knee. He covered it with his own, feeling the shape of her fingers as a practical fact in a place his senses had decided was impossible.
  They crossed the moon's dark side above a fall of silver panels caught in an old safety lattice. Beyond the broken edge, the ocean bent away into a blue depth filled with lamps. The whole town, all its doors and arguments, occupied a lit shore under water that had no natural shore at all.
  “I wish I knew what to say,” he murmured.
  “You don't have to say anything.”
  She leaned against him. The train moved slowly enough for them to see a strip of painted stars inside the torn sphere, an old night sky abandoned by the people who had maintained it.
  At the upper workshop, Micah found the first failure he could understand without translating scale: a bracket worn oval around its pin. Someone had made three successive repairs without replacing the piece that took the strain.
  He measured it, asked what spare material existed, and began drawing.
  Nell watched from the doorway. His fear had not vanished. It had found room beside competence.
  On the return journey, he fell asleep against her shoulder. She stayed awake through the dark curve, carrying that small weight with an unreasonable happiness.
Chapter 7
A Dress for No Occasion
Nell bought the dress because she had no reason to buy it.
  It hung in a market booth between rain capes and a child's coat with one sleeve missing. The cloth was dark green, embroidered near the hem with uneven silver fish. It had been made for somebody broader at the shoulders and narrower at the waist.
  The seller offered to alter it.
  “For what occasion?”
  Nell opened her mouth. She could name meetings, inspections, a service, a public supper. All were plausible. None was why she had touched the fabric.
  “An evening,” she said.
  The seller nodded as though evenings deserved clothes.
  Micah had spent the day in the moon workshop. He returned with a burned thumb and a face brightened by having made something fit. Nell heard him on the stair and suddenly wanted to put the dress away.
  He stopped at the door.
  For a terrible moment she thought he would ask where she was going.
  “Turn around,” he said softly.
  “Why?”
  “Because I want to see the rest.”
  She did. The repaired hem swung around her ankles. He put down the tool case without looking at it.
  “I should have bought you one.”
  “I bought it for myself.”
  “Good.”
  He crossed the room. When he kissed her, she could feel that he was smiling. She had been desired before, but surprise could make desire seem new. His hands stopped at her shoulders, asking without a question, and she drew him closer.
  They went out to eat because the shared kitchen held three people frying fish and one furious person trying to dry clothes. Dore directed them to a balcony cafe whose owner served whatever the market had failed to sell.
  Their table overlooked a flooded square. Lamps inside the water made the submerged paving shine. A man in a small boat delivered bread through a second-story window. Music came from somewhere they could not locate.
  Micah asked about a story from her childhood. She began with the useful part, the thing it had taught her. He shook his head.
  “What happened?”
  She told him about stealing a pear, being caught, and being forgiven so quickly that she had resented losing the chance to make an elaborate defense. He laughed until she began laughing too.
  On the way home they stopped at the theater steps, where an old musician was playing beneath a rain cape. He had covered the instrument more carefully than himself. Water from a high collection channel fell behind him in a narrow curtain, striking the flooded paving with a continuous whisper.
  Micah put a coin in the open case. The musician looked at the green dress and began a different tune.
  “He thinks we're celebrating something,” Nell said.
  “We could.”
  “What?”
  He considered, solemn enough to make her laugh.
  “You wearing something with no pockets.”
  “There is a pocket.”
  “Of course there is.”
  She showed him the little hidden seam. Inside she had put the overshoe token, a scrap of paper, and the empty envelope. He touched its edge and recognized his handwriting.
  “You're still carrying that?”
  “You told me it wasn't finished.”
  His face changed, losing the joke without losing its warmth. He drew her away from the instrument case so the musician would not have to turn them into a public event.
  “I used to think I'd know what to say if I ever got enough time,” he said.
  “And now?”
  “Now I get time and want to be quiet with you.”
  She leaned against the stone rail. The green cloth darkened where the mist touched it. He stood close enough that her breathing changed before his hand reached her waist.
  “You can be quiet,” she said.
  He kissed her. The music went on, unconcerned with whether their moment had reached the right ending. She felt his thumb move against the seam at her waist and wanted the short walk to their room to become shorter.
  They stopped once more to buy bread for breakfast. Micah waited outside while she chose it, unable to stop smiling at a damaged lamp. When she came back, she pressed the warm loaf against his chest and told him not to drop tomorrow's happiness.
  He carried it upstairs with exaggerated care. At the landing she took his free hand, pulling him toward the door. The bread survived on the chair while they remembered the things they had not said on the steps.
  Back in the room, they left the window ajar. The cloth slipped from her shoulders. He kissed the place beneath her ear where she had been waiting all evening for his mouth.
  Later, with the sea's light moving across their joined beds, she thought of nothing beyond the room. The work would return in the morning. Tonight she let it wait.
Chapter 8
The People Who Would Stay
Micah met the stayers while looking for a pump handle.
  They lived on the outer terrace, where the older houses stood against the carrier's curved wall. The clinic occupied three connected rooms with doorways widened by successive generations of impatient carpenters. Its senior attendant, Daru, asked him whether he could make a handrail that did not chill a person's palm.
  He could.
  “Then do that before the pump,” she said. “People keep falling while somebody writes a grander repair plan.”
  He spent an afternoon fitting smooth sleeves over the existing rail. A woman named Ansel watched from a chair and criticized the curve. Her husband had died aboard. Her granddaughter wanted her to leave. She wanted both the granddaughter and the right to remain near the rooms where her life made sense.
  “They call us afraid of change,” she told him. “As if leaving everything wasn't also a way of being afraid.”
  Micah listened without agreeing on behalf of the granddaughter.
  The larger repair plan divided Serein into zones. Some could remain occupied if the new regulators held. Others would have to empty during a sea transfer. Nobody yet knew how much of the system could be restored. A safe temporary hold was possible only if enough trained people stayed to maintain it.
  Daru needed a fabricator for the clinic's aging support frames. Local workers were already committed to the water plant.
  Micah said he would speak with Nell before promising anything beyond the six-week visit.
  “Does she give permission?” Ansel asked.
  “She has a life with mine in it. I should ask what a change does to it.”
  Ansel looked at the new rail.
  “That's a better answer.”
  He told Nell that evening while she washed the work dust from her hair. She listened from the basin, water running down her neck.
  “How long?”
  “I don't know. I haven't agreed.”
  “Do you want to?”
  He had expected questions about the work. The directness of this one unsettled him.
  “Some of it. I know how to help there.”
  She dried her face. In the mirror he could see her trying to separate the fear of losing him from the engineering question.
  “Let's go together,” she said. “I want to meet them.”
  He kissed her wet forehead. Neither called the conversation settled. It was the beginning they had once failed to make before a journey.
Chapter 9
The Map of Leaving
The evacuation map had no arrows for grief.
  Nell knew why. A route diagram could show loads, distances, receiving capacity. It could not show where a woman kept the mark of her child's height on a doorframe. Yet when the council described a district as movable, people heard a statement about the whole of their lives.
  She went with the first household survey, entering homes only after the residents agreed. The questions were practical. Stairs. Equipment. Animals. Work. A receiving place someone actually wanted to reach.
  In one house a man had packed nothing except his wife's music books. In another, a boy wanted to take a bathtub because his dog slept in it. At the clinic, Ansel asked Nell to sit in the chair before recommending a shuttle seat. The support she needed made the standard harness painful.
  Nell sat. Daru fastened the harness. Within minutes Nell could feel where its force crossed the wrong place.
  “Now imagine four hours,” Ansel said.
  The standard transfer was removed from her plan.
  That evening Nell returned to the room with three new tasks and a temper. Micah had made supper. She began explaining the harness before she had put down her bag.
  He listened until she stopped for breath.
  “Will you eat while you tell me?”
  “I need to write it first.”
  “The food will be cold.”
  “Then don't wait.”
  The words struck harder than she intended. He set down his spoon.
  She saw the old pattern at once: his meal becoming her interruption, her urgency becoming the measure of what mattered. He saw his own part too, the request for supper carrying the unspoken weight of every evening she had once missed.
  “I'm asking for twenty minutes,” he said. “Not proof that you love me.”
  She put the bag down.
  They ate. The first minutes were stiff. Then he told her Ansel had ordered him to improve a hinge that had apparently been offending her for fifteen years. Nell smiled despite herself.
  Afterward he helped design a wider support cradle for the clinic transfer. She wrote the requirements in plain language while he sketched. They sent the design to Daru, who rejected their first handle placement.
  At bedtime Nell laid her head on his chest.
  “I don't want you to have to become urgent before I'll hear you.”
  He drew his fingers through her hair.
  “I don't want supper to become a test.”
  Outside, a ferry bell sounded across the flooded square. Neither got up to investigate it.
Chapter 10
Music Beneath the Water
The city held a dance on the night its second sea pump failed.
  The failures were related only by the date. A band had been booked months earlier. People had altered clothes, arranged care, and saved fruit for the punch. Ysra saw no safety reason to cancel the gathering in the upper theater, whose evacuation paths were clear.
  “We can be frightened tomorrow as well,” Dore said. “Tonight I've done my hair.”
  Nell wore the green dress. Micah borrowed a dark coat from a man who warned him not to improve any of its buttons without asking.
  Asa attended with Nera, who wanted a steady arm on the wet steps and no interpretation of what that meant. He danced once, badly, then sat beside the musicians and watched people have an evening he could not turn into evidence for anything.
  Micah had never learned to dance properly. Nell knew enough to make him feel the deficit.
  “Stop looking at your feet.”
  “They're where the mistakes are.”
  “Look at me.”
  He did. The floor moved beneath the rhythm of people who understood the steps. Nell guided him, then stopped guiding so fiercely. They found something smaller than correctness and stayed inside it.
  Beyond the high windows, the sea shone with scattered lamps. Long shadows moved through the blue. Inside, wet coats steamed by the door. An old woman slept with her mouth open while her husband protected her untouched cup from helpful people clearing tables.
  Nell felt Micah's hand at her back. She knew the exact callus beneath his thumb. Here, inside all this impossible scale, her body recognized him without effort.
  “Stay with me tonight,” she said.
  He looked startled.
  “I'm going home with you.”
  “I mean here. In this. Don't go away to make a plan.”
  He understood. He had been thinking about the clinic shift, about the six weeks, about whether happiness was something one had to secure before enjoying it.
  He bent his head and kissed her on the crowded floor.
  During the slower dances, the musicians lowered the lamps near the stage. The ocean beyond the windows became more visible, an immense moving blue against the close warmth of the hall.
  Micah touched Nell's ringless hand. They had made no ceremony of being together. He liked that freedom and sometimes wondered whether it concealed something neither had asked.
  “Would you ever want to marry?” he said.
  She missed a step.
  He immediately began explaining that he was not asking her to decide on the dance floor. She stopped him with her hand against his mouth.
  “Let me hear the question.”
  He waited.

Chapter 16

  He took his coat. They walked to the canal together, leaving the council to examine the remaining options with the people who would carry them.

  She felt the sentence in her stomach before it reached her understanding.
  “For how long?”
  “Until the frames are finished and another fabricator can maintain them.”
  “That's not a length of time.”
  “I know.”

  “I know that in my head.”

Chapter 21
Forty Hours
They had forty hours before the survey launch had to leave.

Chapter 22
What They Said Beside the Bed
The quarrel began over a second pair of gloves.
  Nell had packed them in Micah's tool case. He moved them to hers because the launch route would be cold. She moved them back because his workshop gloves were already splitting.
  It should have ended there.
  “I can look after myself,” he said.
  “Then answer when I ask whether you have what you need.”
  “You've asked me four times.”
  “Because you keep making it sound easy.”
  He looked at the bed, at the two keys, at the half-empty shelves.
  “Does this look easy?”
  She knew it was the wrong moment to continue. Exhaustion narrowed the room around them. She had spent the day being competent about a departure she had stopped wanting; he had spent it arranging to remain without letting his patients become an accusation against her.
  “You only learned to stay when it meant leaving me,” she said.
  The sentence seemed to strike his face.
  He stepped back.
  “And you only want the door closed when you're on the other side of it.”
  She stared at him.
  Neither statement was true enough to keep. Both contained a small piece of something recognizable, sharpened into a thing that could hurt.
  Micah picked up his coat. Nell wanted him to remain so she could take back the words. She also wanted him to go because she could not bear watching what they had done.
  He said he was walking to the canal and would return in half an hour. The care of that explanation made her cry after the door closed.
  He returned in twenty-seven minutes.
  She was still sitting beside the cases.
  “That was cruel,” she said.
  “So was mine.”
  “I don't believe you learned to stay for that reason.”
  “I don't believe you want to shut me out.”
  They said the corrections without asking the hurt to disappear. He sat on the other bed. For a long time the gap between them seemed impossible to cross because it was so small.
  He asked whether she wanted to hear what he had been doing beside the canal. She shrugged, afraid that the answer would become a defense.
  “Trying to decide whether coming back was going to make you think I'd won.”
  She looked at him.
  “Won what?”
  “I couldn't work that out.”
  The honesty was so unflattering that she felt her anger loosen a little. She had been making her own preparations while he was gone: how to explain the quarrel if he stayed away, which sentence proved he had never understood her, what silence would be most dignified when he returned.
  She told him.
  He rubbed his face with both hands.
  “We're making a future trial out of a room we still share.”
  “I wanted to be ready.”
  “So did I.”
  Neither laughed. It was too close to how they had lived through earlier injuries: assembling the case before someone else could define it. There were reasons for the habit. Those reasons did not make it useful between them now.
  Nell got up and brought water. She put a glass beside him, then sat again rather than using the task to move out of reach.
  “Tell me what you think staying means,” she said.
  He described the clinic agreement, the workers learning the frames, the date when the role would be reviewed. He wanted to finish without becoming the only person who could finish. He wanted to leave without making Ansel feel his departure meant she had been wrong to remain.
  Nell listened until he stopped.
  “And what do you think going means for me?”
  He began with the survey. She shook her head.
  He tried again. A route back to the people she knew. Work she was fitted to do. The chance to make later transfers possible. A room without him in it. He said the last thing quietly, and she cried because he had finally reached the part she was standing in.
  Then she described what she thought staying meant for him. When she reached the place where he would have to come back alone to their room, he set the glass down before his hand could betray him.
  Understanding had not changed the plan. It had changed who was alone inside it.
  She did not yet know whether that would be enough. He did not ask her to say it was.
  At last she held out her hand.
  “I'm going to miss you badly.”
  He crossed the room and knelt before her. The gloves lay on the floor between their feet. She pressed her face into his shoulder while he held the back of her head.
  “Then miss me,” he said. “Don't make yourself stop so leaving will be easier.”
  They slept after that, not peacefully, but together. At dawn each put one glove from the disputed pair in the other's case. It was an impractical arrangement. For once neither tried to improve it.
Chapter 23
The Platform at the End of the World

  “I know that too.”

Chapter 24
Where the Sky Broke
From the survey launch, Serein looked like a lantern being carried through a dark house.
  Its outer shell hid most of the sea. Through the open maintenance galleries Nell could see narrow bands of blue, the silver curve of the broken moon, districts lit with a steadiness the people inside could no longer trust.
  Then the receiving frame ahead gave the first warning.
  A reference lamp moved outside its tolerance. Nell called a hold. Damas held Mercy clear while the receiving station checked a mechanical latch. The passenger transport remained behind the last safe marker.
  The crack in the frame had widened under a load that had passed the earlier test. It could still receive the launch if they approached without a side correction. It could not safely accept the transport at the planned angle.
  Nell stared at the figures. She could see the empty places where new decisions would have to go.
  “We don't force it,” Damas said.
  “I know.”

  Nell wanted another surveyor beside her. There was none. She asked the station operator to read every value back. She asked Damas to keep the complete record. She did the work with fear present rather than waiting to become a person without it.

  At the receiving frame, Nell crossed. The launch settled against its attachment cradle with a hard jolt. A bruise opened across her hip where the restraint caught her. She could breathe. The seals held.

  When the final restraint released, the frame was isolated behind them.
  The return path disappeared from the display.
  Nell touched her ring with a shaking thumb. She had known the window would close. Watching it become a physical fact was something her knowledge had failed to prepare her for.
  She sent Micah one line before the relay switched to emergency traffic.
  We are through. I love you. The way back is closed.
Chapter 25
The First Message
Micah heard her voice nine days later.
  The text confirmation had reached Serein immediately, stripped to its essential lines. Longer personal recordings waited for a freight relay with spare capacity. The message arrived in the workshop after he had burned a pot of rice and spent an afternoon resenting everybody who asked whether he had eaten.
  He took it to their room.
  Nell appeared on the receiver with a bruise at the edge of her cheek. Behind her was a temporary wall, clean and unfamiliar. She looked at the lens, then away, then back.
  “I keep starting with the journey,” she said. “You have the report. I want to tell you I hate this room.”
  He sat on the bed.
  She described a window that faced a freight wall, the blue bowl unpacked beside the basin, the way she had reached for him in her sleep. She had eaten the first pears on a night she could not stop crying. She wanted him to know they were good.
  “I don't want you to think every message will be like this,” she said. “But this one is.”
  The recording ended with her hand reaching toward the controls.
  Micah played it once more, then made his answer before he could turn it into reassurance.

Chapter 26
A Place That Was Almost Home
The receiving settlement was called Lark Reach, a maintained waystation on the public line rather than the Lark Basin cooling station they already knew. Its resemblance to home was mostly in the things it lacked.
  No ocean over the roofs. No broken moon. No man kneeling beside a bed wheel, making a room workable before he believed he was allowed to rest.

She had expected the familiar negotiation to steady her. Instead she found herself watching couples carry ordinary purchases down the street.

  “There are survey stages I need to finish.”

Chapter 27
The Weight of a Falling Door
The clinic door fell because a temporary support was removed out of sequence.

Chapter 28

Chapter 29
The Last Message of Winter

Chapter 30
What the Preacher Would Not Bless
Nell found Asa at West Orchard after taking an ordinary passenger route for the first time in months.

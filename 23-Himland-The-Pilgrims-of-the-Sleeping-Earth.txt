  Chapter 1
 The Country of a Hand
  By dusk, the pilgrims had crossed three villages without leaving the giant's hand.
  Wheat grew in the hollows between his knuckles. An orchard spread across his wrist, its roots working into stone that had once been skin. The people called their country Himland. They planted, married, buried their dead, and taught their children where the mountain roads became dangerous.
  Nell had expected terror when she finally stood upon him. Instead, she smelled bread.
  Six months after their wedding in Serein, she and Micah had agreed to one last survey before returning to North Terrace for the promised visit. They had left the blue bowl on its widened shelf. The calendula had been entrusted to Cor, who charged them two stories and the right to object to the watering instructions.
  Mercy of Morning brought them to the outskirts of Himland. Damas remained with the vessel while Nell and Micah joined a local inspection party. A road collapsed beneath their return convoy. The rescue boats carried Micah toward the heart city; Nell's party was forced west by a river suddenly released from beneath the stone.
  Eleven days later, the public relays were still silent.
  She wore her ring on a cord beneath her shirt so the mountain would not take it from her finger.
  A woman was taking loaves from an oven built into the base of a thumb. Behind her, children chased a red hoop across the village square. Every few moments the hoop vanished behind the same crack in the paving and reappeared on the other side, though there was no space beneath the stones.
  Nobody remarked upon it.
  Tomas stopped his cart beside a well. His lame leg had swollen inside its boot, and he was trying to conceal the effort it took to remain standing. Sella lifted her sleeping daughter from the flour sacks. Eli, who was sixteen and hated to be called the youngest, went to look down the well.
  He returned without drinking.
  "Someone down there said my name."
  "Wells repeat things," Tomas said.
  "It said it before I did."
  Nell covered the lantern with her coat. For eleven dusks its flame had drawn them west, away from the broken crossing where she had last seen her husband. She had found it burning in a roadside station after the river took the bridge. Beneath it lay a strip of wood bearing an image of a vessel and a sentence cut deep enough to survive fire.
  BRING WHOEVER YOU FIND.
  She had taken it as permission to keep walking. She had not yet decided whether it was permission to hope.
  At the last village, the road divided. One branch climbed toward the shoulder. The other passed beneath a stone arch hung with hundreds of little bells.
  The lantern flame turned toward the arch.
  The bread seller watched it.
  "You'll need to leave your cart."
  "What's through there?" Nell asked.
  "The way he fell."
  The woman wrapped two loaves and placed them in Nell's hands.
  "We don't charge people taking that road."
  Tomas looked at the cart he had built from the doors of his ruined workshop. Everything they still owned was on it.
  "We'll come back," Nell said.
  The woman glanced at the bells.
  "Leave it where somebody can use it."
  Beyond the arch, the ravine walls bore great curving channels. Eli brushed moss from one and followed its course until it joined another.
  Fingerprints.
  He drew his hand back. All afternoon he had accepted the giant as something ancient and distant. Now he could imagine that enormous hand reaching for something.
  "What happened to him?"
  The bread seller, standing under her bells, gave no answer.
  

  Chapter 2
 Three Knocks Beneath the Water
  They descended until the village lamps disappeared. Ahead, a dark lake filled the hollow beyond the giant's fingertips. Across it rose a woman's face at the same impossible scale. Her brow was white with snow. One shoulder broke the water, wooded with firs.
  The two stone hands stopped short of touching.
  Beside the shore stood a shelter containing a folded blanket, a cup, and a book beneath glass. Nell wiped the dust away.
  Its first page held one sentence.
  He was still reaching for her when the stone took him.
  "Did he reach her?" Eli asked.
  Nell looked across the strip of water between those immense hands.
  "No."
  The lantern bent downward. Steps had been cut into the submerged palm. They led beneath the clear shallows to a door marked with the vessel.
  A light answered from its other side.
  Then came three knocks.
  Sella drew her daughter close. "Don't answer."
  But little Pip had already lifted her head.
  "They're cold."
  Nell knelt. The submerged door was barely deeper than her height, yet its light seemed miles away. She tapped the water three times with her fingers.
  The lake divided.
  It opened without violence, drawing back along two upright walls. Tiny fish hung in the water as if they had forgotten how to fall. A stairway stood between the walls, wet and shining.
  They went down.
  At the bottom, the door opened inward. An elderly man waited with a towel over one arm. Behind him stood a kitchen full of steam, shelves, and hanging onions. His right hand trembled so badly that he had to steady the door with his shoulder.
  "How many?" he asked.
  "Five," Nell said.
  He looked beyond her.
  A sixth person was climbing down through the lake: a thin boy with a brass collar and a bloody rag wrapped around one foot.
  Eli stepped toward him. "Where did you come from?"
  The boy pointed upward, toward the giant's chest.
  "They said the Ark was full."
  The old man set down his towel.
  "They've said that for a very long time."
  He held the door while Nell helped the boy over the threshold. Only when all six were inside did the lake close above them.
  The kitchen windows showed dark water. Something enormous passed beyond the glass, carrying a forest of lamps upon its back.
  Pip pressed her nose to the pane.
  "Is that a fish?"
  "Once," said the old man. "Sit down before the soup burns."
  

  Chapter 3
 The House Under the Lake
  The old man called himself Abel. He gave the wounded boy a basin and asked permission before touching the brass collar.
  "What is your name?"
  "Six hundred and twelve."
  "Your other name."
  The boy watched the soup.
  "Ash."
  Abel nodded as if he had been entrusted with something breakable.
  They ate beneath a ceiling that dripped stars. Each drop vanished before it reached the table, releasing a faint scent of cold grass. Tomas studied the beams and decided, aloud, that none of them ought to hold.
  "They don't," Abel replied. "The house is held from underneath."
  He showed them a floor window. Far below, a vast hand of light supported the little kitchen.
  Sella moved Pip's chair away from the glass.
  Ash ate until he was sick, cried because he thought the food would be taken away, then slept with bread clenched in his hand.
  Only afterward did Abel speak of the road.
  Before he did, he unlocked a metal case beneath the table. Its seal showed two linked towers and the words HIGH LIAISON BUREAU. Within lay a receiver without a visible speaker.
  "You won't hear the living giants yourselves," he said. "Their messages pass through a restricted office. I hold one field channel."
  "Why?" Eli asked.
  Abel checked the receiver's dark face.
  "That explanation fills buildings. Tonight you need soup."
  Nell had seen enough official equipment to know when a question would consume a journey without helping anyone take its next step. She left it there.
  The Ark lay beyond the sleeping giant, past the city built around his heart and the country along his face. A sickness was spreading through Himland. Wells spoke with borrowed voices. Winter grass grew warm. Occasionally a road remembered the day it was made and returned its travelers to that morning.
  "Is he waking?" Nell asked.
  "Something is forcing him toward waking."
  Abel brought a brass instrument from a shelf. It had a needle, but the needle pointed toward Ash's collar instead of north.
  "The heart city is taking people. It tells the villages the giant must be restored or the country will crumble."
  Tomas flexed his swollen ankle. "And will it?"
  "Eventually. Stone weathers. But there are other ways to keep a home."
  Nell uncovered the lantern. "Why did this bring us here?"
  Abel looked at the sleeping boy.
  "You found somebody."
  She disliked how small the answer was. She had imagined instructions, a map, perhaps a reason Micah had disappeared while she survived.
  Instead, Abel gave them clean blankets.
  That night Nell woke to a woman's breathing beyond the wall. She went to the window and found the submerged stone face looking toward the giant's unfinished reach.
  For one terrible moment, the enormous eye opened.
  Inside it was a sky full of burning ships.
  When Nell turned, Abel stood behind her.
  "That was a memory," he said.
  "Whose?"
  "Hers."
  He drew the curtain gently.
  "It isn't yours to carry alone."
  

  Chapter 4
 The Toll at the Wrist
  Morning arrived inside the house before it reached the lake. Abel opened another door, and the pilgrims stepped onto a hillside far above the water.
  He remained in the doorway.
  "I keep this crossing. I cannot walk yours."
  He gave Tomas a walking stick, Sella a packet of salt, and Ash a wool scarf to hide the collar. To Nell he gave nothing.
  She was ashamed that she noticed.
  The path crossed the wrist at a bridge suspended between two cliffs. A man in a clean gray coat sat beside a balance. Behind him stood shelves crowded with little bottles.
  "One memory each," he said. "Pleasant ones buy the safest passage."
  Tomas reached for his purse.
  "We don't accept money."
  From the nearest bottle came a child's laughter. Sella went white.
  "What do you do with them?"
  "Preserve them. Surely you have happiness to spare."
  Nell thought of Micah at the washbasin, trying to shave while she deliberately made him laugh. She had been guarding that morning for eleven days, returning to it whenever the broken crossing came back.
  She could give the collector an uglier memory. She could give him the last sight of Micah on a rescue boat beyond the broken bridge.
  The man's eyes brightened.
  "Grief has value too. You'll feel better without it."
  Behind the bridge rail, Eli found an old footpath descending into brambles. Its first steps were broken. Far below, white water hammered the rocks.
  "There's another way."
  "For the able," said the collector, glancing at Tomas.
  Tomas planted Abel's stick.
  "Then they'll have to help me."
  The descent took all day. Twice Nell regretted their choice. Tomas fell hard enough to split his lip, and Pip cried from hunger. They carried Ash across a stream and divided the last loaf beneath a dripping rock.
  At sunset they reached the bridge's far end.
  Above them, the collector uncorked a bottle and smiled at somebody else's summer.
  Sella rubbed warmth into Pip's hands.
  "I almost gave him her first word."
  "What was it?" Eli asked.
  Sella laughed, unexpectedly.
  "More."
  Pip, waking against her, whispered the same word.
  They all laughed then, even Tomas, though it reopened his lip. The collector stopped smiling.
  

  Chapter 5
 The Orchard of Unfinished Days
  They entered the orchard at noon and found the same noon waiting beneath every tree.
  Fruit hung among small glass spheres. In one, a woman remained forever at a gate, seeing her son return from war. In another, a man held a fishing line just before the greatest catch of his life broke the river.
  The orchard keeper wore an apron stitched with watch hands.
  "You may rest inside any hour you like."
  Tomas sat down heavily. "For how long?"
  "Until you want something else."
  Nell saw her kitchen.
  There was no mistaking the green cup with its chipped handle. Micah stood at the table, peeling an apple in a single ribbon. Her own hands reached to steal a piece.
  She walked toward the branch.
  "Nell," Sella said.
  "I know."
  But she did not stop.
  The sphere warmed against her palm. She could smell the apple. She could hear rain beginning, ordinary rain, before the crossing had taught her another way of losing him.
  Micah looked up.
  "You've been gone a long time."
  Her knees weakened.
  Behind her, Ash cried out.
  The orchard keeper had pulled down his scarf and was examining the collar.
  "Escaped property," he said. "A serious matter."
  Nell let go of the sphere.
  The kitchen shattered into harmless light. She crossed the orchard and put herself between the keeper and the boy.
  "He is traveling with us."
  The keeper's face lengthened. Roots lifted beneath his boots.
  "Then you share his debt."
  Tomas seized a fallen branch and pushed Ash toward the gate. The trees leaned inward. Their fruit began to speak, offering each traveler precisely what would make them turn.
  Sella heard her dead mother. Eli heard applause. Tomas heard someone say his leg was whole.
  Nell heard Micah call her cruel for leaving him twice.
  She walked through the gate shaking so violently that Sella had to support her.
  Outside, the lantern had nearly gone out.
  "Was it him?" Nell asked.
  Nobody answered quickly.
  At last Tomas said, "It knew what you wanted. That isn't the same thing."
  Nell touched the cord beneath her shirt. They had already survived eight months of broken messages between Serein and the route home. She had promised at their wedding to return to difficult conversations. She would not exchange the man who might disagree with her for an obedient hour inside a fruit.
  Nell sat on the road until she could breathe again. Then she stood, took Ash's hand, and continued without looking back.
  

  Chapter 6
 The Valley That Repeated a Name
  Ash's fever began at dusk. Beneath his collar, the skin had split into a fine red seam.
  They sheltered in a ruined chapel where the roof admitted moonlight in the shape of a wheel. Sella boiled water while Tomas examined the metal. There was no hinge, clasp, or join.
  "I could cut it."
  Ash caught his wrist with surprising strength.
  "They hear."
  He pointed to a pinprick in the brass. From it came a sound like distant machinery.
  Beyond the chapel, the valley answered.
  Six hundred and twelve.
  The words rolled across the dark hills.
  Ash tried to crawl beneath a bench. Nell knelt beside him and asked about his home. At first he shook his head. Then he spoke of a mother who made candles, a window above a goat pen, a sister who could whistle through two fingers.
  "Her name?"
  "Ada."
  The valley repeated the number.
  "And yours?"
  "You know."
  "Tell me again."
  "Ash."
  Eli took it up, then Sella. Pip woke and said it sleepily. Tomas kept working while the little chapel filled with the boy's name.
  When his saw finally bit the brass, every window brightened.
  Men stood outside carrying white lamps.
  Their leader wore a gray coat buttoned at the throat. He raised an empty hand.
  "Return the child and nobody else needs to be inconvenienced."
  Tomas leaned hard on the saw.
  The leader sighed.
  "You've been frightened by language. He is a voluntary contributor to a public restoration. His family signed."
  Ash began to sob.
  Nell remembered how often hungry people signed whatever stood between them and bread.
  She moved a bench across the door.
  The brass broke.
  The white lamps went black. Outside, the men shouted to one another in suddenly ordinary voices.
  Tomas held up the two pieces. His hands were bleeding.
  "That," he said, "was badly made."
  They escaped through the chapel's empty graveyard. Behind them the valley attempted the number once more, but the sound faltered halfway across the hills.
  By dawn Ash's fever had begun to fall.
  

  Chapter 7
 The Country on the Arm
  For three days they climbed the giant's forearm. Forests grew from the old folds of his skin. Rain moved uphill through silver channels and gathered in pools that reflected constellations nobody could see overhead.
  At a shepherd's hut they learned the giant's name.
  Oren.
  "Himland's what we call where we live," the shepherd said. "Oren's the name on the old liaison maps. My grandmother kept one."
  His wife set out bowls of barley. She had six fingers on one hand and wore six wedding rings, one for every year her husband had spent missing in a winter that lasted elsewhere for a week.
  They spoke of such things without surprise.
  Then Nell mentioned the men with white lamps.
  The wife stopped serving.
  "You shouldn't bring that trouble here."
  Ash lowered his bowl.
  The shepherd looked at him for a long time. He had a grandson in the heart city. The family had received a letter saying the boy was essential to the restoration and could not return until spring.
  There had been three springs since.
  "Did you know a boy called Petren?"
  Ash shut his eyes.
  "There were so many."
  The shepherd left the room.
  His wife followed him, and through the wall the pilgrims heard an argument conducted in whispers. Sella began collecting their things.
  But the shepherd returned carrying a pair of boots small enough for Ash.
  "They were his," he said. "They ought to go somewhere."
  Nell offered payment. He looked offended until she put the coins away.
  That evening Eli helped him repair a fence. The old man worked slowly, pressing each nail straight with a broad thumb.
  "Do you think he's alive?" Eli asked.
  The shepherd kept his eyes on the wood.
  "I think you'll be more use to him if you reach the city than if you stay here trying to give me the right answer."
  Before they left, he drew a route upon a scrap of sailcloth. It avoided the watch roads and crossed the elbow by a shepherds' stair.
  Under the route he wrote his grandson's name.
  Nell folded the cloth carefully. Their journey had acquired another person, though he was not yet with them.
  

  Chapter 8
 The White Ships
  They reached the elbow beneath a sky full of ships.
  The vessels flew upside down, their dark hulls above their masts. Rotors turned within rings of blue fire, appearing motionless until a blade caught the sun. Great nets hung toward the ground, each containing barrels, machinery, or people.
  Ash dragged them under a rock.
  "Supply fleet."
  A bell sounded from the largest vessel. Along the mountain, hidden stations answered with brief white flashes.
  One net snagged a stone pinnacle. A crate fell, burst open, and scattered bright metal plates across the slope. The ship lurched. Beneath the broken crate swung a smaller cage containing two children and a woman.
  Its rope began to fray.
  Tomas was already moving.
  "We can't reach them," Sella said.
  "We can reach where they fall."
  They hauled an abandoned shepherd's awning from a wall and stretched it between them. Eli climbed the pinnacle to cut the cage loose before it struck the rocks. Nell shouted until the woman inside understood to crouch over the children.
  The cage fell.
  The awning tore. Tomas went down with a cry, but the cage struck cloth before stone. One child broke an arm. All three lived.
  Above them the vessel circled.
  Eli hung from the pinnacle, too frightened to climb down. A white searchlight found him.
  Nell lifted the lantern, though she did not know what she meant to accomplish.
  Its flame spread across the rock face, revealing hundreds of doors where there had been only stone.
  One opened beside Eli. A woman's hand caught his collar and pulled him inside.
  The searchlight swept past.
  The fugitives crowded into a second doorway. Within lay a narrow passage smelling of warm wool and smoke.
  A woman with a scar through her eyebrow shut the stone behind them.
  "Who gave you that?" she asked Nell.
  "I found it."
  The woman's hard expression changed.
  "Then somebody knew we were running out of time."
  Her name was Ruth. The passages, she explained, had once been places the giant sheltered living creatures beneath his skin.
  They had not opened for human hands in a hundred years.
  

  Chapter 9
 The Rooms Within the Stone
  Ruth led them through chambers lit by veins of amber. In one grew a forest whose pale leaves whispered against a ceiling of crystal. In another, a river crossed the air above their heads, carrying blind fish through darkness.
  Families occupied the smallest rooms. They cooked on iron stoves and kept gardens in trays. Children learned letters by scratching them into dust.
  "Runaways," Ruth said. "Some from the city. Some from villages that sold them."
  The rescued woman sat with her children while Sella helped an old nurse splint the broken arm. Tomas's leg was worse. He admitted it only when he could no longer stand.
  Nell asked Ruth about the Ark.
  "I used to guide people there. Before that, I worked field recovery for the Federal Passage Office. We handled local evacuations. The High Liaison Bureau handled giant contact."
  "Used to?"
  Ruth showed her a doorway bricked from the inside.
  "The heart city bought the upper road. Anyone who couldn't pay disappeared below. I brought people back down here until the patrols found the entrances."
  "You could come with us."
  Ruth glanced toward the rooms full of sleeping families.
  Nell understood the invitation had been too easy.
  That night Pip wandered into the amber forest. Nell found her beside an enormous shape enclosed within translucent stone: a winged animal curled around three eggs.
  Its eye followed them.
  Pip raised her hand. On the other side of the crystal, the creature raised a feathered claw.
  "It isn't dead," Pip whispered.
  Ruth arrived carrying a blanket.
  "Very little here is."
  She told them Oren had once carried creatures through a burning country. When the burning reached him, the passages sealed. The creatures slept. People built homes upon the shell that protected them.
  "Then the giant chose this?" Nell asked.
  "I don't know what he chose. I know what his body has done."
  From somewhere deep within the stone came a slow, heavy beat.
  Every lamp guttered.
  In the crystal, the winged creature clutched its eggs.
  Ruth closed her eyes and counted until the sound ended.
  "That used to happen once a year," she said.
  "How often now?"
  "Tonight is the third time."
  

  Chapter 10
 A Place at the Table
  They remained three days because Tomas could not travel and the rescued child could not sleep without screaming.
  Nell hated those days at first. Every delay felt like a hand holding Micah farther away. She repaired blankets too fiercely, snapped at Eli, and spent an hour trimming the lantern wick though it never shortened.
  On the second evening, Ruth put a sack of onions beside her.
  "You can do those while you worry."
  They worked at a table scarred by years of knives. At its other end, Ash was showing Pip how to make a whistle from a nutshell.
  "My husband may be ahead of us," Nell said.
  Ruth kept peeling.
  "May be."
  "I saw him carried west. The flood boats were going toward the heart city."
  "Then we'll ask when we get there."
  Nell looked up.
  Ruth nodded toward a younger woman setting out bowls.
  "Dena knows the passages now. She says I've been using everybody needing me as an excuse to stay where I understand the danger."
  For the first time Nell noticed how tired Ruth looked.
  At supper a man refused to sit beside Ash. He had lost his own son to the collectors and could not bear to look at the collar scar.
  Ash rose without protest.
  Nell caught his sleeve.
  Then she moved her bowl to the empty place beside him.
  Nothing remarkable happened. The man ate with his face turned away. Halfway through the meal he began to cry, quietly and angrily. Nobody required Ash to comfort him.
  Later he left an apple beside the boy's blanket.
  The next morning, Tomas managed six steps with Abel's stick. Sella sewed pockets into the inside of everyone's coats. Ruth unbricked the road and gave Dena her ring of keys.
  Before leaving, Nell went back to the kitchen and finished the last sack of onions.
  It would save somebody an hour.
  She had begun to suspect the road to the Ark contained more such hours than grand revelations.
  

  Chapter 11
 The Market of Reasons
  The market stood along the giant's upper arm, beneath canopies made of stitched clouds.
  Here a traveler could purchase a convincing excuse for almost anything.
  One stall sold Necessity in paper packets. Another offered Peace of Mind by the spoonful. A richly dressed woman advertised clean consciences for magistrates, householders, and those obliged to make difficult decisions.
  "We need food," Sella said. "Actual food."
  They found a baker between a dealer in inherited grievances and a man selling apologies that sounded sincere in seven languages.
  Nell had almost paid when a familiar voice spoke behind her.
  "You always did forget to bargain."
  Micah stood beneath a yellow awning.
  For a moment she could not move. His hair was shorter. There was a pale scar beside his mouth. He wore a gray city coat with the sleeves turned back, exactly as he used to wear his work shirts.
  She struck his chest with both hands, then held him so tightly that he laughed and winced together.
  "Where were you?"
  "Trying to find you."
  They spoke at once, correcting one another, touching sleeves and faces to make sure there was substance beneath the sight.
  The rescue boat had carried him to the city's lower docks. He had asked for a relay to Serein and discovered every public channel suspended. Work clearance seemed the quickest way to obtain a search permit. He had repaired what he was given until a supervisor finally let him leave the city.
  His injured knee was swollen again. Nell saw how he leaned on the awning post and remembered the white orchard, the chair Dore had set beneath the tree for their wedding.
  "You should be sitting."
  "I've been looking for you."
  "You can do the next part sitting beside me."
  He laughed shakily and lowered himself onto a crate. She knelt before him and put her forehead against his. For a little while neither tried to turn being together into an explanation.
  "You can all come back with me. There's food. Real beds."
  Ash backed behind Ruth.
  Micah noticed.
  "What's wrong?"
  Nell told him about the collars.
  His expression tightened, but not in surprise.
  "That's an emergency program. There have been abuses. They're being corrected."
  Ruth said nothing.
  Micah took Nell's hands.
  "You don't understand how close we are to losing the whole country."
  From the stall beside them, a merchant held out a packet of Necessity.
  Micah looked at it, then away.
  Nell kept her hands in his. She had found him. The finding had not settled what they must do next.
  

  Chapter 12
 The Husband Returned
  They slept in a boardinghouse where the stairs led to different floors according to what each lodger owed.
  Micah paid for everyone. For one night the stairs behaved.
  Nell sat beside him at a narrow window overlooking the market. His familiar shoulder against hers felt almost unbearable. She wanted to ask ordinary questions. She wanted to tell him about the awful bread Tomas had made from wet flour, the way Pip snored, the mornings she had forgotten he was gone until she reached for him.
  Instead, she asked what work he did.
  "Inventory. Parts, mostly. Rail couplings. Conducting plates."
  "And people?"
  He rubbed his face.
  "I don't handle that department."
  She waited.
  He told her about the restoration engine. Oren's heart had weakened. The city believed a great charge of living memory could make it beat strongly enough to restore flesh. Once awakened, the giant would carry everyone to safe land.
  "Everyone?" Nell asked.
  "The approved refuge platforms."
  "What about the villages?"
  Micah looked down at the street.
  "They're building more platforms."
  Below, an old woman argued with a merchant over a reason she had bought. It had worked when she abandoned her brother. Now, months later, it had stopped working.
  Nell drew her knees toward her chest.
  "Come with us."
  "Into caves? With a lamp you found?"
  She flinched. He saw it and reached for her, but she moved before he could take her hand.
  They sat apart until the market lamps went out.
  Then Micah said, "I thought you might be dead. They said the western stations were gone. I needed a permit. Then I needed something to do while I waited."
  Nell knew the hunger beneath that sentence. She had carried it herself.
  "You matter without their engine."
  He cried only once, soundlessly, with his forehead against the cold window frame.
  In the morning he agreed to take them into the city. He would show them the records. If the program was what they feared, he would help.
  Ruth demanded his word that he would not report Ash.
  Micah looked at the boy for a long time.
  "You have it."
  

  Chapter 13
 The Bell Beneath the Ribs
  The heart city appeared at sunset, tier upon tier of white houses climbing the giant's chest.
  Its central tower passed through a crack between two petrified ribs. Suspended within the tower hung a black bell so large that clouds collected inside it.
  Every street pointed toward the bell.
  At the gate, clerks measured their shadows. Those whose shadows bent toward the tower entered freely. Those whose shadows leaned away were directed into another queue.
  Ash's shadow did neither. It trembled beneath him like a trapped bird.
  Micah laid his permit on the desk.
  "Family and dependents."
  The clerk counted.
  "You registered one wife."
  "We've acquired difficulties."
  For the first time since the market, Nell heard the old humor in his voice.
  The clerk did not smile. He reached for Ash's scarf.
  Ruth dropped her bag. Its contents scattered across the paving: thread, dried beans, a comb with missing teeth. While the clerk complained, Micah moved his work badge over the permit.
  The clerk's eyes changed. He stamped it without further counting.
  They entered.
  Nell waited until the gate was behind them.
  "What does that badge mean?"
  Micah buttoned his coat over it.
  "More than I said."
  The city smelled of soap and hot metal. Public fountains played music. Above the streets, white ships carried families to platforms anchored along the giant's breastbone. The platforms shone with polished rails and flower boxes.
  Below them, workers in brass collars hauled stone.
  Pip pointed toward a fountain where water formed a woman's smiling face.
  "Why does she keep smiling?"
  Sella turned her gently away.
  That evening Micah took them to a room overlooking the lower yards. Workers filed beneath a gate, removing gloves and caps for inspection.
  Ash seized the window frame.
  Across the yard stood a girl with cropped hair and a scar on her chin. She was helping a smaller child tie his shoe.
  "Ada."
  Nell reached for him too late.
  He was already running toward the stairs.
  

  Chapter 14
 The Girl Who Kept the Names
  Ruth caught Ash before he reached the street, but his cry had carried.
  Across the yard, Ada looked up.
  She did not shout or wave. She moved the smaller child behind her and touched two fingers to her mouth.
  A whistle rose, thin and piercing.
  Ash began to laugh and cry together.
  That night Micah arranged a meeting in an empty laundry. Ada arrived carrying sheets over one arm. She had learned to walk as though every journey were an errand somebody important had ordered.
  Ash held her around the waist until she could barely breathe.
  "I thought they'd taken you below," she whispered.
  "I got out."
  "I know that now, idiot."
  She kissed the top of his head.
  Ada had kept a record of the children transferred to the engine chambers. She stitched their names into the hems of uniforms, where inspections rarely looked.
  Ruth unfolded a shirt. Inside its hem ran lines of tiny blue letters.
  Nell found Petren's name halfway down a sleeve.
  Beside it Ada had sewn a little circle.
  "That means he's alive," she said quickly. "Lower ward. His chest is bad."
  "What does a cross mean?" Eli asked.
  Ada took the shirt from him.
  "That I don't know where they went."
  She looked at Micah's badge.
  "You can get below."
  He shook his head too quickly.
  "My clearance is for supplies."
  "Supplies go below."
  For a moment the laundry was silent except for dripping pipes.
  Then Micah asked how many people remained in the wards.
  "Four hundred and nine yesterday. They took twelve this morning."
  He closed his eyes.
  Ada watched him with an exhaustion that made her seem older than everyone else in the room.
  "You can be sorry on the stairs," she said. "If you're coming."
  Nell expected him to defend himself.
  Instead he unbuttoned his coat and placed the badge on the table.
  "Show me what you need."
  

  Chapter 15
 The Engine of Borrowed Lives
  Micah took Nell and Tomas below as inspectors. Ruth remained with the others, planning an escape route through the city drains.
  The engine chambers lay within the space beneath Oren's ribs.
  Nell had imagined furnaces. Instead, she found beds.
  They stood in long rows beneath glass pipes. Each bed held a person wearing a brass collar. Light traveled from the collars into the pipes, carrying fragments of voices, gardens, songs, arguments, the smell of a father's coat.
  At the far end, those stolen moments turned a wheel.
  The wheel pressed an iron tongue against the black bell's stem.
  With every stroke, somewhere deeper, the giant's heart answered.
  Tomas leaned against a bed rail. His face had gone gray.
  "They're burning what people remember."
  "Not burning," said a voice behind them. "Converting."
  A tall man stood in the doorway. His coat had no wrinkles. When he moved, his shadow remained perfectly still.
  Micah bowed his head.
  "Director Vey."
  Vey approached a sleeping child and adjusted her blanket. The gesture was careful, practiced, almost tender.
  "This country holds millions of lives," he said. "Would you lose them all to spare a few hundred unpleasant weeks?"
  Nell looked at the child's hand. The nails were bitten to the quick.
  "Do they get their memories back?"
  Vey straightened the blanket again.
  "There are things a person can live without."
  Tomas asked to see the refuge plans.
  "You're a carpenter?" Vey said.
  "Among other things."
  "Then you understand a structure must rest upon something."
  He opened a leather folder. The refuge platforms covered only the heart city. Vast areas of the giant's arms, hands, and face were shaded red.
  Nell recognized the lake, the bread seller's arch, the shepherd's hut.
  All red.
  "Evacuation zones?" Micah asked, though his voice showed he knew.
  "Acceptable losses."
  Micah reached for the folder.
  Vey closed it.
  "Be grateful your wife found you before the rising."
  

  Chapter 16
 The Man Without a Shadow
  Vey invited Nell to his office while Micah and Tomas waited outside under guard.
  The office windows looked directly into the black bell. Around its inner rim were inscribed thousands of figures. As Nell watched, one changed.
  Vey offered her a chair.
  "Your husband repaired the transport system. Don't punish him because you've encountered what we transport."
  "You call people property."
  "Administratively. Language keeps large undertakings manageable."
  She did not sit.
  Vey opened a drawer and took out a child's shoe. It was blackened along one side.
  "My daughter was seven when the eastern ridge collapsed. I carried her for two days before I accepted that she had died."
  His voice remained steady. That made it worse.
  "I decided there would be no more such helplessness."
  Nell looked at his motionless shadow.
  "What happened to it?"
  He followed her glance.
  "The first engine required a willing contributor. I gave it everything that prevented me from continuing."
  "Including her?"
  His hand closed around the shoe.
  For the first time his expression slipped.
  "I remember the facts."
  Outside the window a child in a distant ward began to sing. The song stopped halfway through a line. Vey waited until the pipes carried the fragment away.
  "There will be a place for your companions. Even the escapees. Give me the lantern and I'll sign the order now."
  Nell touched its warm handle.
  "Why do you need it?"
  "It belongs to a liaison field set. Old doors respond to it, even when the public routes are closed."
  She thought of Ruth's hidden rooms, the creature protecting its eggs, the hand beneath Abel's kitchen.
  "You can't reach all of him."
  Vey set down the shoe.
  "I can reach enough."
  Nell backed toward the door.
  He did not stop her. He merely rang a small bell on his desk.
  Outside, the guards took Micah's badge. Tomas swung Abel's stick, breaking one man's wrist before the other struck him down.
  As they dragged Nell away, Vey called after her.
  "You will still have to choose who you can afford to save."
  The lantern vanished from her hand.
  For the first time since the broken crossing, she stood in complete darkness.
  

  Chapter 17
 The Prison of Good Intentions
  Their cell had a window, clean blankets, and a printed explanation of why their detention was necessary.
  Tomas lay on a bench with blood drying in his hair. Micah sat beneath the window, looking at his empty hands.
  Nell wanted to hurt him.
  She wanted the hurt to make sense of the beds, the red map, the badge that had opened every door until the doors mattered.
  "How much did you know?"
  He answered without looking up.
  "Enough to ask."
  She waited for more. He gave her none.
  For a long time she walked between the wall and the door. Her anger needed distance, and there was nowhere to put it.
  At last Tomas spoke.
  "Water."
  They both moved. Nell held the cup while Micah lifted him. For a moment they had to work together with the old, practiced closeness of their marriage. It made her cry, which made her angrier.
  Micah let her set Tomas down before he said, "I was afraid you'd look at me like this."
  "Then you should have helped them before I arrived."
  "Yes."
  There was no defense to strike against.
  Near midnight a tapping came from the drain.
  Eli's voice followed it.
  "If either of you has finished being in prison, we've found a route."
  Nell knelt.
  The drain cover was bolted from inside. Tomas had no tools. Micah searched his pockets and produced a thin brass gauge used for measuring conducting plates.
  He put it into Nell's hand.
  "It might fit."
  It did.
  They worked for an hour. Tomas could not crawl without help, and the tunnel smelled so foul that Nell gagged. On the other side waited Ruth, Sella, the children, and Ada with a stolen ring of keys.
  "Where's the lantern?" Ruth asked.
  Nell told her.
  Micah reached for Ada's keys.
  "I know the service stair to his office."
  Nell caught his sleeve. She was not ready to trust him. She was also not ready to let him disappear again.
  "We go together."
  

  Chapter 18
 The Night of Uncounting
  Ada had spent months waiting for somebody with access to stop being afraid. She had used those months well.
  Laundry carts stood ready beside the lower wards. The night cook had hidden food in coal sacks. A guard whose brother wore a collar had agreed to look away at the western door.
  The plan was fragile, crowded, and dependent on people who had every reason to change their minds.
  It began with Eli ringing the wrong work bell.
  Workers poured into corridors before their supervisors arrived. Sella and Ruth entered the wards carrying clean linen. Ash went with them, refusing every attempt to make him wait.
  Nell and Micah climbed toward Vey's office.
  Halfway up, she stopped.
  "I can't promise you we'll be all right."
  He nodded.
  "I know."
  "I mean us."
  He looked at her then.
  "I know."
  They climbed on.
  Vey's office was empty. The lantern stood beside the blackened shoe. Micah wrapped it in his coat while Nell opened the refuge folder and took the red map.
  In the drawer beneath it lay rolls of names, each matched to a number.
  They carried those too.
  Below, Tomas used a stolen cutter to break collars. He worked with both hands trembling. Every freed person had to be told where to go, whom to follow, what belongings to leave.
  Petren could barely breathe. Ash and Ada put him on a laundry cart and pushed him toward the drain stair.
  Then the black bell rang.
  It had never rung before.
  The sound passed through walls and teeth. Every brass fragment leaped toward the tower. Tomas threw himself over Pip as broken collars flew like knives.
  In the office, the window split.
  Vey stood beyond it on an iron maintenance bridge, his coat whipping in the bell's wind.
  "You have mistaken delay for mercy," he shouted.
  He pulled a lever.
  Deep beneath the city, Oren's heart struck hard enough to lift the floor.
  

  Chapter 19
 The Broken Platforms
  The city moved three feet upward and settled six feet lower.
  Nell fell against Micah. He caught her before the broken window could take them both.
  Outside, the refuge platforms swung upon their anchors. One struck a tower. Houses burst open, showing beds, supper tables, a room painted for a baby.
  Vey stared upward.
  For a moment he seemed no more capable of understanding the disaster than anyone beneath it.
  Then he ran toward the engine stair.
  Nell could have followed. Instead she saw a child clinging to a flower box on the nearest platform.
  She went through the window.
  Micah seized her belt while she crawled onto the maintenance bridge. Below her, streets folded into cracks. She stretched Abel's towel, still tucked in her pack, toward the child.
  "Hold this. Both hands."
  The child's mother appeared above him, trapped beneath a railing. Nell could reach one or the other.
  Vey's words returned with horrible clarity.
  Choose who you can afford to save.
  She shouted for help.
  A clerk on another bridge looked away.
  "You!" Nell screamed. "Take this end!"
  He stared, then stumbled toward her. A second person followed. Together they made a line across the bridge. Micah climbed past them to lift the railing.
  They saved the child and his mother because Nell could not save them alone.
  Below, the western drain door collapsed just after Ruth brought the last laundry cart through. Twelve people remained trapped on the city side.
  Ada counted them twice. She refused to turn twelve into an acceptable loss.
  Tomas found a coal chute. By the time Nell and Micah reached the others, the twelve were crawling out one by one, black with dust.
  Four hundred people stood in the giant's dark passages.
  Behind them the city bells began calling residents to the platforms that had failed.
  Nell uncovered the lantern.
  Its light fell upon her own hand, then moved toward the sound of people crying in the streets.
  She closed her eyes.
  "We have to open the doors."
  

  Chapter 20
 The House of Many Enemies
  Ruth led them to a great inner chamber where a petrified tree supported the ceiling. Its roots formed rooms. Its branches held nests large enough for carts.
  The freed workers crowded beneath it while Nell returned to the outer wall.
  With the lantern against the stone, she walked from one hidden door to the next. Eli followed, shouting instructions into the ruined streets.
  People came carrying children, birdcages, ledgers, silverware, things they could not imagine leaving until the doorway became too narrow.
  A city official demanded priority.
  Ruth gave him two water buckets.
  "The spring's through there."
  He stared at her. She waited. At last he took them.
  There were fights that night. A freed worker recognized a guard. The guard recognized the boy who had broken his brother's nose. A platform resident complained that somebody had stolen her blankets, and Ada asked which ward the blankets had originally come from.
  Nell could not settle it all. She helped Ruth make rules simple enough to remember: nobody alone with someone they had harmed; no weapons at the sleeping places; food served by need; grievances written down for later hearing.
  The guard surrendered his knife. The worker refused to forgive him. Both received soup.
  Micah worked beside Tomas repairing stretchers. Nell watched him take instructions from Ada without explaining who he used to be.
  Near dawn, a girl arrived at the last door carrying Vey's blackened shoe.
  She had found it in the collapsed office and thought somebody might need it.
  Nell took it.
  The shoe was smaller than Pip's.
  Outside the chamber, Oren's heart beat twice, painfully close together. Dust fell through the roots.
  Ruth spread the shepherd's sailcloth on a table. Beyond the chest, the road continued toward the giant's throat. A mark there showed the last route to the Ark.
  Behind it, another hand had drawn a warning.
  DO NOT SPEAK WITH HIS VOICE.
  

  Chapter 21
 The Throat of the Mountain
  They left the sanctuary under Dena's care. She had brought the hidden families along the lower passages and now had more keys than she could carry comfortably.
  Ruth remained with the pilgrims. Ada came for the engine records. Petren stayed with the nurse, a letter to his grandparents folded beneath his pillow.
  The route to the throat crossed a cavern filled with suspended stones. Each stone held a word the giant had once spoken.
  Some were small as seeds. Others were larger than houses.
  When Eli brushed one, a voice filled the cavern.
  COME HOME.
  He fell to his knees.
  Across the cavern, doors opened onto things they knew: Sella's old yard, Tomas's workshop, the lane where Ash and Ada had played.
  Nell heard Micah call from behind her, though he stood at her side.
  Ruth pointed to the warning on the sailcloth.
  "Don't answer as him."
  They passed a word shaped like a black sphere. Within it stood an image of Vey at his engine, listening through a speaking tube.
  Micah understood first.
  "He's trying to make the giant command himself awake."
  The engine could move the heart. It could not give Oren's consent. Vey needed the voice chambers to turn a stolen instruction into the giant's own word.
  Then a smaller stone broke away and floated toward Nell.
  SAVE HIM.
  Inside it she saw Micah beneath the floodwater. Her hand reached toward him before she remembered where she stood.
  Micah took that hand.
  "I'm here."
  The stone stopped.
  One by one, they spoke their own names. The false doors closed. The great words drifted aside, admitting a narrow path.
  At the cavern's center stood a gate without hinges. Nell placed the lantern against it.
  Inside stood a deserted field station. Its door bore the same two towers as Abel's case. Ruth found the power key behind a broken panel and brought the receiver to life.
  Abel's voice arrived after a long delay.
  "Your field lamp has registered. Keep this channel clear."
  Nell gripped the desk. "Can you reach him?"
  "The office is carrying your request. Wait."
  No great voice filled the room. There was only the small ordinary noise of the receiver and five people breathing.
  At last Abel returned.
  "The authorized response asks who is walking upon him."
  Nell swallowed.
  "People who need his help."
  Abel relayed her words. A minute passed.
  "He wants to know whether he has hurt them."
  

  Chapter 22
 What the Giant Chose
  The station wall brightened. Abel identified the image as a released memory record, not an open conversation. The liaison office had cleared only this fragment.
  They stood upon a plain beneath a red sky. Cities burned beyond the horizon. Above them flew the first white ships, their hulls inverted to escape a fire that climbed toward anything living.
  Oren knelt in the plain, alive and immense.
  Across from him stood Ilyra, the woman whose face now rose from the lake. Between their hands lay a cradle containing an entire valley.
  People ran along the valley's roads. Animals filled its forests. Nell saw the winged creature from the crystal chamber, flying with her eggs held carefully beneath her breast.
  The fire came west.
  Oren held out his hand to Ilyra. She had already begun to turn to stone.
  "There isn't time," she said.
  He lowered himself across the burning land. Stone climbed his arms. His body became shelter as living creatures entered the chambers beneath his skin.
  Ilyra lay opposite him, shielding the valley's other edge.
  Their fingertips almost met.
  The fire passed overhead.
  Then the memory changed. Centuries ran through the sky. Seeds arrived, then shepherds, then roads. People built homes upon the hands that had protected them. They forgot the fire. They forgot the choosing.
  Oren remained aware enough to feel their small lives, but too deeply asleep to speak.
  A caption crossed the station glass.
  ONE ATTEMPT AT MOVEMENT. SETTLEMENT LOST.
  Nell saw it: a hillside collapsing while a young giant's grief became a thousand human funerals.
  The next caption took longer.
  VOLUNTARY STILLNESS MAINTAINED.
  Sella held Pip against her.
  Nell pressed the receiver key. "Tell your office they're forcing his heart. Vey's engine is pulling against him."
  Pain passed through the recorded image. The red sky split, showing the black bell beyond it. Abel asked for the engine's location, then left them waiting while he contacted another desk.
  "The reply confirms the injury," he said when he returned. "His strength is being drawn where the machine pulls it."
  "Where is the Ark?"
  The wall showed a far horizon. A light stood there, small and steady.
  "Still coming," Abel said.
  Nell had walked so far toward a place she believed was waiting.
  Now she understood the vessel was traveling too.
  "What must we do?"
  Abel listened for so long that Nell thought the line had failed.
  "One request has been released to the field team," he said at last. "Let him keep his hands still."
  Ruth wrote it down. Nobody asked how many desks the sentence had crossed. They had their work.
  

  Chapter 23
 The Road Across His Face
  They emerged beneath Oren's jaw into a morning filled with snow.
  The road ran along his face. Villages occupied the hollows beside his mouth. Bridges crossed the channels beneath his closed eyes. Along his brow, white cedars bent beneath ice that rang softly when the wind changed.
  From here, Himland stretched behind them for days of travel. Smoke rose from the heart city. Beyond its ruined towers, the refuge platforms hung crookedly over the chest.
  Far west, beyond the giant's forehead, a light moved through the clouds.
  The Ark.
  It was still too distant to distinguish from a star.
  At noon they reached a village where the doors stood open and every clock had stopped at a different hour. The residents had left for the heart city's promised platforms.
  A woman remained in a rocking chair beside the stove.
  "My husband will be back from the fields," she told them.
  Her husband had been dead nine years. The neighbor who usually cared for her had fled that morning.
  They could not make her understand the journey. Sella asked her name instead.
  "Hester."
  "Would you help us look after Pip, Hester?"
  The old woman examined the child gravely.
  "She needs a warmer hat."
  She packed one, then allowed herself to be led away.
  They took turns helping her along the road. Their pace slowed. Nell watched the distant light and felt the old fury of delay rising again.
  Then Hester stumbled, and Nell caught her.
  The old woman squeezed her arm.
  "You're a good girl. Your mother will be wondering where you've got to."
  The words found an unguarded place in Nell, a longing to be somebody's child for an hour, with nothing required but coming home.
  She stopped looking at the light for a while.
  That evening a tear formed at the corner of Oren's closed eye. It was clear as glass and larger than the village they had left.
  It hung there, trembling, while somewhere far behind them the engine pulled again.
  

  Chapter 24
 The Bridge of the Almost Saved
  The tear fell during the night.
  Its flood tore through the cheek roads, carrying bridges, cedars, and the abandoned village toward the giant's jaw.
  Ruth heard the water before anyone saw it. She drove them uphill toward an old suspension bridge crossing the crease beside Oren's eye.
  Hester could not run. Micah lifted her, though she struck his shoulder and cried that he was taking her from home.
  Behind them came other travelers: a family who had turned back from the city, three platform guards, an injured mule pulling a seed cart.
  The bridge keeper began cutting the ropes.
  "It'll take the flood if I leave it!"
  "People are still coming," Ruth shouted.
  He pointed to the water. His own children waited on the safe side.
  Ruth caught the knife in his hand.
  The blade went through her palm.
  For an instant both stared at the wound. Then she closed her fingers around his wrist.
  "Help them cross."
  He let go.
  Tomas braced a broken plank while Eli crawled back for the seed cart driver. Sella took the keeper's children farther uphill so he could work without watching them.
  The flood hit the lower ropes.
  Nell lost her footing. Micah caught her with one hand and a cable with the other. His face twisted with effort.
  "Let the bag go!"
  Inside it lay the engine records, Ada's shirts, Vey's refuge map.
  Ada cut the strap herself.
  The bag vanished into white water.
  Together they dragged Nell onto the bank.
  The bridge broke just after the mule crossed. Nobody spoke for a long time. Ruth's blood dripped steadily into the snow until Sella bound her hand.
  Ada sat beside Nell.
  "I remembered the names before I stitched them," she said.
  She began reciting.
  Ash joined her. Then, one by one, the others repeated what they could, fixing the names among them where no single flood could take them all.
  

  Chapter 25
 The Ark Beyond the Brow
  They reached the giant's brow at dusk.
  Beyond it lay a sea of cloud. The Ark rose through that sea with a sound like timber settling beside a warm hearth.
  It was enormous, yet its size did not diminish the people waiting on the shore. Windows shone along its dark hull. Gardens grew beneath glass. Birds circled the upper decks, returning through openings shaped for wings of different breadths.
  A gangway lowered toward the stone.
  Abel stood at its foot, carrying his towel.
  Nell stared.
  "You said you kept the crossing."
  "This one too."
  Behind him, people came forward with blankets, water, stretchers. Hester allowed a woman with gray hair to lead her inside. Pip went only after Sella promised to follow.
  Nell remained on the stone.
  "The engine's still running."
  Abel's face grew grave.
  "Yes."
  "There are thousands behind us. More than thousands."
  "There are."
  She wanted him to say the Ark would solve it. She wanted an end to being asked for the strength she had already spent.
  He showed her the vessel's lower doors. Several opened upon stone, sealed against it. Roots of pale light extended from their frames into the giant's brow.
  "We can reach the chambers he shelters. But the engine has drawn the pathways toward the heart. Until its grip is broken, the crossings cannot widen."
  Ruth lowered her bandaged hand.
  "Then we go back."
  Abel did not praise her. He brought a chair and made her sit while someone changed the dressing.
  Tomas examined a model of the engine on a nearby table. It had been built from Micah's description, sent ahead through one of Oren's old passages while they crossed the face.
  He pointed to the great turning wheel.
  "Stopping that won't be enough. The bell's under strain. Release it wrong and the whole stored charge goes into him at once."
  Micah nodded.
  "We have to let it go somewhere else."
  Nell looked toward the Ark's gardens, its warm windows, the room where Pip was already asleep.
  For the first time, safety was close enough to refuse.
  

  Chapter 26
 Those Who Went Back
  They did not all return.
  Sella stayed with Pip and Hester. Ash stayed because Ada asked him to, then argued until she admitted she wanted one person she loved somewhere beyond the engine's reach.
  Tomas tried to rise. His leg would not bear him.
  Abel brought a worktable to his chair.
  "Tell us what to build."
  By dawn, Ark carpenters had made six hinged braces and a wooden housing for the lantern. Tomas checked every pin. His hands, which had built a cart from ruined doors, were steadier when there was work before them.
  The plan depended upon the giant's shelter passages. If the lantern could open those passages beside the engine, the stolen living memories might find their way back to the people from whom they had been taken. Any remaining force would be carried into the Ark's empty lower reservoirs, built for fire long before any pilgrim had been born.
  Nobody promised it would work.
  Nell, Micah, Ruth, Eli, and Ada went back.
  The route beneath the skin shortened what had taken them days. Ruth followed a route released through Abel's restricted field channel. Whatever the living giants knew about these old shelter passages, the pilgrims received only directions.
  They passed the winged creature's chamber. Cracks now spread across the crystal. A pale beak pushed through one egg.
  "Hurry," Ruth whispered.
  At the sanctuary, Dena had organized the refugees into work groups. The city official carried water with blistered hands. The former guard slept under watch beside a child he had helped bring through a collapsed door.
  They gave the sanctuary leaders the evacuation plan. When the passages opened, everyone would follow the warm wind.
  Then Micah took Nell aside.
  "There's something about the engine I didn't tell you."
  She felt her body prepare for another wound.
  "The final governor is manual. Somebody has to hold it while the charge drains."
  "Where?"
  He pointed toward the deepest line on Tomas's drawing.
  Inside the wheel.
  Nell stared at him.
  "And you waited until now?"
  "I was afraid you'd think I wanted dying to make me right."
  "Do you?"
  He answered slowly.
  "I want to live long enough to become somebody you can believe."
  

  Chapter 27
 The Last Argument of Vey
  The city above the engine had emptied into frightened pockets. Streets ended at fresh ravines. White ships hovered over broken platforms, unable to land.
  Vey remained below.
  He had dismissed the last operators and attached the conducting harness to himself. Light ran through his coat. His shadow crawled across the wall, no longer matching his movements.
  Nell entered carrying the lantern in its wooden housing.
  "It's over," she said.
  Vey laughed once.
  "Look outside. It has only just begun."
  He showed them the great viewing aperture. Through it they could see the giant's hand far below, the fingers beginning to flex. Villages broke apart along the knuckles.
  "He will rise now whether I guide him or not."
  Micah moved toward the controls. Vey drew a pistol.
  Ruth stopped beside Nell. Eli and Ada remained behind the doorway, holding Tomas's braces.
  "Your daughter," Nell said.
  Vey's face hardened.
  She placed the little black shoe on the railing.
  For several seconds he stared at it.
  "You don't get to use her."
  "Neither do you."
  His pistol trembled.
  Nell did not move closer.
  "There are children in the passages. You can help us open them."
  "And let everything I've done amount to this?"
  Below, a pipe burst. Out of it came laughter, a lullaby, somebody saying goodbye. The sounds swirled through the chamber seeking bodies that were no longer there.
  Vey covered one ear with his free hand.
  "I kept them alive."
  "Some," Ada said from the doorway.
  She began naming the missing.
  Vey turned the pistol toward her.
  Micah struck his arm. The shot broke a gauge. Glass fell like hail.
  Ruth kicked the pistol beneath a machine. Eli ran for the first brace while Nell pushed the lantern housing against the engine's central seam.
  Vey tore free of Micah and reached for the master lever.
  "You cannot bear what happens if you're wrong."
  Nell held the lantern steady.
  "Then help us be less wrong."
  The seam opened.
  Warm wind entered the chamber.
  

  Chapter 28
 The Hand That Remained Still
  Doors opened throughout Himland.
  They appeared in cellars, behind ovens, among the roots of forest trees. At the shepherd's hut, a wall of stone swung inward and revealed Petren leaning upon a nurse's arm.
  His grandmother dropped her bowl.
  At the bread seller's village, the little bells rang together. People followed their sound beneath the arch, carrying children and the things they could manage.
  Deep within the engine, Eli locked the first brace. Ada fitted the second. Ruth, working one-handed, wedged the third beneath a pipe while Micah climbed into the governor cage.
  Nell lifted the lantern.
  Light poured from the beds' empty collars. It passed through the opened seam in threads, each seeking the person to whom it belonged.
  In the sanctuary, a woman suddenly remembered her son's face. She dropped to her knees so hard that Dena thought she had been struck.
  On the Ark, Ash remembered his mother singing while she poured candle wax. He called for Ada before remembering where she had gone.
  The engine wheel slowed.
  Then the fourth brace broke.
  The charge struck Micah's cage. He screamed but held the governor down. Across the viewing aperture, Oren's fingers rose another fraction.
  Nell felt the giant's pain through the lantern handle. Beneath it she felt his effort, immense and failing, to remain still.
  "We're opening the roads," she whispered. "They're coming through."
  Vey stood beside the master lever, light tearing from the harness beneath his coat.
  He looked through the aperture at the villages upon the hand.
  For the first time, he seemed to see houses rather than red areas on a map.
  He seized the broken brace and pushed its metal end into place.
  The current took him.
  Micah shouted. Vey could not answer. His shadow climbed the wall and vanished into the open seam, carrying the shape of a small child against its chest.
  The wheel slowed again.
  Outside, the giant's fingers stopped.
  Nell saw roofs suspended above falling stone, people running toward an open doorway, a mother turn back for a dog.
  Oren held his hand still.
  

  Chapter 29
 Whoever You Find
  The governor cage began to close around Micah.
  Its bars were not moving. The chamber itself was contracting as the stored charge drained from the engine. Iron folded inward, returning to a shape it had possessed before the city built its machinery.
  Micah could no longer reach the exit.
  "Go," he said.
  Nell set down the lantern.
  She had feared another separation on every road since Serein: another failed message, another closed passage, another chance to do whatever might keep him.
  This time she did not step into the machinery alone.
  "Ruth! Eli! I need you!"
  They came.
  Ada pulled a conducting cable loose. Ruth wrapped it around the cage. Eli found a beam for a lever. Nell pushed beside him, her shoulder burning against hot iron.
  The gap widened enough for Micah's arm.
  He caught Ruth's belt. Together they dragged him through while the cage crushed the place where his chest had been.
  Nell held him only long enough to know he was breathing.
  Then she turned toward Vey.
  He lay beside the broken brace. The harness had burned through his coat. His eyes were open, but he seemed unable to understand what stood before him.
  "Leave him," Eli said.
  Nell looked at the boy. He had seen the beds. He had carried people Vey had numbered.
  "You don't have to touch him. Help Micah."
  She went to Vey herself.
  He tried to speak. She bent close.
  "I don't remember her voice."
  Nell took the blackened shoe from the railing and placed it in his hand.
  "We'll carry you."
  She and Ruth lifted him onto a fallen door. Ada took its other end.
  The lantern's light stretched toward the seam. Beyond it lay the Ark's lower deck, crowded with people holding ropes.
  They hauled the injured through.
  Nell came last, carrying the lantern. Behind her, the engine folded into silence.
  For a heartbeat the seam showed the whole sleeping country: the great hand upon the world, Ilyra beyond the lake, the little doorways receiving people from roads that would never appear on an official map.
  On the Ark's deck, Abel held the field receiver.
  "Status?" he asked.
  "The engine is stopped. Our team is through."
  He relayed the report. Nell sat on the floor beside Micah and held his uninjured hand while they waited.
  At last Abel lowered the receiver.
  "Message received," he said. "He is resting."
  There was no further explanation. Nell had no strength left to want one.
  

  Chapter 30
 The Shore After Morning
  Oren did not rise.
  The Ark's windows looked east as the sun came over Himland. Across the giant's hand, damaged villages lay quiet. His fingers rested where they had for centuries, sheltering the valley between him and Ilyra.
  Stone would weather. Roads would need rebuilding. The people aboard the Ark began arguing about both before breakfast.
  Nell found the noise comforting.
  The vessel had room, though room did not mean ease. Families quarreled over sleeping places. Children were sick after eating too quickly. People who had lost everything discovered they still had opinions about soup.
  Ruth organized passage crews. Dena opened a school in a room whose windows showed three different seasons. Tomas sat at a workbench with his injured leg supported, teaching Eli how to fit a joint that would hold without a nail.
  Ada began the names again.
  This time she wrote them in a book, with space beside each for what the person wished to say. Some pages filled quickly. Others remained blank. Nobody was required to speak before they were ready.
  Petren came aboard with his grandparents and the old boots Ash had outgrown during the journey. The shepherd tried to return them. Ash insisted he keep them for somebody else.
  Vey lived.
  He remained under guard in the infirmary. He would answer for the wards and the missing. Nell visited once and found him struggling to write his daughter's name without copying it from the back of her shoe.
  She left paper and did not tell him what forgiveness ought to feel like.
  Micah recovered slowly. Some mornings Nell wanted him close. Other mornings the sight of his city coat brought the wards back. They spoke about those days without allowing eleven frightened days to erase the years before them.
  He had not built Vey's plan. He had helped keep its transports running, and he would have to live honestly with what he had failed to question. She could love him and still require that honesty. Their vows in the white orchard had left room for difficult answers.
  They sent a message to Serein and another to North Terrace. Damas answered from Mercy of Morning: alive, waiting, profoundly irritated, and willing to postpone the irritation until he had seen them. Asa sent a shorter message. Tell us where to come. Tavi included a drawing of a brace for Micah's knee with a stern note about leaving the fitting to somebody trained.
  Nell read every message twice.
  One afternoon they peeled apples in the galley.
  He broke the ribbon of peel halfway through and looked absurdly disappointed.
  Nell laughed.
  The laugh surprised them both. They did not try to make it mean everything was mended. They finished the apples and carried them to the children.
  On the seventh evening the Ark lowered a smaller vessel toward the lake between the giants' hands.
  Nell went with Abel, Pip, and Sella. The water had cleared. Beneath it the submerged door stood open, its steps leading into darkness.
  Across the lake, Ilyra's stone face caught the last sunlight.
  Pip pointed.
  Between the two immense fingertips, a green shoot had risen from the water. Its leaves rested against both hands. When the wind passed over it, the little stem bowed without breaking.
  "Did he reach her?" Pip asked.
  Nell watched the leaves touch the stone.
  "Something growing between them did."
  They left a basket of bread inside Abel's shelter. In the book beneath the glass, beneath the sentence about the giant still reaching when the stone took him, Nell added another line.
  He kept a place for us while he waited.
  On the receiving table lay a closed folder from the High Liaison Bureau. Its cover reported contact with a living giant beyond Himland's eastern boundary. Nothing inside had been cleared for the passengers.
  Nell left it closed. Micah had saved her a place at supper.
  Back aboard, the lantern stood beside an open lower door. Its flame leaned toward the country behind them.
  Nell looked to Abel.
  "More?"
  He nodded.
  Beyond the threshold, three knocks sounded against the hull.
  She went to answer them.

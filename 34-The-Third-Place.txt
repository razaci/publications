CHAPTER 1
The Third Hand
Mara had cut the bread into three pieces before she remembered there were only two of them.
  She put the smallest piece beside her bowl. Elias pretended not to notice. They had been working since morning, and there was a kindness in allowing a tired person her small mistakes, provided you did not call it kindness afterward.
  Outside the archive bungalow, floodlights washed the nearest pyramid white. Beyond it, the wooden hull of the Ark lay on its cradle, longer than the accommodation buildings and dark except for a line of windows along its upper promenade. Wind moved sand against the kitchen door. The sound reminded Mara of someone brushing crumbs from a table.
  “You have given him three speeches about the window,” she said.
  “He is frightened of what is outside it.”
  “Then let him close it.”
  Elias took the pages from her and searched for the passage. The old novel had arrived in an archive carton that afternoon: a clothbound volume with a blank spine, accompanied by instructions to prepare a spoken adaptation. They were supposed to record the first scene before submitting their report. A woman and a man shared supper inside a wooden vessel. There was a garden somewhere beneath them. Neither character had a name.
  He read the paragraph again. “If he closes the window, we lose the garden sound.”
  “We could hear the garden through the door.”
  “There is no door in this scene.”
  Mara turned her pencil between her fingers. At the edge of the table, a pale crescent marred the varnish. Elias had burned it with a kettle on the first evening they worked together, eight months before. He still placed his cup over it whenever they argued, as though the table needed protecting from further damage.
  “You cannot keep everything,” she said.
  He smiled without looking up. “That should go on your gravestone.”
  “And yours can say he was still revising.”
  For a moment she could see the life they might have somewhere beyond the compound: a different kitchen, his coat over the wrong chair, ordinary quarrels whose outcome did not matter. They had never quite discussed leaving together. Each waited for the other to supply a first sentence that could survive daylight.
  Elias moved his bread onto her plate.
  “You haven't eaten.”
  “I am deciding whether your character deserves to.”
  He reached beneath the table and took her hand.
  The gesture was so familiar that she nearly missed the sentence forming on the page. The paper did not shine. No ink crawled across it. A line simply became readable beneath the paragraph she had crossed out, as though her eyes had finally learned to focus.
  He reaches beneath the table and takes her hand.
  Mara stopped moving her pencil.
  “What?” Elias asked.
  She pointed. He read the line, glanced down at their joined hands, and gave a small embarrassed laugh.
  “You added that.”
  “No.”
  He tried to lift the page. She held its corner because she was afraid of what might be underneath.
  A second direction appeared.
  Neither acknowledges the hand resting on top of theirs.
  Something cool settled across her knuckles.
  There were five separate points of pressure. A narrow palm. A fingertip touching the old nick beside Elias's thumbnail. Mara looked into his face and knew, from his effort to keep looking into hers, that he felt it too.
  For several seconds neither moved.
  A little tapping came from the kitchen. The loose cupboard catch had been bothering them all week. Mara waited for that familiar noise to restore the room to itself.
  The tapping stopped.
  Elias loosened his fingers. Whatever rested over them tightened, very slightly, as a sleeping person might tighten her grip when another person tried to leave the bed.
  “There is someone under the table,” Mara said.
  She disliked herself immediately for saying it. It made the situation sound practical. They could lift the cloth, apologize, ask a person to come out.
  They had no tablecloth.
  The space beneath the table was plainly visible between their chairs. Their knees faced each other. The floor was bare.
  Elias swallowed. “I can feel it.”
  A chair scraped overhead.
  The bungalow had a low flat roof. Mara had climbed onto it herself to clear sand from a drain. Above the kitchen ceiling there was insulation, wiring, and no room in which a person could sit.
  The weight lifted from their hands.
  On the table between the bowls, the smallest piece of bread was gone.
  Mara stood too fast and struck her knee. Elias caught the water glass before it fell. Neither checked the bread basket. Neither looked up.
  From the speaker beside the recorder, in a woman's tired, perfectly ordinary voice, came three words.
  “Leave mine there.”
CHAPTER 2
The Place Between Them
Elias pulled the recorder's power cable out of the wall.
  The little red lamp stayed on. He stared at it until Mara reached past him and opened the battery compartment. Two cells rolled across the counter. The lamp went dark.
  “That doesn't explain the hand,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “I need you to say that before we start explaining the speaker.”
  He set the plug down carefully. “It doesn't explain the hand.”
  They searched the bungalow together. Mara opened cupboards while Elias checked the windows and the small recording booth built into the spare bedroom. Its door stood open. Inside were one stool, a microphone, and a square of gray acoustic foam that had peeled away from the wall. Nobody had hidden there. Nobody could have passed them in the corridor.
  At the kitchen doorway she stopped him with a hand against his chest.
  “Don't go into the roof alone.”
  “I was getting the torch.”
  “Then say you're getting the torch.”
  He nodded. The simple agreement made her feel worse. They had begun making rules for being frightened.
  They went outside and shone the torch across the roof. Nothing moved except the weather flap on the ventilation pipe. The guards' lodge lay beyond a fenced service road, its windows empty. At this hour the small night crew worked in the pyramid's control offices. Elias called the duty desk. A technician answered over the hum of a fan and said the bungalow access log showed no visitors.
  “Could someone have routed audio into our equipment?” Elias asked.
  The technician asked which equipment. Elias gave the serial number, waited, then said he would bring it over in the morning. He did not mention the hand.
  Mara did not blame him. She would have found it easier to report a fire than the temperature of those fingers.
  Inside, they restored the batteries to examine the recording. Their camera had filmed the table for the adaptation notes. Its memory card held forty-seven minutes.
  They watched themselves discussing the window. Mara saw her own face from an angle she disliked, concentrated and ungenerous. She watched herself divide the bread.
  “Stop.”
  Elias paused.
  A third chair stood between theirs.
  It was turned slightly away from the camera, tucked beneath the far edge of the table. Its woven seat sagged around three small hollows. A strip of blue cloth hung from its back.
  Mara looked at the real table. Two chairs. Their own.
  “Play it.”
  On the screen Elias pushed his bread onto Mara's plate. Then he reached beneath the table. His other arm moved as if accommodating somebody seated close beside him.
  The kitchen cupboard tapped. A second later, recorded Mara looked toward the third chair.
  Live Mara leaned forward.
  Her recorded mouth opened, but the camera caught no words. The audio meter still registered sound. It was as though the missing sentence belonged to a frequency the speakers could not carry.
  Then both people on the screen stood. The chair between them slid backward of its own accord.
  Elias stopped the video.
  The scrape they had heard overhead matched the scrape on the recording.
  Mara folded her arms tightly. “I looked at her.”
  “We can't see anyone.”
  “I looked at someone. Watch my eyes.”
  He replayed three seconds. She was right. Her own eyes met a face at seated height. There was nothing random in the movement. She recognized the weary patience in her expression, the look she gave Elias when he had forgotten an obvious thing.
  They returned to the dining table. Elias brought the stool from the recording booth and put it in the empty space. Mara wanted to ask why he was doing that. Instead she fetched a third bowl.
  Her hands shook when she set it down.
  The stool was too low. Anyone sitting on it would barely reach the table. She fetched two folded towels, placed them on the seat, and felt foolish relief at having solved a small problem.
  They sat without touching the manuscript.
  “Are we encouraging something?” Elias asked.
  “We're putting out a bowl.”
  He looked at the empty place, then at her. “All right.”
  Before the recorder's batteries could run down, Mara copied the card onto two drives. On the file listing, the session already had a title. Neither of them had typed one.
  SUPPER THREE.
  Beneath it was another file, dated the following morning. Its length was zero seconds.
  It was called SUPPER TWO.
CHAPTER 3
A Line Without a Speaker
They did not sleep. By dawn the stool had become unbearable to look at, and removing it seemed worse.
  Mara carried the third bowl to the sink. Beneath it lay a damp circle, though she had put nothing in the bowl. There was a grain of soil in the water. She caught it on the tip of her finger before it went down the drain.
  Elias photographed it beside a ruler.
  “Garden soil?” he asked.
  “Dirt. Until we know more.”
  He wrote that down exactly. She loved him a little for accepting the correction without making her defend it.
  At the control office a technician named Fen tested the recorder. Its wireless receiver had been disabled before their assignment. The recorded voice occupied the same track as their conversation. Fen could find no inserted audio, though she warned them that not finding an edit proved little.
  “Who else was reading?” she asked.
  “No one,” Elias said.
  Fen played the voice again. Leave mine there.
  “Sounds close to the microphone.”
  “Yes,” Mara said.
  Fen looked between them, realized there was something neither wanted to say, and offered to retain an untouched copy. Mara accepted. She wanted a witness whose memories had not yet become part of the work.
  They returned to the bungalow with a written equipment report, breakfast in a paper bag, and no explanation.
  While Elias made coffee, Mara examined the manuscript under the kitchen light. The directions about their hands remained. The paper felt ordinary. She held it against the window and saw shallow pressure marks beneath the printed supper scene.
  Someone had erased a paragraph.
  She put a clean sheet over the page and shaded it gently with the side of her pencil. Letter fragments appeared, but so did an ugly tear at the corner. She stopped at once.
  “I'm damaging it.”
  Elias switched off the kettle.
  They took the volume to the conservation desk. The archive custodian, Halden, wore soft sleeves over his wrists and spoke to books with the distracted courtesy other people reserved for animals. He checked the accession number and frowned.
  “This was returned to circulation?”
  “It was in our assignment carton,” Mara said.
  “There should have been a restriction.”
  “What kind?”
  He turned to the terminal. The record had a blank field beside access status. He searched older backups, then brought them a paper return slip. Both researchers had signed it eleven months before.
  Mara studied her signature. It had the cramped final stroke she used when writing on a surface lower than her elbow. Elias's was beneath it. The document listed three contributors. The third space had been filled with a label cut from blank adhesive paper.
  “You worked on the original inventory,” Halden said.
  “We met eight months ago,” Elias replied.
  The custodian took off his glasses. He did not tell them they were mistaken. He asked them to sit.
  Mara remained standing. “Do you remember us here?”
  “Not clearly enough to be useful.”
  He sounded troubled by that admission. He remembered the flood in an upper store, the broken lift, who had borrowed a stapler before he retired for the first time. He should have remembered an entire research team.
  Under raking light, the erased paragraph emerged in parts. Halden made an image without touching the surface. The recovered line began with a speaker designation, but its letters had been scraped too deeply to recover.
  The rest was readable.
  Leave mine there. I am coming up from the garden. If you move the chair I shall have to begin again.
  Beneath that, faint but intact, was a description of a dining room aboard the Ark. It included a table damaged by a hot kettle.
  Mara bent closer.
  The final sentence described the burn as a pale crescent, open toward the window.
  She thought of Elias covering the mark with his cup, and of eight months reduced to something neither could trust.
  “Don't peel that label yet,” she told Halden.
  He withdrew his hand from the third signature.
  “Why?”
  She could not explain it without sounding irrational. Still, the fear was specific enough to deserve words.
  “I want to know whose name it is before we remove anything else.”
CHAPTER 4
The Missing Paragraph
Halden gave them the conservation image and access to the wooden vessel. He also gave them the original return slip in a transparent sleeve.
  “Keep an ordinary record,” he said. “Dates. Meals. Who was present. I would rather have an embarrassing amount of detail than another blank.”
  “Another?” Elias asked.
  The custodian looked at the slip, where three people had become two beneath a little rectangle of paper.
  “I don't know why I said that.”
  On the service path, the Ark rose above them in long dark courses. Its hull retained the proportions used in the complex's architectural documents: three hundred cubits in length, fifty across, thirty high. Their survey standard treated a cubit as eighteen inches. Within that great wooden volume, the builders had divided six working decks around a central opening, with passageways and ramps fitted to the descending hull. The archive called it an inhabited reconstruction. Its timbers were old, but its history belonged to the complex.
  Mara had catalogued those plans for weeks. She knew which ramp ran between the animal quarters and the upper promenade. She could draw the garden stair without checking a reference. Yet the vessel's smell struck her as unfamiliar: dry timber over a deeper scent of water and roots.
  Elias held the access gate while she passed. Their shoulders touched. Each moved away too quickly.
  They climbed to the dining deck. Wooden chairs stood around long communal tables. Along the promenade, benches faced planting troughs where dust had settled on dead stalks. A children's chalk drawing survived beside a serving hatch: a bird with six feet and an enormous beak. Mara recognized its absurd seriousness and almost smiled.
  The dining room in the recovered passage lay farther aft, beyond a small pantry.
  Its table was missing.
  A clean rectangle marked the floor. Four round dents showed where its legs had stood. Against the wall leaned three chairs with woven seats. One wore a strip of faded blue cloth.
  Elias touched the cloth, then snatched his hand back.
  “What?”
  “Wet.”
  Mara felt it. The cloth was dry.
  He rubbed his fingertips together, ashamed of how quickly he had withdrawn. She wanted to tell him it was all right. She was beginning to resent how much of their vocabulary had become reassurance.
  They measured the floor marks. The distance between them matched the bungalow table. On the nearest chair's underside, a repair had been secured with wooden pegs. Mara remembered Elias crouching beside their kitchen chair with a screwdriver, complaining that somebody had used the wrong fitting.
  Had that happened? She could see him doing it. She could not remember when.
  They returned to the bungalow and turned their table on its side.
  Beneath the apron was a shipping label from the Ark stores. It had been covered by a second label identifying bungalow furnishings. The table had not merely resembled the one in the manuscript. It had been brought from the vessel.
  Mara sat on the floor.
  “The kettle,” she said.
  He was kneeling opposite her. “I burned this.”
  “I watched you.”
  “I remember apologizing.”
  “So do I.”
  Their memories agreed. That no longer reassured them.
  Under the table ran a narrow carved line, too shallow to be ornamental. Elias fetched the torch. Three sets of letters lay along it. The first was MARA, the second ELIAS. The third had been gouged away until only the bottom of an upright stroke remained.
  Mara pressed her fingertips into the ruined name.
  A smell of crushed mint rose from the dry wood.
  For an instant she remembered laughing with her head beneath a table while somebody above her said, If you carve yours crooked, you'll have to keep it.
  She pulled away.
  Elias had gone very pale. “Did you hear that?”
  “What did you hear?”
  “A woman laughing.”
  Mara began to cry, abruptly and without knowing whom she missed.
  He reached across the overturned table. She let him take her wrist, but when his fingers approached her palm she recoiled. His face changed before he could hide it.
  “I can't,” she said. “Not yet.”
  Above them, the kitchen cupboard opened.
  A cup slid forward on its shelf and stopped against the lip.
  There were three cups in the set. Mara had always wondered why she could never find the blue one.
CHAPTER 5
The Table in the Ark
Moving the table took both of them and an electric stores cart. They carried it with its damaged underside facing inward, afraid of losing another splinter from the third name.
  Fen met them at the service gate with the equipment report. She looked at the table, then at their faces.
  “Is this part of the sound problem?”
  “Yes,” Mara said.
  Fen waited.
  “We don't know how yet.”
  That was enough for her. She helped them lower the table onto the cart and asked where it belonged. Mara showed her the plan. Fen marked the location in her own notebook. They agreed to check in by radio every hour.
  Inside the Ark, the cart wheels sounded enormous. Animal quarters lay behind timber partitions on the lower occupied deck, stalls empty now except for old bedding caught in corners. Feeding hatches opened toward a broad service passage. Higher up, the ramp crossed beneath walkways where railings enclosed the central promenade. Everything had been built for people to move through while carrying things. Mara found that ordinary provision unexpectedly comforting.
  They set the table over the four marks in the dining room. It settled level without adjustment.
  The chair with the blue cloth fitted between theirs.
  For a while nothing happened.
  Elias opened a window shutter. Light spread across the pale burn. From somewhere below came the drip of water, although the garden services had been shut down months before.
  Mara set the three cups on the table. She had brought the missing blue cup wrapped in a towel, unwilling to leave it in the cupboard overnight. On its handle was a tiny repair she recognized beneath her thumb.
  A woman had once told her how to turn the cup so the crack would not catch her lip. The memory arrived without a face. Mara tried to hold it and felt it dissolve under the effort.
  “Tell me something certain,” she said.
  Elias looked around. “The table fits.”
  “About us.”
  He took longer.
  “When you are very tired, you turn questions into instructions.”
  She gave an unwilling laugh.
  “You asked for something certain.”
  “Something kind.”
  “I like that you assume we can do something, even when you are frightened.”
  She looked down at the blue cup. “I don't know if that is always kind.”
  “Neither do I.”
  They ate the breakfast they had forgotten. Elias left a third portion in its wrapping. It was going stale. Mara nearly put it away, then remembered the voice.
  On the table lay their adaptation. She read the recovered paragraph silently. Its description had become more detailed since the conservation image: sunlight reached the woman's left sleeve; the man had cut his finger carrying the table; a blue cup stood at the third place.
  Elias looked at his thumb. A splinter had drawn blood near the nail.
  “We should stop touching the pages,” he said.
  “And then?”
  He sat beside her. They listened to the dripping.
  Under her chair, something brushed her ankle. Mara froze. A small piece of blue thread lay across her shoe. It matched the strip tied to the empty chair, though that strip remained several feet away.
  She picked it up.
  This time the memory stayed long enough to hurt.
  A woman was tying cloth around Mara's wrist in a garden. Her hands were brown with soil, her right forefinger bound in a clean dressing. She said, If the alarm goes, find me before you find the stairs. I know which doors still open.
  Mara remembered answering. She could feel the shape of the promise in her throat.
  Then the woman's face was gone, leaving only the dressing and the blue cloth.
  Elias had been speaking to her.
  “What?” she asked.
  “I said your name. Three times.”
  She wound the thread around her finger.
  Below the dining room, through timber thick enough to muffle machinery, a woman began coughing.
  It was an ugly human cough, the kind that exhausted someone before she could draw another breath.
  Mara stood.
  “Radio Fen,” she said. “Tell her we're going down to the garden.”
CHAPTER 6
What She Remembers
The garden stair ended at a locked gate.
  Behind it, darkness lay over planting beds and a narrow stone path. Elias shone his torch through the bars. The beam caught a wheelbarrow, a stack of shallow trays, and the white underside of a fallen leaf. No person stood where the coughing had seemed to come from.
  Mara pressed the radio button. Fen answered immediately.
  “The garden access is closed,” Mara told her. “Can you check the lock authorization?”
  A keyboard clicked. “That part of the vessel was isolated after the lower water leak. The public stair is blocked. There's another entrance from the pyramid service lift, level twenty-one.”
  The pyramid complex descended through twenty-four occupied levels. The Ark sat outside it, connected by an enclosed service passage and a series of deep utility galleries. Mara had drawn their connections often enough. Seeing the route on her hand-held plan, she found a room beside the garden labeled CONTROL ANNEX. Someone had written over the label in pencil.
  WAIT HERE IF YOU LOSE US.
  The handwriting was hers.
  She showed Elias. He photographed it before either of them could touch the page.
  “We take it to Halden,” he said.
  “The coughing stopped.”
  “I noticed.”
  “If somebody is there—”
  “We tell Fen. We get access. We don't break a locked gate and lose our way.”
  His restraint irritated her because it was sensible. She had wanted him to agree quickly enough that she would not have to think about the woman struggling for breath.
  As they turned back, Mara heard a thin scraping behind the gate. She swept her torch over the beds. A folded piece of cloth had appeared on the stone path.
  Blue.
  Elias saw it too. He described its position into the radio. Fen asked them to stay together and wait while she checked the service entrance.
  They sat on the bottom step. Their shoulders touched again, this time without either withdrawing.
  Mara told him about the promise in the garden.
  He took out Halden's notebook. “What else do you remember?”
  “She had a dressing on one finger. She was annoyed with me.”
  “Because?”
  “I was smiling while she gave the instructions. I thought she was making a fuss.”
  He wrote slowly, leaving space between statements. She could see him refusing to improve the account into something more orderly.
  “And she didn't like sweet tea,” Mara said. “She made it, but she didn't drink it. She said it was a waste of a leaf.”
  The memory surprised a laugh out of her. Then she began shaking.
  “What if I made her up?”
  Elias kept the notebook open. “Then we'll find out.”
  “What if we're making her together?”
  “I don't know.”
  He offered her the pencil. She took it, crossed out the word knew beside the woman, and substituted remember. They were different claims.
  Fen called. The service entrance was locked from within. She could find no current heat signature in the rooms accessible to the sensor array. That did not include the enclosed garden itself.
  “I've requested a safety opening for morning,” she said. “No one is going in alone tonight.”
  Mara agreed. Before climbing the stair, she looked through the gate once more.
  There were now two lengths of blue cloth on the path.
  One was folded. The other lay in a loose loop, no larger than a wrist.
  Back at the bungalow, she washed her hands and found soil beneath the nail of her right forefinger. The finger had begun to ache. When she bent it, the skin resisted as if a dressing were wrapped tightly around the joint.
  Elias was waiting outside the bathroom.
  “You said something on the stairs.”
  “What?”
  “You said, 'I told you I'd come up when I'd finished.' You sounded angry with me.”
  “I don't remember.”
  “I wrote it down.”
  She let him see her finger. Nothing marked the skin.
  That night, seated far from the table they had moved back into the Ark, Mara dreamed without sleeping. She watched her own hands lift seedlings into trays while rain struck a roof above her. The garden had no open roof. There had been no rain in the compound for months.
  Someone was calling her to supper.
  She wanted to answer, but she could not remember which name belonged to her.
CHAPTER 7
The First Rehearsal
Halden arrived at seven carrying the first aid kit he used for paper cuts and a thermos large enough for four people.
  Mara had called him before dawn. She had not known how to describe the night except to read from Elias's notebook. The custodian listened without interruption. Now he asked her to state her name, where she was, and whether she wanted medical help.
  She answered and said she wanted a doctor to check the finger. A visiting medic found no swelling or injury, documented the sensation, and told her to return if it worsened. The normal examination steadied her. Her fear could coexist with ordinary care.
  The garden opening had been postponed. Water had flooded the lower service vestibule during the night. Fen was isolating the line.
  They gathered in the Ark dining room instead. Halden put his thermos near the blue cup and laid out the conservation image.
  “We have two descriptions of this room,” he said. “An older paragraph and one that changes. We can compare them without reading either aloud.”
  Mara looked toward the garden stair.
  “The voice spoke after we read the scene at the bungalow.”
  “And the first time,” Elias said, “before we knew we were reading instructions.”
  “So you want to repeat it?” Halden asked.
  “Four lines,” Mara said. “With an observer. No deletions.”
  They set limits. Halden would stop them if either failed to respond to her own name. Fen would monitor by radio. The outer doors would remain open. Nobody would sit in the third chair.
  Elias read the man's first line.
  “Will you close the window?”
  Mara answered as the woman.
  “If I close it, we won't hear her coming.”
  Below them, a foot touched a stair.
  Halden raised one hand. They listened. No second step followed.
  Mara could feel Elias looking at her. She nodded, and he read the next line.
  “She knows the way.”
  A second footstep.
  Mara forced her eyes to stay on the paper.
  “Knowing the way isn't the same as being expected.”
  Three steps sounded quickly, followed by a breath close to the floor outside the dining room.
  Halden said, “Stop.”
  They stopped.
  The breath stopped too.
  Mara listened until her own lungs began to hurt. At last she inhaled, furious with herself. A shadow lay beneath the closed pantry door. It was narrow at the top, wider below, as if someone stood facing them with a long skirt brushing her ankles.
  That door had been open when they entered.
  Elias rose. The shadow shifted toward his movement.
  “Stay there,” Halden said, and Elias obeyed.
  Fen's voice came over the radio. “There are four of you now.”
  Mara pressed the talk button. “Three.”
  “I'm counting four breathing patterns on the room microphone.”
  Halden looked at the microphone, then at Mara. He kept his raised hand steady.
  “Describe the fourth,” he said.
  “It stops whenever the woman stops reading. Is someone holding their breath?”
  Mara stood. Her chair legs grated across the floor. Beneath that sound she heard a single relieved inhalation from the pantry.
  The door handle turned a fraction.
  Mara almost read the next line. It was only six words: We saved a place for you.
  Elias caught the page before she lifted it.
  “We said four lines.”
  “She can't breathe.”
  “We don't know that.”
  They stared at one another, his fingers on the paper, hers white around the pencil. From the other side of the door came a little knock.
  Three times. Patiently.
  Then a woman's voice said, “You always stop there.”
  Mara sat down.
  The shadow faded. The pantry door swung inward, revealing shelves, folded linen, and no passage behind them.
  Halden checked the original floor plan. Beside the pantry, where a heavy timber wall now stood, the older drawing showed a doorway.
  Elias read its penciled designation under his breath, then looked stricken at having given it a voice.
  Garden ascent. Private.
  In the blue cup, a little steam rose from tea no one had poured.
CHAPTER 8
The Removed Door
They waited until the tea cooled before taking the cup away from the table. Fen collected a small sample in a clean vial. It smelled of mint and tasted, she later reported, like ordinary weak tea. Mara did not ask why she had tasted it. She was beginning to understand the desire to make an impossible thing answer a simple question.
  The water in the service vestibule had stopped rising. Maintenance needed time to dry the electrical enclosure. The garden remained out of reach.
  Halden returned with three versions of the vessel's plan. The oldest showed the private ascent; the next labeled it redundant; the newest replaced it with solid wall. A revision note named Elias as the person who requested the change.
  He read the note twice.
  “I don't make architectural changes.”
  “You requested a change to the record,” Halden said. “Not necessarily the wall.”
  The distinction settled over them unpleasantly.
  They moved the pantry shelves. Behind them, boards had been fitted across an opening. The grain ran against the surrounding wall. Mara had passed those boards for weeks without noticing how readily they could have been removed.
  The fastenings were wooden wedges. Elias tapped one loose with the handle of a stores mallet. There was nothing violent in the work, yet every small knock seemed an announcement to somebody waiting on the other side.
  When the final board came away, they found a door.
  It had no handle on their side.
  A thin line of warmer air passed around its frame. The air smelled of onions cooked slowly in a pan, the same supper they had eaten at the bungalow. Mara put her palm against the wood.
  From beyond came her own voice.
  “You have given him three speeches about the window.”
  She stumbled backward into Elias.
  Their opening-night argument continued beyond the door, muffled but unmistakable. Elias heard his own explanation about the garden sound. The sentences came with the little hesitations the manuscript had not recorded.
  Halden set a recorder on the floor. He wrote the time on a sheet and held it in front of its camera. His hand shook so badly that Mara steadied the paper for him.
  The voices reached the moment when Mara said you could not keep everything.
  Someone else laughed.
  It was not a sinister laugh. The woman sounded tired and amused by people she knew well.
  “If you're going to cut things,” she said, “start with his speeches. Leave my door alone.”
  Silence followed on their side of the wall.
  Beyond it, the other Mara replied. “It's only an adaptation.”
  Elias closed his eyes.
  They had never heard that exchange. It did not exist on the camera card from the bungalow. Yet Mara could picture herself saying it with a mouthful of bread, impatient to finish a day's work she considered harmless.
  The sound continued. A cup touched the table. There was a soft dragging noise, then the third woman said, “Whose copy is this?”
  Recorded Elias answered too quickly. “The working copy.”
  “No. The ending. Who wrote that?”
  A scrape. Paper turning.
  The woman said, very quietly, “You promised to show me all of it.”

  “We don't get that far,” she said.
CHAPTER 17
The Price of Restoring Her
They could not keep the door open indefinitely. When Iona was called back into the supper, the frame grew cold and Mara's shoulder began to go numb. She stepped away only after promising to return.
  The door closed softly. No one heard a latch.
  The medic waited in the dining room with blankets and a portable monitor. He had seen Iona from the passage but had been unable to approach before the door closed. He asked about food, water, and any injuries they had observed. Mara answered as accurately as she could. She was grateful that he spoke of a patient rather than a phenomenon.
  Halden spread the records over the benches. Fen had kept copies outside the vessel and outside the preservation network. Her own notes remained unchanged. Inside the Ark, the script had begun recovering names and missing lines wherever Iona had spoken during the open door.
  “The changes seem limited,” Fen said. “Materials brought into the original recording. People who took part. The furniture and the rooms connected to it.”
  “Limited enough to take a year,” Mara said.
  Fen did not argue.
  Elias had found a technical report describing the receptive panels. The engineers did not understand their origin. They had measured repeated speech, retained sensory impressions, and the way a complete account seemed to release a room from repetition. The authors warned that a stable retained scene should never be mistaken for a safe inhabited space.
  The alternate closure procedure had been experimental.
  “So there wasn't a guarantee,” Elias said.
  “No,” Halden answered.
  “Just a page that sounded certain.”
  He put it down.
  Restoring the omitted passages had restored other memories too. Mara recalled a winter evening she had cherished as the first time Elias stayed late to help her. Iona had been there. The bowl of soup Mara remembered him bringing had been Iona's, carried upstairs after she noticed Mara hadn't eaten.
  Elias remembered a conversation in the garden he had treasured for its privacy. Iona had been working behind the screen, plainly visible. She had excused herself so they could be alone. The memory had later removed the courtesy along with the person who offered it.
  They sat with those alterations for a long time.
  “Were any of our beginnings ours?” Mara asked.
  Elias looked at the blue cup.
  “I don't think being helped makes them less ours.”
  “But I made her disappear from them.”
  “Yes.”
  He let that answer stand. She trusted him more for it than for anything he had said about their future.
  The recovered script now included a passage in Iona's hand, written before the recording. It described the garden as a place built by many people whose names were missing from the official architectural account. She wanted the project to preserve their work. Their muddy shoes, their failed repairs, the jokes told while carrying water.
  Her wish had become the reason she could not leave.
  Closing the retained scene without a fixed keeper would destroy its permanence. The panels would cease holding the inhabited garden. Some of the preserved voices had no other surviving recording. Iona's return might cost the work she had stayed to protect.
  They took that information to the door.
  It opened only a hand's width. Iona listened from the dark garden.
  “Can we copy it first?” she asked.
  “We've tried copying the audio,” Fen said from beside Mara. “The copies hold what the microphones can capture. They don't hold the room.”
  “Then keep those.”
  “There may be parts we lose.”
  Iona looked beyond them toward the table. Her eyes filled, but she did not ask anyone to comfort her.
  “I planted things because I expected them to grow somewhere I could see them,” she said. “I didn't agree to stay here forever.”
  Mara nodded.
  For the first time she understood that saving Iona would not mean restoring everything they had lost. They would have to let a place end. They would have to allow her to leave it.
  “Tell me the plan before you try it,” Iona said.
  “We will.”
  “All of it.”
  Mara looked at Elias. He took the hidden ending from his pocket and handed it through the gap.
  Iona read it in silence. When she finished, she folded it once, with great care.
  Then she gave it back.
CHAPTER 18
The Last Revision
The hidden ending was less than a page long.
  At supper the keeper relinquishes the third place. A witness remains to preserve the garden. The departing pair acknowledge completion.
  There were blanks for names. Someone could replace Iona. A fresh pair could leave while another person remained in the record. The procedure made that exchange sound like an ordinary correction to the cast.
  Elias began to say something.
  Mara heard the first breath and knew what it would become.
  “No.”
  He looked at her.
  “You are thinking about staying.”
  He did not deny it.
  “I don't get to call it right because it would hurt me,” he said after a while.
  She sat beside him on the bench.
  “No. And I don't get to agree because I'm frightened of losing her.”
  They let the proposal pass without making it noble. Through the window, daylight moved across the table. Its old burn had a sharper edge now, as if it had been made recently.
  Halden brought the oldest pages from the project box. The first closure sheet, marked incomplete, had never been designed as a dramatic ending. Its final space was blank because each participant was meant to speak without being assigned a line.
  Below the space were three columns: speaker, response, acknowledgment.
  Elias had mistaken the blank for missing text. Mara had approved a finished scene because a page filled with instructions looked more reliable than one waiting for a person to answer.
  Halden traced the sequence without touching it.
  “The early tests ended when every participant could correct the record. The fixed version ended when the required lines had been spoken.”
  “Even if the lines were false,” Mara said.
  “Apparently.”
  Fen stood near the pantry, listening for Iona. “Can you reopen the old sequence?”
  “We can offer it,” Mara answered. “I don't know whether the room will accept it.”
  They tested a harmless correction first. The changing script described the blue cup as belonging to Elias. Mara set it at the third place and said, aloud, that it belonged to Iona Reed. Elias acknowledged hearing the correction.
  Nothing happened.
  Iona's voice came from the closed door.
  “I lent it to him.”
  Mara turned. “Then I was wrong.”
  On the page, the sentence altered. The cup rested before Elias, its loan acknowledged in a smaller line beneath the description.
  Mara sat down slowly.
  The room had accepted Iona's correction. It had needed hers.
  They tried a second correction. Elias said he had brought the table upstairs. Iona told him she had helped, carrying one end while he complained about the stairs. He accepted the correction. The shipping notation on the underside gained a second initial in a space that had been blank.
  The change was small enough to be convincing. Nothing announced that the haunting had been solved. Iona remained on the other side of the door. The unspoken exit still waited.
  They worked until evening, assembling an honest account instead of a script that instructed anyone how to feel. They included what each person remembered, what the documents supported, and what remained uncertain. Iona dictated her corrections from the threshold. Mara wrote them. Elias read them back.
  When they reached the supper, Mara's hand began to shake.
  “I told you it was only an adaptation.”
  “Yes,” Iona said.
  “I said we could explain afterward.”
  “Yes.”
  “I didn't ask whether you wanted to stay.”
  “No.”
  Mara wrote the answer.
  There was no change in the room. No release arrived as a reward for admitting the truth. They still had work to do.
  At the bottom of the account, she left three empty spaces.
  Halden asked what title to put on the cover.
  Mara looked at Iona.
  “The Garden Requires Three?” she asked.
  Iona shook her head.
  “It doesn't require any of us to stay.”
  For the first time since the door opened, she smiled a little.
  “Just call it Supper.”
CHAPTER 19
Setting Three Places
They chose the dining room in the Ark because it held the table's original marks, the private ascent, and the open window through which the garden could be heard. Everything they could control would be familiar. Everything they could not would be written down.
  Fen stationed the medic and another worker in the passage. She would observe from outside the recorded group. Halden held the separate copy of their account. They had no wish to turn helpers into new participants by handing them roles in the scene.
  Mara made the same supper. Onions, greens, bread. She found herself slowing at each ordinary task, afraid the pan would boil dry while she was thinking about the next hour. Elias asked whether he could help. She gave him the knife and told him exactly what she wanted.
  Neither treated the exchange as a lesson. They needed to eat.
  The smell reached the pantry before the food was ready.
  Iona opened the door.
  She stood in the other kitchen, holding the bread from the first night. It had become hard and gray at the edges. Her eyes followed the steam rising from Mara's pan.
  “That's a cruel smell,” she said.
  Mara set down the spoon. “I'm sorry.”
  “Don't put it away. I want it.”
  They waited while Elias moved the third chair into place. He tied the blue cloth to its back where it had been in the camera footage. He set the repaired cup at Iona's place and left its handle turned safely away from the crack.
  Iona watched him do it.
  “You remembered.”
  “Some of it.”
  She nodded, accepting the limit.
  Mara cut the bread into three pieces.
  For a moment her hand remembered doing that in the bungalow without knowing why. The smallest piece belonged to the person who always came late and insisted she was not hungry before eating what was left in the pantry.
  She made the pieces equal.
  They placed the old script beside the new account. Nothing had been burned or torn out. The false ending would remain part of what they admitted doing. It would not determine what Iona had to say.
  Halden checked their names aloud. Mara answered. Elias answered. From the threshold, Iona answered too.
  The dining room dimmed.
  Beyond the window, evening and floodlit night occupied the same panes. Mara could see the pyramid, and over it the faint reflection of the bungalow cupboard. The Ark wall gave way to kitchen plaster, then returned when she shifted her gaze.
  Elias sat opposite her.
  Iona's chair remained empty.
  “Can you come to it?” Mara asked.
  Iona tried. Her body shivered at the threshold. She stopped before the effort became a fall.
  “The first part,” she said. “I think you have to get to the first part.”
  Mara looked at Elias. They had agreed they would stop if Iona asked, even if stopping meant another attempt later. He gave a small nod.
  She lifted the old script.
  “You have given him three speeches about the window.”
  The room settled with a sound like a deep wooden hull taking weight.
  “He is frightened of what is outside it,” Elias answered.
  “Then let him close it.”
  Each line drew Iona farther into view. Her shadow reached across the floor to the third chair. She took one step, then another, her face fixed with effort.
  Mara continued until the argument reached the garden sound. The conversation had once felt like theirs alone. Now she heard all the space they had allowed around each sentence without inviting the person standing in it to speak.
  “There is no door in this scene,” Elias said.
  Mara waited.
  Iona was beside the chair.
  “Yes, there is,” she said.
  The pantry door swung wider.
  Mara looked directly at her. “You're right. We took it out.”
  A long seam opened in the wall panel beneath the window. No timber fell. Fen moved back in the passage, keeping the path clear.
  Iona lowered herself onto the chair.
  The seat creaked under her weight.
  Elias put bread on Mara's plate. He reached beneath the table and took her hand. She felt his fingers tremble.
  Iona laid her hand over theirs.
  It was cool, narrow, and unmistakably human.
  On the old page appeared the sentence that had frightened them at the beginning.
  Neither acknowledges the hand resting on top of theirs.
  Mara did not look away from Iona's face.
  “I feel your hand,” she said.
CHAPTER 20
The Third Hand
For Iona, the first evening had never quite ended.
  There had been sleep, or something close to it. There had been long intervals in the garden when she could count trays and mend a shoe and believe that the passage upstairs would open if she waited for the right moment. But the pan always began to smell of onions again. Mara always cut the bread. Elias always hid the page.
  At first Iona had shouted her own name.
  Later she had tried useful things. She straightened a cup. She tapped the cupboard catch because Mara always noticed an unfinished repair. She moved a chair because Elias complained when furniture obstructed a camera angle. She tried to become the kind of small inconvenience they could not ignore.
  Once, leaning over the table, she had seen the words change beneath Mara's pencil. The manuscript could describe what they would not look at. Iona had pressed her hand over theirs and put all the strength she could gather into remaining there.
  The pressure had frightened them. They stood. She took the bread because it was the only thing they might miss.
  Leave mine there, she said, and heard herself come through the little speaker.
  Then the kitchen went dark.
  She remembered that success through the nights that followed. Somewhere outside, someone had heard a whole sentence. She practiced other sentences while tending plants that lived and died in uncertain seasons. She did not know which words would reach the room next.
  Now Mara was looking at her.
  “I feel your hand.”
  The words were simple. Iona waited for them to vanish beneath the familiar argument.
  They remained.
  Elias turned his hand beneath hers without pulling away.
  “I feel it too,” he said. “Iona Reed is sitting with us.”
  The kitchen and the Ark dining room stood around the table together. In one window it was night. In the other, the last daylight rested on wooden railings. Iona could see the empty stool from the bungalow, towels stacked on its seat, and beneath it the old garden path.
  She had spent so long trying to make herself visible that being seen felt dangerous. She wanted to hide her damaged finger. She wanted to apologize for the dirt on her sleeves.
  Mara kept her eyes on her.
  “You came up from the garden,” she said. “We didn't let you finish.”
  Iona nodded.
  Elias read from the new account. His voice shook over his description of hiding the warning. He did not call it protection. Mara read her own approval of the cuts. She did not say she had been deceived without also saying what she had known.
  When they finished, Iona corrected them.
  “I didn't agree to the recording because I wanted the garden to last forever,” she said. “I wanted people to know we'd been there.”
  Mara wrote it down.
  “And I was angry before I was frightened.”
  Elias acknowledged that too.
  The old script's final instructions darkened on the page. The retained participant must not contest closure.
  Iona could feel the room waiting for her to become quiet. It had always become easier when she stopped struggling. The garden returned then. She could sit by the pump and pretend the supper was late rather than unfinished.
  She looked at the three blank cards.
  “What happens to the garden?”
  “The physical beds stay,” Mara said. “We will lose the room's preserved version. We have recordings and your notes. We can't promise more than that.”
  “My trees?”
  “Some died. We haven't checked every tray.”
  Iona closed her eyes. She had wanted a kinder answer, though she knew what plants did when nobody could reach them.
  “Don't tell me you can bring them all back.”
  “I won't.”
  She opened her eyes again. Elias was still holding her hand. He had begun crying quietly, and she discovered she could look at him without either forgiving him or turning away.
  “I don't know what I feel about you yet,” she told him.
  “I know.”
  “No. You don't. Neither do I.”
  He nodded.
  It was the first answer he had given her that left her room to change her mind.
  Mara placed Iona's card before her. “You can say whatever you need to say. Or we can wait.”
  Iona listened to the garden through the open window. Water moved somewhere far below. The sound was imperfect now, thinning into a drip, no longer the complete living place the panels had held. She could remember other people working there, but she did not hear their voices. She would have to carry them in the ordinary way.
  She took her hand from theirs.
  Nothing forced it back.
  “I am leaving the room,” she said. “I want to go outside. I want someone to come with me.”
  Mara answered first.
  “I heard you. I'll come.”
  Elias repeated her choice without changing it.
  “Iona Reed is leaving the room. She has asked us to go with her.”
  Mara made her own statement. She was leaving, she said, and she would not certify that the account was complete without Iona's answer. Elias said he was leaving too, and that nobody would remain in his place.
  Each waited until the other two had answered.
  The sound of the alarm began beneath the floor.
  Iona's chair shifted. For one terrible second she thought they would stand without her again. She reached for the table's edge.
  Mara stayed seated.
  “We're here.”
  The window went dark. A thin crack ran through the receptive panel by the pantry. The letters on the old script lost their sharpness, as if water had passed across ink. Iona felt the familiar pull toward the stairs.
  She put both feet flat on the floor.
  The seat held.
  Outside the passage, Fen said their names. One after another, carefully. Halden answered from farther away. The ordinary voices gave the dining room depth. Iona could hear where people stood and how far she would have to walk to reach them.
  Mara rose slowly, still facing her.
  “May I help you up?”
  Iona nodded.
  Elias moved the chair back. It made the scrape they had heard overhead on the first night, but this time its source was plain. Iona stood between them. Her legs weakened almost immediately.
  They caught her before she fell.
  There was no kitchen beyond the pantry door now. Only shelves and the private stair, descending into the Ark's garden. The alarm stopped. Warm food remained on the table.
  Iona looked down at her hands. They were empty.
  “Where's the bread?” she asked.
  Mara took a fresh piece from the plate and gave it to her.
  The medic met them at the passage. Iona flinched at the bright light on his monitor. He lowered it, introduced himself, and asked before touching her wrist. Mara stayed where she could see her. Elias followed with the blue cup wrapped in a clean towel.
  At the service gate, Iona asked them to stop.
  Night air moved across the open deck. Beyond the Ark, the pyramids rose above their work lights. A maintenance cart crossed the road. Someone called to its driver to slow down. Iona watched the cart continue past the junction, disappear behind a shed, and remain gone.
  She began to cry.
  Mara held her while the medic waited.
  They could not recover the year in a single night. In the weeks that followed, Iona slept badly, tired quickly, and sometimes reached for a door before asking whether she was permitted to open it. Her memories arrived unevenly. So did theirs. Some days she wanted Mara beside her. On others, Fen took her to the garden and asked no questions.
  The preserved scene did not return. Halden retained the old script, the changing pages now still, with the independent recordings and the names of everyone involved. He refused suggestions to describe the event as a successful test.
  They repaired the water line before reopening the beds. Most of the mature plants were dead. In a sheltered tray beside the annex, several roots had survived. Iona made them wait while she examined each one. When Elias offered to replace the broken watering can, she asked him to repair its handle instead.
  He did, and showed her where the repair might fail.
  Mara and Elias did not leave together as they had once imagined. They stayed through the repairs and the uncomfortable meetings and Iona's slow recovery. Their affection survived without settling every question. When Mara wanted him to stay one evening, she asked. When he had something difficult to tell her, he told her before deciding what she could bear.
  The dining table remained aboard the Ark. Nobody sanded away the burn or filled the gouge beneath it. Iona did not want her name recarved over the damage. She put a new label beside it, in her own handwriting, where anyone bending to examine the table could read who had been missing.
  One evening, much later, Mara cooked onions and greens while Elias opened the window. There was work below in the garden. Water ran through a cleared channel, and someone complained about an incorrectly numbered tray.
  Mara smiled before she could stop herself.
  She cut the bread into three pieces.
  Elias reached beneath the table and took her hand. For a moment they both listened, remembering how fear had once made them keep their eyes on one another rather than turn toward the empty place.
  Footsteps came up the private stair.
  “Leave mine there,” Iona called.
  “We have,” Mara answered.
  Iona entered with soil on her sleeve. She pulled out her own chair, sat down, and began telling them what had happened in the garden.
  They let her finish.

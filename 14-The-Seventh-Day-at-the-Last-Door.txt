Chapter 1
The House with the Open Curtains
Mara washed the kitchen window before she let Daniel come home. There was nothing wrong with the glass. She wanted him to see the garden from his chair, and she wanted to do one thing whose outcome did not depend on a receiver.
  June put the birthday crown in a drawer. Then she took it out again and placed it beside the salt. They had been together for nineteen days. Nobody had learned how to stop checking whether the others were still in the room.
  Daniel's injured arm rested in a sling. When he tried to open a jar, Mara reached for it too quickly. He withdrew his hand.
  They stood there until June said, "I can open a jar without either of you losing a war."
  For a moment they laughed like the family they had been before breakfast disappeared.
  Outside, Alder Crossing was filling slowly. Some residents had returned from the ancient valley. Others remained on Elian's lists. The grocer reopened with handwritten prices. A child left her shoes by the bed in case the next displacement came at night.
  That evening, a national broadcast announced a coordinated pause in gateway activity. Sunday would become a common day of recovery. Workers would be paid to stay home; receiving centers would rest their equipment; families would gather to remember the missing.
  Daniel listened to the promise of a day without alarms.
  "I'd take that," he said.
  Mara would have taken a day without fear too. She wanted the proposal to be good. Wanting it embarrassed her less than the suspicion she could not put down.
  At Meridian, Elian received a new image from the river camp. Ada had written six words on a piece of packaging.
  We are still here. Please answer.
  Behind her, on the far bank, Rook's capsule had acquired a flag.
Chapter 2
A Day Everybody Needed
The first Sunday gathering took place in the school gym. Volunteers brought bread and photographs. The basketball hoops had been raised, leaving pale rectangles on the walls where generations of children had aimed.
  Pastor Ruth Mercer read the names of the missing without asking what church they belonged to. A Muslim family stood beside an atheist mechanic. Miriam's photograph occupied a chair because Elian had no address to which she could send it.
  Mara brought Daniel in a borrowed wheelchair. He disliked the chair until he saw another man in one and exchanged the weary nod of people tired of being congratulated for arriving.
  Ruth spoke about rest as something nobody should have to earn through usefulness. Mara found herself crying. The sermon had given her permission to feel tired without making fatigue another failure.
  Then Ruth introduced the regional recovery commissioner, Adrian Kest.
  He had lost his wife in the March crisis. He said so without displaying her photograph. He promised that the new authority would repair Rook's damage, open its accounts, and support the exhausted receiving teams.
  The proposed act would last ninety days. It required no religious declaration. Its Sunday schedule followed existing staffing patterns and public custom.
  People applauded. Mara did too.
  At the exit, volunteers distributed registration cards. June asked why an event about rest needed a household list.
  "So nobody gets overlooked," a woman answered.
  June remembered lists that had overlooked people deliberately. She kept the card without filling it in.
  After the chairs were stacked, Ruth found Kest reviewing attendance figures with an aide.
  "We had a good turnout," she said.
  "We need a complete one."
  He smiled before she could decide whether he meant next week's memorial or the whole country.
Chapter 3
The Exception in Small Print
Hale read the act on a screen at two in the morning. Elian had gone home, then returned because sleeping beside an unanswered signal felt like abandoning somebody at a locked door.
  The legislation funded relief, capped compulsory shifts, and provided a shared maintenance window. Those were useful measures. Its emergency appendix allowed the authority to define activities essential to temporal continuity.
  "That could mean anything they connect to a receiver," Hale said.
  "It could also keep hospitals running."
  He nodded. They had learned to distinguish a dangerous power from every use of it.
  Elian submitted technical testimony. Receivers required rest according to heat, load, and alignment. A weekday chosen for civil convenience could organize maintenance, but the machinery did not recognize a holy day. Independent inspection mattered more than synchronization.
  Kest's office thanked her. Her comments appeared in the public record under support for coordinated shutdown.
  The qualification had been removed from the summary.
  Elian called the office and received an apology. The summary remained online through the evening news cycle. By morning, three commentators were citing her as proof that the law would stabilize time.
  Meanwhile, the new permit office asked Meridian to renew its receiving license. Approval required participation in the national schedule and submission of passenger identities.
  Hale placed the forms beside Ada's roster. Thirty-seven people at the river camp were confirmed alive. Three needed urgent care. None could wait for the completion of a civil appeal.
  "We can sign the maintenance agreement," he said. "We can't sign away their permission."
  Elian wrote two separate replies. One offered access to the equipment records. The other refused blanket access to the people.
  The office acknowledged only the first.
Chapter 4
Across the River
Ada had made the camp's school from a freight container. Children scratched sums on the inside of its doors. When rain came, everyone crowded beneath the same roof, and arithmetic gave way to arguments about elbows.
  Nobody knew the settlement's year. Watches disagreed. The stars resembled diagrams from several eras without matching any of them closely enough. Their own days followed the river's morning mist and the evening cooling of the stones.
  Rook arrived with a functioning battery and a promise to determine the way home.
  He had survived Shepherd's fall in a capsule designed for directors, not workers. Ada recognized the polished fastening on his coat before she recognized his face. She did not welcome him. She did not prevent the camp medic from treating his burns either.
  He offered the battery in exchange for a place in the radio hut.
  "Lend it," Ada said. "We record what it powers."
  He laughed as though she had made an affectionate joke.
  Miriam stood beside the doorway. Elian knew her only from the drowned street and the camp image, but here she had become the woman who remembered which children woke frightened and which adults concealed fever to keep working.
  She took the battery. Ada wrote its serial number.
  That night a transmission arrived from Kest's authority. Sunday service would resume access to authorized recovery channels. The camp should nominate a representative.
  Rook volunteered before anyone asked.
  Ada requested the complete message. Its attached clock put Sunday thirty-one hours away.
  Her own notebook put their next rest day tomorrow.
  For the first time, the distant authorities had told them what day they were living in.
Chapter 5
Bread at the Counter
Renn bought groceries for Meridian because he could not reach Ada and had to carry something somewhere. He chose apples she would have disliked and put them back. Then he chose dried fruit that would survive a journey.
  At the counter, the clerk scanned his recovery card. A notice asked him to confirm the household's Sunday registration. He declined because he did not live with a household and the receiving center was not his home.
  The transaction paused.
  "It's just the subsidy," the clerk said. "You can pay full price."
  Renn could. The woman behind him could not. She had two children and a bag containing oats, milk, and potatoes. Her congregation met on Saturday. The registration form offered only a Sunday assembly location.
  The clerk called a supervisor. He advised her to choose a location now and explain later. She asked whether choosing meant agreeing to attend.
  He showed her the declaration. Participation was a condition of recovery assistance.
  Renn paid for her groceries. The terminal flagged his second purchase for diversion review.
  Outside, she tried to give him the potatoes. He said the camp had enough. It was untrue, and she heard it.
  "What's your name?" he asked instead.
  "Esther."
  He wrote it on the receipt so the event would remain a woman's difficulty rather than become a rumor about policy.
  At Meridian, Esme inspected the transaction code. It belonged to a licensing system licensed from Shepherd's surviving creditors.
  The authority had changed its emblem. It had kept the part that could stop a purchase while the food sat within reach.
Chapter 6
The Woman Who Kept Saturday
Esther repaired coats in a room behind the bus station. On Saturdays she closed the door and left a handwritten notice offering emergency repairs without payment if somebody had nowhere else to go.
  Mara visited with Daniel's torn sling. Esther repaired it while explaining her Sabbath practice. She was a Seventh-day Adventist. Rest, for her, included worship, family, and helping people; she did not think a hungry child should wait for sunset.
  Mara admitted that she had imagined otherwise.
  "People imagine things about each other when they don't have to share a table," Esther said.
  They shared one over tea.
  The following day, the recovery office ordered Esther to join a Sunday rota. Her Saturday closure would be treated as unauthorized withdrawal from essential service if she also declined the national assembly.
  She proposed working a longer weekday shift. The supervisor said the issue was civic participation, not total hours.
  Mara heard the distinction immediately. Equipment maintenance had become a question about the person.
  Ruth came to the workshop after learning about the order. She worshipped on Sunday and had supported the law's relief funding. She read the letter twice.
  "They used our gathering as the measurement," she said.
  Esther asked whether Ruth would testify to that. Ruth looked at the children doing homework beside the sewing machine and said she would.
  Before leaving, Mara paid for the sling. Esther had intended to give it freely.
  "Then use it for somebody else's groceries," Mara said.
  That evening, she told Daniel about Esther. He rubbed the repaired seam with his good hand.
  "So we keep Sunday and defend her Saturday."
  It sounded simple while they were still allowed to say it at their own table.
Chapter 7
A Record from Tomorrow
Kest demonstrated a new warning service to the national press. A refrigerated warehouse would lose power at 4:17 the next afternoon. He showed the log already received from a future branch.
  He dispatched technicians publicly. At 4:17 the predicted fault occurred. The food remained cold because the technicians had brought generators.
  This time the warning saved something. Elian watched without denying it.
  Kest called her afterward.
  "You can see we aren't Rook."
  "I can see your crew did useful work."
  "People need confidence."
  "Then show them where the confidence ends."
  He offered her access to the complete log. She found gaps, contradictory branch labels, and an omitted warning about a later relay fault. The service could receive probable fragments. It could not promise which future would remain after intervention.
  At the river camp, Rook used a similar packet to predict the fall of a tree beside the school container. He moved the children. The tree fell in a storm that evening.
  Ada thanked the workers who moved the school. She asked Rook how long he had held the warning.
  He did not answer.
  Miriam found its timestamp while charging the radio. He had known for three days, including the morning when a carpenter asked permission to cut the unstable tree. He had advised waiting.
  Ada wrote this in the camp record. Some residents called her ungrateful. Their children were alive, and suspicion felt like an insult to that relief.
  Miriam stayed beside the radio until dawn. A truthful prediction could still become a lie about the person making it.
Chapter 8
The Signature Nobody Read
Daniel's rehabilitation appointment was canceled on a Thursday. The clinic would reopen after Sunday registration was reconciled with the national patient directory.
  Salma argued with the administrator. Missing a session would not kill Daniel, but the rule covered treatments that could not safely be postponed. The administrator asked her to submit an emergency exemption for each person.
  She asked why a medical appointment required an assembly record at all.
  "It doesn't," he said. "It requires a valid recovery identity."
  The identity required the record.
  Mara found the declaration on their family account. Daniel had signed it during discharge, believing it authorized transport home. Beneath the medical consent, a smaller paragraph enrolled the household in weekly civic attendance.
  He stared at his signature. His hand had shaken so much that the last letters wandered below the line.
  "I wanted to get out of the hospital."
  "I know."
  "You were tired too."
  "I know."
  June requested withdrawal through the account portal. A notice warned that the family would lose priority in unresolved passenger claims. Daniel's transfer history still connected them to the river roster.
  Withdrawing could affect people they had not yet brought back.
  Mara printed the notice. She placed it beside the crooked crown, then moved both away from the food because she could no longer bear every meal becoming a hearing.
  At the clinic, Salma restarted Daniel's appointment under a charitable service code. The administrator warned her it would trigger inspection.
  She entered her name clearly.
  When Daniel arrived, she asked him what movement he wanted to regain first.
  "Opening a jar," he said.
Chapter 9
The Name of the Missing
Miriam's message reached Elian in separate pieces. One contained her voice; another showed a silver locket; the third held a question about a house that Elian had not lived in since childhood.
  Miriam was her father's younger sister.
  Elian remembered a woman who had left the family after a bitter disagreement nobody explained properly. Later came a notice about an accident. Her father had kept the locket's matching photograph in a drawer.
  The dates did not fit. Miriam had disappeared before the publicly known gateway experiments.
  Elian sent questions only the family could answer. Miriam described the crack in a kitchen tile and the way Elian's father sang when he thought nobody could hear. She also described how Continuity recruiters had offered her a paid research position decades earlier.
  The network's history was longer than its brochures.
  Elian sat alone after the message ended. She had searched the drowned street for a stranger because the face felt familiar. Now she had a name for the feeling and no convenient person to ask why it had been hidden.
  Hale found her holding the locket image beside her father's old photograph.
  "Do you want me to leave?"
  She shook her head.
  Miriam's last packet listed eleven residents taken in early trials. Several families had received death certificates. Rook knew where the older records were held. He would trade them for official control of the camp channel.
  Elian forwarded the names to investigators before replying to him. She would not make a private bargain with the years her family had lost.
  Then she sent Miriam a photograph of her father, old and laughing in his garden.
  There was no way to give Miriam those years back. There was still a way to let her see him.
Chapter 10
The Hearing on Rest
Ruth testified in a room whose clock had stopped during the first crisis. Nobody had repaired it. The hearings ran on screens that were easier to synchronize.
  She said the recovery law had helped her congregation feed people. She said she had supported the funding. She said attendance at worship must never become the price of bread.
  The chair asked whether the civic assembly was worship.
  Ruth read its revised program: an affirmation of the national covenant, a blessing approved by the authority, and a pledge of obedience to the recovery schedule.
  "If you require words of devotion," she said, "you cannot excuse the compulsion by changing the room's name."
  Esther described the closed purchase terminal. Esme supplied the transaction chain. Elian showed that the receivers could be safely maintained on several schedules.
  Kest listened from the front row. When he spoke, he described families whose missing relatives depended on coordination. He accused nobody of malice. He asked whether private conscience should endanger common rescue.
  Mara stood when public testimony opened.
  "My daughter was missing. I would have signed almost anything. That doesn't make almost anything right."
  Her voice broke at the last word. She resented the cameras for making that the useful part.
  The chair referred the licensing questions to an emergency panel. Existing conditions would remain until the panel reported.
  Outside, a man called Ruth a traitor to her own faith. Esther moved beside her, and they walked down the steps together.
  At Meridian, a courier delivered a notice suspending the center's passenger privileges during review.
  The hearing had not changed the rule. It had told the rule exactly where to press.
Chapter 11
The Children at the Fence
June volunteered at the reunion office because school had become a place where adults asked whether she wanted to discuss her experience and then watched her too closely when she said no.
  She sorted messages. Many were photographs of doors, watches, and handwriting that somebody hoped would prove a relative alive. She learned to say that a photograph could help without saying that help meant certainty.
  Outside the receiving compound, children waited behind a temporary fence. Their parents lacked renewed credentials. A guard brought them water but would not let them into the shade inside.
  June asked why the fence was necessary. He said it separated verified arrivals from other applicants.
  One boy had been verified three times. His father kept the papers in a plastic sleeve. Each verification used a different directory, and the latest one had expired overnight.
  June brought chairs outside. The guard objected until she asked whether the chairs had credentials.
  He helped carry the last two.
  That afternoon a lesson appeared in the recovery schools. Children should report household resistance so counselors could prevent dangerous isolation. The example described an adult refusing the Sunday covenant.
  June's younger cousin asked whether Esther was dangerous.
  June turned off the lesson and called the teacher. The teacher had not reviewed the new module; it arrived centrally and played during lunch.
  Together they printed the section and sent it to Ruth.
  Before leaving the office, June noticed that the guard had moved the fence to include the shaded pavement. It was a small change. She wrote his name in her notebook anyway.
  The system kept records of disobedience. She wanted a record of care too.
Chapter 12
A Table for Two Days

A table for two days
The coalition met in Esther's workshop. There were Sunday Christians, Sabbath keepers, people of other faiths, and people who wanted no religious test anywhere near a food card.
  Nobody agreed about prophecy. They agreed that relief should reach people without a pledge.
  Ruth proposed an open kitchen operating every day. Esther offered the workshop's rear room. The mechanic from the first memorial brought a refrigerator and refused to have his name placed above it.
  They planned shifts around volunteers' chosen rest days. The arithmetic took an hour. The authority had said such coordination was impossible at scale, but twelve tired people made a beginning with a pencil.
  Mara prepared soup. Daniel chopped what he could with his good hand. June labeled ingredients so allergies would not become another emergency.
  At the first meal, a man insisted that accepting food on Saturday betrayed Esther's beliefs. Esther handed him a bowl.
  "You may rest while I help you," she said.
  He ate without looking at her.
  The kitchen grew faster than its supply chain. Farmers sent potatoes; an authorized distributor refused their order when the payment system detected an unregistered gathering site.
  Ruth showed the refusal to her congregation. Some members feared losing their own assistance if they contributed. Others arrived after dark with grocery bags because courage had not yet made them comfortable in daylight.
  Mara recognized them and said nothing about the hour.
  On Friday evening, Esther closed the sewing machine. Ruth took the kitchen roster. On Sunday, Esther would do the same for her.
  No receiver overheated. No century collapsed because one woman rested while another worked.
Chapter 13
The Order at the Door
Inspectors arrived before lunch carrying food-safety forms. Mara welcomed them. The kitchen needed refrigeration advice, and the last thing they wanted was to make hungry people sick.
  The first inspector checked temperatures. The second asked for attendance records.
  Ruth showed the volunteer rota. He asked for the names of everybody who ate there.
  "For food safety?" she said.
  "For recovery compliance."
  She refused. He sealed the pantry under an emergency order that cited both regulations without explaining how either justified taking the food.
  Esther's son watched from beside the refrigerator. He had seen enough adults in uniform to understand that shouting would not make them leave.
  Mara asked the inspector to leave formula and medically necessary food accessible while the order was appealed. He telephoned a supervisor. They were permitted to distribute six specified packages.
  She signed for those packages and refused to sign a statement agreeing that the kitchen had endangered continuity.
  Daniel wanted to stand in the doorway. His body would not cooperate. He sat down angrily, and June sat beside him before his anger became something he had to bear alone.
  The inspectors left a notice with a red border. Ruth photographed it, copied the legal references, and sent it to counsel.
  Outside, customers waited. Mara explained what had happened without naming the families whose records had been demanded.
  A farmer lifted the soup pot into his truck. They could not open the sealed pantry. They could serve a meal from supplies that had never entered it.
  That night, the kitchen became three kitchens. It was harder to inspect a table that moved with the person willing to share it.
Chapter 14
Winter on the Timber Road
Renn drove the first mobile kitchen into the hills. Snow remained in the shaded cuts, although the valley had begun planting. He remembered the earlier evacuation and carried chains instead of trusting a forecast to keep a promise.
  At the maintenance depot, Nessa waited with her baby against her shoulder. Her husband had lost his hauling contract after refusing the covenant. They had joined relatives at the depot rather than remain in an apartment they could no longer afford.
  Renn recognized the child born during the forest flight. Her name was Lark. She had grown enough to seize his finger with unexpected strength.
  Nessa asked whether the road to Meridian remained open. Yes, he said. The checkpoint farther south was restricting subsidized fuel, but ordinary traffic could pass.
  She wanted a map on paper.
  He gave her one and marked fuel stops with different symbols for confirmed and unconfirmed supplies. She folded it into the baby's bag.
  That evening an elderly traveler became ill. The depot called the ambulance service. The crew arrived without asking for a covenant record. Their employer later requested explanations for the unapproved route.
  Renn helped them document the call. He had become weary of the way ordinary decency needed evidence after the fact.
  The snow closed the higher road before midnight. They stayed together, heated two rooms, and checked the generator outdoors. Nessa slept at last while Mara held Lark on a chair beside the window.
  Mara had come to bring supplies. She remained because the baby settled against her chest, and because leaving had become a decision she preferred to make in daylight.
Chapter 15
The Gate with Two Calendars
Hale established a narrow communication link with the river camp. The channel used low power and could not move a person. He documented the limit in every transmission because hope tended to enlarge machinery.
  Ada placed her notebook in front of the camera. The camp's day count differed from the authority's by four days. Their interval had drifted during an alignment fault.
  Kest required Sunday attendance according to the national clock. Rook required it according to the camp clock. Compliance with both would create nearly continuous assemblies during some weeks.
  "Ask which day the battery believes in," Ada said.
  Hale laughed before remembering that people were losing meals over the answer.
  He found a transport opportunity. A surviving ground receiver could align with the river bank during a ninety-minute interval. The safest window began on Saturday afternoon, Meridian time. Its cooling cycle made postponement until Sunday unsafe.
  Elian submitted an emergency request with the heat curves attached. Kest's office offered a Sunday window instead.
  She explained again. The reply cited national unity.
  Miriam came into the frame carrying a sick child. Ada had forty-six people now, including arrivals from an older branch. Several were declining despite the camp medic's care.
  Elian said she was working on transport. Miriam asked her not to promise a day until the machinery and the people agreed.
  After the link ended, Hale drew Saturday's window on the board. Beneath it he wrote the receiver capacity, safe load, and available power.
  The figures made a route. The law had made a barrier across it.
Chapter 16
The Covenant on the Screen
The revised Sunday assembly included a national address. Kest appeared on screens at schools, depots, churches, and receiving halls. His image looked directly into the camera while local attendants checked household cards.
  He spoke of shared suffering. Then he introduced the continuity covenant. People would affirm obedience to the authority's schedule and reject unauthorized rescue activity.
  Ruth stopped the screen in her church before the affirmation.
  Some congregants applauded. Others asked whether she had just canceled their benefits. She could not tell them there would be no consequence.
  "We can pray here," she said. "We can feed people here. I cannot require this promise from you."
  One woman left in tears because her missing son's claim depended on attendance. Ruth followed her outside and helped her telephone the recovery office. The woman enrolled elsewhere. Ruth did not call her faithless.
  At the river camp, Rook projected the address onto the container doors. He stood beneath Kest's face as if they shared an altar.
  Ada refused the pledge. Rook stopped battery power to the medical refrigerator.
  The medic shouted. Miriam restored the connection while Ada called the whole camp to witness what had happened.
  Rook insisted he was conserving energy for registered users. The battery meter showed enough charge for three days of cooling.
  A fisherman stepped between Rook and the hut. Another resident joined him. They did not attack him. They told him the refrigerator would remain connected.
  In Meridian, Elian watched the saved footage with her hands flat on the desk.
  The image could make promises. The people standing beneath it could make refusal hungry.
Chapter 17
The Call Nobody Returned
Peter received a private offer. If he supplied the coalition's locations, the authority would expedite Celia's treatment and clear the restrictions on their accommodation.
  He had once betrayed shelter records for news of her. The memory had not made him incapable of wanting an easier way. It made the wanting unbearable.
  Celia found the message open on his phone.
  He began explaining before she asked.
  "I haven't sent anything."
  She read it to the end. Then she sat down because standing had begun to hurt since her captivity.
  "Last time you told yourself it was for me."
  "It was."
  "It was also because you couldn't bear not knowing what to do."
  He looked away. She waited until he looked back.
  They took the message to Esme. Peter gave a statement, including the part about being tempted. He expected condemnation and was almost disappointed when Esme asked practical questions.
  How had the sender known Celia's next appointment? Which office held the accommodation file? Had anyone telephoned?
  The offer came through a contractor running compliance investigations for Kest's authority. Its access reached medical scheduling and refugee housing.
  Celia consented to release the relevant records. She declined a televised interview.
  That evening Peter brought dinner to their room. He asked before sitting beside her.
  She said he could stay. Forgiveness, if it came, would not arrive as a document he could file and stop reading.
  His phone rang during the meal. The contractor wanted an answer.
  He silenced it and let Celia finish telling him about her day.
Chapter 18
The Woman in the Old Photograph
Miriam's next message contained a name Elian had not heard: Dr. Ansel Voss, her grandfather. He had worked on the original alignment model and left when Continuity refused to publish its human trials.
  His departure had not erased his work. Later teams copied the model and replaced its consent protocol with administrative authorization.
  Elian found his papers in a university archive available through an ordinary public request. There was no prophecy code, only patient diagrams and notes admitting uncertainty.
  One page described alignment as a relation between locations rather than a road through empty time. Closing a main receiver could leave isolated relationships active. That explained the river camp more clearly than the authority's language of divine correction.
  Another page required two independently controlled endpoints. Neither side should be able to transport people by itself.
  Elian held the page for a long time.
  They had been restoring Rook's routes with one end still capable of making decisions for the other. Her grandfather had identified the danger before she was born.
  Hale began adapting the model. Ada could control a camp-side key. Meridian could control its own. Both would have to authorize every batch.
  The scheme needed a component aboard Haven's receiving station. Chen offered to carry it, then stopped when Milo asked whether this was another trip he might not return from.
  Chen sat beside his son and described the route honestly. A different pilot could go if they chose that together.
  Milo asked to meet the crew first.
  This was slower than issuing an order. Elian thought her grandfather would have approved of the lost hour.
Chapter 19
The Invitation to the Mountain
Kest invited Elian to a retreat at an isolated command lodge. His message offered direct access to the river receiver and a confidential agreement protecting her aunt.
  The public invitation said national recovery specialists were gathering to witness a revelation about the missing.
  Elian asked for remote access to the evidence. The reply said the revelation could be understood only in person.
  Hale pointed out that the lodge held a restored receiver. The authority had moved it there from a public laboratory without updating the licensing record.
  Esme obtained construction photographs. Beneath the meeting hall, power cables entered a room absent from the visitor plan. The equipment could receive previews and project a human image above the gathering.
  Ruth received an invitation too. It called her refusal a misunderstanding that spiritual unity could heal.
  She wrote back asking whether Esther could attend without pledging. There was no reply.
  Elian declined the private bargain. She offered to evaluate the equipment at the public laboratory with independent observers.
  Kest telephoned.
  "Your aunt has waited decades. Do you want her to wait because you dislike the setting?"
  Elian almost said yes to the lodge. She heard how small the conditions sounded beside Miriam's years.
  Then she remembered her grandfather's page: two ends, two permissions.
  "Send Ada the operating controls," she said. "Then we'll discuss a journey."
  He ended the call politely.
  At the river camp, Rook announced that Meridian had refused an offer of rescue. He omitted the controls.
  Miriam asked to read the correspondence. He told her he did not want to burden her with technicalities.
  She went to find Ada.
Chapter 20
The Saturday Window
The receiver test succeeded with an empty crate. Ada inspected it at the river bank, placed a colored stone inside, and authorized its return. The stone reached Meridian without the crate moving outside the marked area.
  They repeated the test with sensors, then with plant samples under containment. Salma and the safety team reviewed the receiving conditions. A person would require more than a successful object transfer.
  The camp medic sent the urgent cases. The window could safely carry nine people per cycle. Four cycles were possible before cooling. Forty-six residents would require a second window or a separate route.
  Ada chose priorities with the residents, recording objections and arrangements for the people who would wait. Rook offered a larger receiver capable of taking everyone at once.
  He would not provide its transport logs.
  On Friday, Kest issued a revised emergency order. Unauthorized Saturday transfers were suspended to protect the national Sunday recovery interval. Licensed operators violating the order could be detained; their passenger directories could be seized.
  The physical window would close before the national gathering began. The authority had known this when it wrote the order.
  Esther read the notice at Meridian. She did not say that rescue must stop because it was Saturday.
  "Help them," she said. "That's what the day is for."
  Ruth volunteered at receiving. Salma organized the medical team. Daniel asked to keep the passenger ledger because his arm could not carry a stretcher but his handwriting was still his.
  June wrote the first thirty-six names on separate cards.
  When they finished, Mara counted the cards twice. She remembered how much a number could conceal.
Chapter 21
The Empty Shoes
On Saturday morning, nine people disappeared from a compliance assembly in Dunmere. The building remained. A chair tipped onto its side. One man's shoes lay beneath it because he had removed them to ease swollen feet.
  The authority called the event an unplanned temporal incident. Commentators called it judgment. Someone posted a list accusing the missing of secret resistance.
  The list contained a woman who had led the covenant for three weeks.
  June knew her granddaughter from school. She called before the rumors reached the girl's house. The girl asked whether being taken meant her grandmother was saved or condemned.
  "We don't know where she went," June said. "People shouldn't tell you they know what that says about her."
  Esme compared the building's equipment orders with the absent room beneath Kest's lodge. Both used the same alignment controller. The assemblies were becoming linked collection points.
  The Sunday attendance system supplied exact locations and names. When it failed, it failed around people assembled according to its instructions.
  Elian requested suspension of the controllers. Kest's engineers acknowledged the fault privately. His office blamed unauthorized traffic publicly.
  At Meridian, the Saturday receiver was still cold. They had not opened it when Dunmere emptied.
  Hale preserved the independent power logs. An accusation could arrive faster than a passenger, and he intended to meet it with timestamps.
  Ada sent a message that nine new people had appeared in a clearing upstream. One was missing her shoes.
  The camp now had fifty-five residents.
  June called the granddaughter again. They had a report, she said. Verification was underway.
  She stayed on the line while the girl woke her mother.
Chapter 22
The Reservoir Below the Hall
Meridian's cooling reservoir turned brown during a routine sample. Hale shut the transfer circuit down before opening. It was contaminated by runoff from a damaged industrial line, not by the color of the morning sky.
  The cause was ordinary. Its timing was terrible.

Chapter 1
The Sea Above the Door
Nell recognized Micah by the way he carried a door.
  Five weeks apart, three uncertain messages, and there he was at the receiving platform with his shoulder beneath an oak panel, arguing with someone she could not see. Water ran from his hair. He had a bandage around one wrist and a pencil behind his ear. He looked so ordinarily alive that for a moment she could not get out of the shuttle.
  Beyond him, an ocean occupied the sky.
  It rested on transparent vaults a thousand feet above the platform. Pale creatures moved through it, trailing ribbons of light. Sunlight entered from its far side and broke over the city in restless blue squares. Towers stood beneath those squares like furniture at the bottom of a swimming pool. At the city's center a white orchard lifted its branches toward the water.
  Someone had hung washing between two statues on the platform. A child's sock revolved slowly in the draft.
  Micah put down the door.
  “You're early.”
  “You're wet.”
  “There was a disagreement about what a roof is.”
  She wanted to laugh. Instead she asked about his wrist.
  He held it out, explaining the splinter, the stitches, the indignity of being ordered off a ladder. He did not try to embrace her while she was examining him. That restraint, learned over months at North Terrace, made her stop examining him.
  “Come here,” she said.
  His good hand settled between her shoulder blades. His coat smelled of sawdust and the mineral water of an unfamiliar world. She kept her face against it until a porter asked whether they intended to unload their luggage or establish a religion around it.
  The city was called Pelagos. It occupied an ancient carrier whose outer engines had gone quiet before anyone living there was born. Eleven thousand seven hundred people inhabited the vaults, gardens, markets, and maintenance decks beneath the sea. Their council had asked the harbor settlements for repairs and transport. Micah had volunteered for the first assessment crew.
  Now Mercy had brought Nell, spare equipment, and a map.
  At the customs table she laid the map flat. Micah leaned over it. The ink showed a route through a passage that opened only when two immense pieces of abandoned transit machinery aligned. On the other side lay a cluster of agricultural settlements founded by the ancestors of North Terrace. Their new reply had confirmed working docks, breathable habitats, and places for displaced families.
  “Sixty-three days,” she said. “The opening lasts twenty hours. Six days to reach it from here.”
  Micah studied the little green mark denoting arrival.
  “They really answered?”
  “They sent a woman arguing about school places. I trust that more than a welcome proclamation.”
  He smiled, but slowly.
  Nell noticed.
  “What?”
  Before he could answer, a low vibration passed through the platform. The customs clerk put a hand over her tea. Everyone looked up. Far above, something dark moved along a seam in the transparent sea floor.
  Micah took Nell's bag.
  “There's somebody you need to meet.”
  The clerk waited until the vibration stopped before lifting her cup again.
Chapter 2
A Room with Two Futures
Director Vey met them in a room with buckets beneath the ceiling. Each bucket bore the name of a department that had lent it. Treasury was nearly full.
  A woman in a diver's thermal suit stood beside a structural drawing. Her name was Adra. She had lost two fingers and displayed no interest in making that fact educational.
  “The transparent vault is a roof,” she told Nell. “The field above it carries most of the load. Both have been working beyond their inspected life. We have thirty-one days before we reach the limit of what I can presently certify.”
  “Presently?” Nell asked.
  “There may be repairs. I don't sell them before I've tested them.”
  Vey slid an evacuation chart across the table. There were seats for almost everyone if every contracted vessel arrived, if the west docks reopened, if residents could travel in standard acceleration couches. The final column contained one hundred and eighty-six names.
  Dr. Suri, who had been quiet until then, turned the chart so Nell could read the heading.
  “These people cannot safely make the fast passage in the available equipment. Some need supported ventilation. Some have unstable injuries. Several are children. We can move them locally in shielded hospital barges. The long transit needs suspension cradles we do not have.”
  Micah had repaired the hospital's window frames. He had also promised to stay until its last patient had a safe berth.
  Nell heard him explain this as if she were sitting at the far end of a very long room.
  “Who authorized the promise?” Vey asked.
  “I did,” Micah said. “It commits my work. Nobody else's.”
  Nell looked down at the map she had brought. A route home and a list of people who could not take it occupied the same table without touching.
  That evening Micah showed her the room assigned to visiting engineers. Two narrow beds stood beneath a window overlooking the orchard. He had moved his bag to the workmen's dormitory before her arrival.
  “I didn't know what you'd want,” he said.
  “You could have asked.”
  “I sent a message.”
  “Your message said the hinges here were criminal.”
  “The next one asked.”
  She had not received it. In the harbor, messages still passed from vessel to vessel whenever direct relays failed. They had learned to date everything, and still lost whole pieces of one another's weeks.
  Micah put a kettle on the room's little heater. On the sill lay a folded cloth, a chipped bowl, and a spray of white leaves in a bottle.
  “Are those yours?”
  “The cloth. The bowl is an inheritance from a man who went to stay with his sister. The leaves are theft.”
  “Serious theft?”
  “Council gardener. Terrifying woman.”
  Nell sat on one of the beds. She had imagined seeing him at a doorway that belonged to them, with no next departure already arranged. Instead there were thirty-one days above them and sixty-three ahead.
  He poured the tea too early. She drank it without comment.
  “Stay for supper,” she said.
  He looked toward the second bed, then back to her face.
  “Supper,” she repeated, smiling now.
  “I heard you.”
  Outside, the water shifted. Their small room brightened, darkened, and brightened again.
Chapter 3
The Woman Who Mended Stars
The gardener's name was Sabet. She wore a silver patch over one eye and maintained that the orchard's trees were not white at all, merely covered in a protective coating that reflected the artificial moon.
  “People say white trees because people are lazy,” she told Nell. “They also say moon when they mean a lamp with delusions.”
  The lamp traveled on a circular track beneath the sea. At evening it dimmed the upper light and moved over the gardens, giving their flowering branches a luminous edge. Micah had described it in a message Nell had received during a pump failure. She had read his words with sewage on her sleeve and thought he was exaggerating.
  Now they stood beneath it. Each blossom had a pale green center. Bees sheltered in heated boxes against the trunks.
  Sabet gave Nell a handful of pruning clips and pointed her toward an injured branch.
  “I've come to inspect the distribution network.”
  “It's beneath your feet. You can inspect it while making yourself useful.”
  Micah settled at the next tree. He used his good hand badly until Nell took the clips from him. Sabet observed this without interfering.
  People arrived for the evening shift, bringing baskets, arguments, children who did not want to help. A boy called Oren carried a rabbit in a sling because its cage had been requisitioned for transport documents. He asked Micah whether the ocean would fall before the rabbits left.
  “We're making places for them,” Micah said.
  “That isn't what I asked.”
  “No,” Micah admitted. “It isn't.”
  Nell crouched to look at the animal. Its nose worked rapidly against the cloth.
  “We'll inspect another seam tomorrow. Then we'll know more. Would you like to help mark the cages so nobody forgets one?”
  Oren considered the offer with the seriousness it deserved.
  After he left, Sabet led them through a low gate. Beyond it lay a nursery of seedlings planted in cups, jars, salvaged drawers. One yellow flower had been drawn on a label.
  “From North Terrace,” Sabet said. “A girl sent seeds. Lio. Her mother enclosed extremely firm advice about drainage.”
  Nell touched the edge of the label. Lio's first calendula had once survived on water a thirsty child kept back from herself. These seeds were its descendants. Someone had carried them all the way here before Nell arrived.
  Micah watched her.
  “What?” she asked.
  “You look as if you found a relative.”
  “Perhaps I did.”
  Sabet left them to close the gate. On its latch Micah had installed a small brass star. He said the original handle caught children's sleeves.
  Nell turned it between her fingers.
  “You put stars on things now?”
  “Only where they're structurally necessary.”
  She kissed him beside the latch. He made a startled sound, then steadied her with his arm. For the first time since her arrival, she stopped calculating how long it would take to leave.
  In the branches overhead, the bees settled. Somewhere beyond the wall, Sabet announced loudly that she required her clips back eventually.
Chapter 4
The Preacher beneath the Water
Asa arrived on the fourth day with medical stores, three volunteers, and a letter from Tavi that he would not let anyone read over his shoulder.
  He had been invited to speak in the old observatory. Its glass dome faced upward into the suspended sea. During the sermon a shoal of small fish passed over his head, and half the room followed them instead of his words.
  Asa waited.
  “Beautiful, aren't they?” he said. “I can wait another moment.”
  Nell sat beside Micah on the low wall. She had spent the morning disputing measurements with a council engineer who rounded losses downward because the exact figures frightened people. Her notes lay open on her lap.
  Asa read from First Corinthians about charity. Then he closed the book and described a visit he had once failed to make. A woman had asked him to sit with her dying brother. He had sent someone else because he thought a public meeting needed him more. The substitute had been kind. The brother had died. The woman had never said she forgave him, and he had no right to write that ending on her behalf.
  “When we speak of love bearing all things,” he said, “we must be careful whose shoulders we volunteer.”
  An old transport worker interrupted.
  “People have to leave loved ones. Are you saying they don't love them?”
  “No. I'm asking what we owe each other when staying and going both cost something.”
  “That isn't much of an answer.”
  “It may take work to make it one.”
  Afterward Nell found Asa carrying the borrowed benches back through a narrow doorway.
  “How much of someone are you allowed to bear?” she asked.
  He set his end down. Micah stood farther along the corridor talking with Suri; Nell made sure he could not hear.
  “Do you mean their pain?” Asa asked.
  “Their promises. The ones that decide your life before you enter the room.”
  He looked toward Micah and then deliberately away.
  “You're allowed to say you haven't agreed.”
  “And if that means leaving someone in danger?”
  Asa wiped his palms on his trousers. “Then we need other people in the conversation. Love between two people cannot be the entire evacuation plan.”
  She almost laughed. It sounded too practical for what she had wanted him to solve.
  That afternoon he asked Suri for a list of transport needs and took it to the council. The next morning he missed a proposed second sermon because a family needed help carrying an adult son down seven flights of stairs.
  Nell saw him near the last landing, red-faced and listening carefully while the son explained how to avoid hurting his hip.
  Micah reached her there with bread wrapped in paper. They ate against the stair rail while the group rested.
  “I heard what he said yesterday,” Micah told her. “About volunteering shoulders.”
  She looked at him.
  “I should have talked to you before you got here.”
  “Yes,” she said.
  He stayed beside her after the answer. Neither tried to make it smaller.
Chapter 5
An Invitation to the Last Ball
The invitations were printed on paper made from orchard prunings. Nell received hers in a drain inspection chamber, where a girl in a red coat lowered it on a string.
  “The last ball,” the girl announced.
  “Who says it's the last?”
  “My grandmother. She says that every year.”
  The ballroom occupied the lowest level of the old civic theater. Its windows looked into a disused boating canal, empty now except for mineral stains and the reflection of the moon lamp. Council staff wanted to cancel the gathering. The theater committee argued that people packing their whole lives deserved one evening they could remember without a manifest.
  Permission came with a limit on attendance and an inspected evacuation route. Nell signed off the drainage pumps herself.
  Micah appeared that night wearing a dark coat borrowed from a man substantially wider than he was. Someone had tucked the shoulders. He looked uncomfortable and handsome, and knowing that he had gone to the trouble made Nell look at the floor.
  “What?” he asked.
  “Nothing.”
  “That was a specific nothing.”
  She straightened his collar. “You look like a man who's committed a ceremonial crime.”
  “Then stand close. Hide the evidence.”
  They danced badly at first. Nell had learned turns from people who expected her to know them already. Micah counted aloud until she told him she could hear. Around them elderly couples moved with the economy of long practice. Two children slid under the refreshment tables. Sabet sat in a chair beside the musicians, threatening the drummer with a pruning clip.
  Halfway through the third piece, water appeared beneath the canal doors.
  Nell felt it before she saw it: a cold touch across the sole of her shoe. A service pipe had split beyond the ballroom, feeding the abandoned canal. She left Micah and crossed to the pump indicator.
  The first pump was working. The second had tripped.
  She told the musicians to stop and asked people to walk toward the upper doors. Some obeyed immediately; others looked for coats, partners, a child hidden under a table. Micah climbed onto the band platform and called names, pairing those who needed help with those who could offer it.
  Nell waded to the service recess. Water lifted her skirt against her knees. An attendant held the work lamp while she reset the protection switch, inspected the intake, and found the strip of torn curtain caught across it.
  By the time the second pump began to pull, the last guests had reached the stair.
  Micah waited on the landing.
  “You lost a shoe.”
  She looked down. He was right.
  From below came a single violin phrase. The violinist, dry on the upper platform, tried the tune again as if asking permission.
  Nell held out her hand.
  “One minute,” she said.
  They danced on the landing in wet clothes while the attendants checked the empty room. Micah's coat dripped onto her bare foot. When the violin stopped, he bent his forehead to hers, and she felt how hard his heart was beating.
  Behind them the canal doors groaned. They collected the lamp and went upstairs together.
Chapter 6
The Names That Would Not Travel
Suri's hospital had once been a hotel. Brass hooks still stood inside the wardrobe of each room, and the emergency bell sounded like someone politely requesting breakfast.
  Nell spent the sixth morning measuring its freight lift. A standard suspension cradle would not fit through the door even if they found one. Suri watched her try the measurement twice.
  “People keep arriving with equipment that ends at the corridor,” the doctor said.
  “I was hoping the corridor would have grown.”
  Suri smiled without interrupting her rounds.
  In the next room a former singer named Ilya was teaching Oren a card trick. Her legs were supported by splints after a transport collision. Three beds away, a young father slept with a breathing mask over his face and his daughter's hand resting on the blanket.
  These were the people represented by the final column.
  Micah had asked to attend the planning session. Nell made room beside her. Suri invited four patients and two family representatives; nobody would be planning for them in their absence if they could reasonably attend.
  Ilya studied the route chart.
  “Suppose we decline your ancestral settlements,” she said. “Where else can we go?”
  “West Orchard can receive forty on hospital barges,” Nell answered. “Low Bell has offered thirty-two. Two other settlements are assessing capacity.”
  “That sounds more useful than telling us about a home we've never heard of.”
  Nell felt the correction and wrote it down.
  A mechanic named Rusk said their carrier had once possessed a whole medical transit deck. Its storage inventory had been lost when the north ring separated generations earlier. The ring now traveled on the far side of the broken moon called Vesper. No working dock faced Pelagos. A maintenance railway crossed the stable ridge between its two surviving sections, but a shuttle had to reach the first station.
  “How old are these records?” Suri asked.
  “Old enough to be wrong,” Rusk said. “Recent enough to check.”
  They divided the work. Suri would specify what could genuinely help. Nell would examine access and power requirements. Micah would determine whether the beds could be dismantled for the freight lift. Vey would request local berths without waiting for a miracle from an archive.
  After the meeting Ilya called Nell back.
  “You look disappointed.”
  “I wanted to arrive with an answer.”
  “Try arriving with lunch tomorrow. That's easier to verify.”
  Nell brought lunch. She stayed while Ilya lost at cards and accused Oren of professional fraud.
  Later she found Micah marking the lift frame with chalk. He had a strip of white blossom caught in his cuff from the orchard.
  “Did you tell her we'd go to Vesper?” Nell asked.
  “I told her we'd find out whether we could.”
  She checked his drawing. Two bed sections, angled separately, would clear the door.
  Outside the window, a great luminous animal moved through the sea. The whole room dimmed beneath its passing. Ilya stopped pretending to lose and looked up until the light returned.
Chapter 7
The Map of Returning
Elian's reply arrived with a grocery invoice and three minutes of unrelated harbor announcements spliced into the middle.
  She appeared in Nell's screen wearing the expression she used when she had refused something twice and expected to have to refuse it again. Behind her, North Terrace's common table was covered in manifests.
  “The receiving settlements have confirmed six hundred and forty places for our first convoy,” she said. “Families are deciding. Some want to remain here. Nobody is being entered without consent.”
  Nell paused the message. Six hundred and forty places meant rooms, lessons, tools, time to grow food without first negotiating who owned the light. It meant people could stop calling every accommodation temporary.
  Micah sat at the other end of the workbench repairing a strap.
  “She wants you to lead the survey ship,” he said.
  “How do you know?”
  “Because you're good at it.”
  Nell restarted the recording. Elian asked exactly that. Mercy would carry the convoy's advance team, including a second navigator to learn the route. Departure preparations would begin in forty days. Nell could return sooner if the Pelagos work allowed it.
  The invitation was everything she had wanted before seeing the ocean above this city.
  Micah threaded the strap through its buckle too carefully.
  “Say something,” she told him.
  “I want you to go if you want to.”
  “That sentence has somewhere to hide in it.”
  He put down the strap.
  “I want a room with you. I also want you to have work you don't have to apologize for wanting. I don't know how to make those fit yet.”
  She sat beside him. “I don't know either.”
  For a while they listened to footsteps in the passage. A neighbor was trying to maneuver a table through a doorway, and a running commentary suggested the doorway was at fault.
  Micah took a folded sheet from his pocket. It showed a little house with an enclosed porch. Beneath the window he had drawn a shelf, then crossed it out and drawn it lower.
  “You keep putting cups on high shelves,” he said. “I thought I could improve your life.”
  She laughed, then stopped because her eyes had filled.
  He put the drawing between them instead of pressing it into her hand.
  “It's not a contract.”
  “I know.”
  They changed the porch together. Nell wanted a workbench by the window. Micah argued that wet coats would drip on the tools. They found a place for hooks. Nobody mentioned that they had not chosen a world to build it on.
  Her reply to Elian accepted the survey work conditionally and named the second navigator she would need. She explained the hospital problem in full. She did not ask Elian to solve her relationship.
  When she finished, Micah handed her the drawing.
  “Keep it until we've argued about the roof.”
  She folded it along its existing crease, carefully enough to preserve the little penciled cups.
Chapter 8
Vesper Station
The shuttle approached Vesper along its broken edge.
  Once the moon had been round. A collision had opened one hemisphere into a landscape of terraces and exposed dark stone. The maintenance ring had survived in two pieces, connected along a ridge by a pressurized railway. It looked less like a monument than a machine someone had put down halfway through taking it apart.
  Damas kept the shuttle above the marked debris band. Nell sat beside him checking the rail station's beacon against their approach chart. Micah, Adra, and Rusk rode behind them with tools and enough supplies for three days.
  “Don't admire anything close enough to hit it,” Damas said.
  Nell returned to the instruments.
  They docked at a station containing two chairs, a dead vending machine, and a mural of children looking at an intact moon. Beyond the airlock, the railway carriage waited under maintenance lights. Rusk tested its seals. Adra checked the emergency air. Micah found a bird's nest in a control cabinet, preserved from a time when birds had actually lived in the gardens attached to the ring.
  He photographed it before moving it to a shelf.
  The train traveled slowly. Through the windows the broken landscape rose and fell, ridges lit on one side and utterly dark on the other. Fragments of old habitat roofs glittered in hollows. Far away, Pelagos carried its sea like a blue jewel trapped inside a larger shell.
  Nell sat opposite Micah with her boots against his. There was nobody else in their compartment. Adra was inspecting a coupling; Rusk had followed her to argue; Damas remained with the shuttle.
  “I used to think you'd get tired of waiting for me,” Nell said.
  Micah looked up.
  “After the harbor. After everything kept taking longer.”
  “I did get tired.”
  The answer caught her.
  He leaned forward. “Of waiting without knowing whether you wanted me there. I wasn't tired of you.”
  She studied a scrape on the window frame.
  “I didn't know how to offer anything I could keep.”
  “You offered supper. You kept offering it.”
  The train entered shadow. Their reflections replaced the landscape, sitting across from each other inside a small lit room moving over ruins.
  Nell reached for his hand. She felt the rough skin at the base of his fingers, the pulse at his wrist. There were whole years of his life she knew mostly through explanations of how things had been broken and repaired. She wanted the years themselves.
  “What did you look like at sixteen?”
  “Unfortunate.”
  “That's vague.”
  “There was hair.”
  “Show me.”
  “I was hoping to marry before that evidence emerged.”
  They both went still.
  At the coupling, Rusk dropped something that rang along the carriage floor. Adra swore. The ordinary sounds gave them a moment to breathe.
  Micah did not withdraw his hand.
  “Not a proposal,” he said quietly. “A wish.”
  Nell turned his hand over and laid her cheek against it until the train came out of the shadow.
Chapter 9
The Cabinet of Sleeping Travelers
The medical archive lay behind an airlock that had not opened in sixty-two years.
  Rusk read the maintenance date aloud. Adra checked for a pressure difference. Micah inspected the hinge pins while Nell isolated a power circuit that someone had bridged with an incompatible fuse long before any of them were born.
  They opened the door by hand.
  Inside stood six suspension cradles and eleven empty frames. Dust had gathered along their covers in thin gray lines. A cabinet held service records, diagnostic modules, and a photograph of staff gathered around an absurdly large cake.
  Suri had given them a checklist. They followed it without turning the discovery into a rescue before the tests were done.
  Two cradles failed immediately. One had a cracked support shell. The remaining three passed the preliminary electrical checks but would need calibration, sterile liners, and a physician's review. They could help. They could not carry one hundred and eighty-six people through a single narrow departure window.
  Micah sat on an empty frame.
  “I thought there'd be more.”
  Rusk examined the floor. Drag marks led toward a freight door.
  “There were.”
  The freight records named a relief station called Lantern. The station had received most of the equipment forty years earlier. Its current location was not in the archive.
  Nell copied the records. Under the medical files she found transit diagrams for a second route through the old machinery. A slow service passage followed a different orbit and emerged near Lantern. The diagram contained intervals, mass limits, and a stern warning against using its estimated dates as a navigation plan.
  She read that warning twice.
  At the far wall Micah had opened a staff locker. Inside lay a blue coat, a tin cup, and a letter addressed to someone who had apparently never collected it. He closed the locker again.
  “What was it?” Nell asked.
  “Somebody's.”
  They worked until the station lights changed to evening. On the return journey, Adra slept sitting upright, one glove still on. Rusk counted the secured cases at every station even though nobody could have removed one in transit.
  Nell told Micah about Lantern.
  “A second chance?” he asked.
  “A line on an old chart. We can find out whether it's still usable.”
  He nodded. He had learned how hope sounded in her voice when she was protecting it.
  At a curve the train slowed beside a section of exposed rock. The broken moon filled Nell's window. Micah's reflection lay over it, pale and tired, looking at her.
  “I meant the wish,” he said.
  She rested her head against the seat. “I know.”
  “Does that frighten you?”
  “Yes.”
  He waited.
  “I'd like you to keep meaning it.”
  Outside, a fragment of ancient mirror caught the sun. For one brief instant light entered their compartment from below, illuminating the undersides of their hands.
Chapter 10
The Weight of a Promise
The cradles arrived at the hospital to less applause than Oren had organized. Suri forbade anyone from putting a patient into them until her team had finished the tests. Oren redirected the celebration toward unloading disinfectant.
  At the council, Nell presented the archive records and the limits of what they had found. Vey wanted the Lantern route announced immediately.
  “People need something to believe in.”
  “They need the dates to be real,” Nell said. “We don't have them.”
  He accepted the objection with visible effort. Then he asked if the first main evacuation could be advanced by four days. A crack had lengthened along the southern vault.
  They spent the afternoon rearranging departures.
  Micah was waiting outside with a meal he had packed before the meeting. The bread had gone damp beneath the stew container. Nell ate it anyway.
  “I've been talking as if keeping my promise means doing everything myself,” he said.
  She looked at him over the container.
  “Suri told me I'm not allowed to decide her patients need me more than qualified transport crews.”
  “What did you say?”
  “Something noble. She ignored it.”
  Nell smiled.
  “I can stay until safe local berths are arranged,” he continued. “Then I can travel. The promise wasn't to remain in this building forever.”
  “Do you want the passage?”
  “I want to see what home could be. With you. I also want to stop turning that into an argument against people who need help.”
  They took the empty containers back to the shared kitchen. A cook made Micah wash them before continuing his important conversation.
  That night they met Asa in the observatory. He had asked whether anyone wanted to read Daniel together before he spoke again. Five residents came, then eleven. The passage concerned the great image and the succession of kingdoms. A transport official suggested the broken moon might be the feet of the image.
  “Where does the text name this moon?” Sabet asked.
  “It doesn't,” he admitted.
  Asa kept the reading open on the table. They discussed what people expected from powerful institutions, and how quickly an evacuation director could begin sounding indispensable.
  Micah watched Vey, who had quietly joined them at the back.
  Afterward the director asked for volunteers to review his passenger classifications. He wanted patients and families represented. Sabet agreed, then insisted the meetings start after the orchard watering.
  On the walk home Nell slipped her hand into Micah's pocket because the evening ventilation was cold. He wrapped his fingers around hers there.
  “I've started saying when I need to stop,” he told her.
  “What happens?”
  “Usually somebody else knows how to do the work.”
  She leaned into him. Under the ocean, in a city quietly preparing to leave, that was the first thing either of them had said about their future that made her feel less afraid.
Chapter 11
The House with No Address
On the eleventh day they borrowed an unused room above a bakery to decide what they meant by together.
  The baker lent them the room because her husband was practicing a wind instrument downstairs and she wanted witnesses to the suffering. They could hear every uncertain note through the floor.
  Nell spread Micah's house drawing on a flour-dusted table. Beside it she put two blank sheets.
  “I don't want us to get married because the roof is frightening,” she said.
  “Agreed.”
  “I want work. Sometimes travel. I want a place where leaving for a week doesn't become proof I don't love you.”
  Micah took the pencil. “I want to be told when the week becomes a month.”
  She winced. “Fair.”
  “I want arguments that end without one of us suddenly finding an urgent ladder.”
  “Which of us?”
  “I'm willing to put both names down.”
  They wrote about money, rooms, privacy, time. Nell had been afraid that practical questions would make the wish smaller. Instead she discovered that Micah intended to live with her actual habits. He knew she worked badly when hungry and disliked being touched from behind when concentrating. She knew he grew silent before admitting he could not afford something.
  Downstairs the instrument produced a note so terrible that they both stopped.
  “I'd still marry you if you did that,” Nell said. “But we might need separate buildings.”
  The laughter loosened her enough to ask the question she had avoided.
  “Do you want children?”
  Micah set down the pencil.
  “I've imagined it. I don't know what our life will allow. Do you?”
  “Sometimes. Sometimes I can't imagine being responsible for anyone who can't tell me what hurts.”
  He did not turn her uncertainty into a promise to persuade her later. They wrote that the conversation would remain open, and that either could change without being accused of hiding a verdict.
  When they went downstairs, the baker read the flour on Nell's sleeve and said she hoped they had negotiated cleaning duties.
  “Extensively,” Micah said.
  She gave them two burned rolls free and charged them for the good ones.
  They ate beside the empty boating canal. Work crews had removed the ballroom's ruined curtain. The room looked strangely naked without it.
  Micah touched the place on Nell's hand where a wire had left a thin scratch.
  “I love you,” he said.
  She had heard the words from him before. Here, after discussing the price of bread and the possibility of children, they reached her differently.
  “I love you too.”
  He shut his eyes for a moment. She kissed the closed lids, one at a time. It was an awkward thing to do in public. She liked that they were becoming people who could bear a little awkwardness.
  Above the canal, the artificial moon moved a fraction along its track. Their shadows shifted together over the stone.
Chapter 12
The Second Sea
The southern inspection required Nell to enter a service chamber between the transparent vault and the sea's support field.
  From the city below, that space looked empty. Inside it, pipes hung in a lattice, condensation fell in warm drops, and the immense weight of water made the field emit a sound like breathing through teeth.
  Adra clipped Nell's safety line to an independent rail.
  “You see a blue shimmer, you tell me. You hear a pop, we leave. You drop a tool, it belongs to history.”
  They crawled along a platform barely wide enough for their knees. Through the transparent floor Nell saw the orchard, tiny and orderly. Micah was somewhere beneath it fitting a transport ramp. She had never liked heights. Loving someone below her had not improved them.

  Adra photographed it. Nell checked the sensors and found one reporting almost no strain.
  “That can't be right.”
  “It has been wrong for years,” Adra said. “We've requested replacement.”

Chapter 13
The Messages They Had Lost
Damas delivered six messages that had traveled the wrong way around the harbor.
  One was a request for replacement bearings already fulfilled. Another announced a departure that had happened three weeks earlier. Four belonged to Nell and Micah.
  They played them in order on the workroom screen.
  In the first, Micah showed her the orchard before its blossoms opened. He asked whether he should keep a second cup in his room. In the next, Nell described a cabinet she had repaired badly at North Terrace, laughing so hard she could not finish explaining the mistake.

  “I don't want our next beginning to be another thing we put off because we can get through today without it,” he said. “Could we talk when you come?”

  “I thought you wanted the survey charts immediately,” he said.
  “I did want those too.”

Chapter 14
The Trial of the Keeper
Vey's review committee discovered that his office had assigned the fastest departures to technical staff and their families without explaining the rule to everyone else.
  He defended the decision in the theater's upper hall.
  “If skilled crews are lost, more people die later.”
  A woman from the laundry stood. “You listed your brother as an indispensable records technician. He retired.”
  The hall grew quiet.
  Vey looked down at the copy in his hand. He had signed it. Nell watched him search for the sentence that might turn the fact into something else.
  He did not find it.
  “My brother should be reassessed under the same rules,” he said.
  “Everybody should,” the woman answered.
  The committee proposed independent assessment and a public explanation of the criteria. Medical exceptions would remain confidential, with an appeal available to a separate panel. Vey would no longer approve his own household's placement.
  Asa sat beside him after the meeting. Nell heard only part of their conversation as she gathered charts.
  “I thought admitting it would collapse confidence,” Vey said.
  “What did concealing it do?”
  Vey rubbed his forehead.

  A resident asked whether every disaster was a judgment.
  “We cannot inspect God's purposes the way Nell inspects a cracked seam,” Asa replied. “We can inspect what we have done to our neighbors.”

  “Have you ever kept something from me because you thought knowing would make me leave?”
  He looked at her a long time.
  “Yes.”
  The answer entered the space between them with surprising weight.
  “What?”

The decision had carried medical supplies to another district. He had never told her his part because she came back exhausted and grateful to see him, and then the right time kept moving.
  “Was anybody hurt because of it?”
  “The transport review found no avoidable injury from the change. That doesn't settle what I owed you.”
  Nell remembered the night: a floor, cold lights, thinking nobody knew exactly where she was.
  She released his sleeve.
  “I need the records,” she said.
  He nodded.
  She walked down the stairs alone. Behind her, he did not call love after her as a reason she should turn around.
Chapter 15
What the Record Could Say
Nell requested the Low Bell transport record through Elian, not through Micah.
  It took two days to arrive. During those days she worked, slept badly, and discovered how many times she almost turned to tell him something. He moved back to the workmen's dormitory because she asked for space. He left the notebook in her room.
  The record confirmed the decision. Two urgent loads needed one functioning transport. Micah had reported the patients' condition and recommended the route. A dispatcher had authorized it. Nell's work party had been marked as having shelter, which was accurate. Their failed local communication had not reached dispatch until later.
  There was no simple villain in the route change. There was a choice Micah had helped make, and a later silence he had chosen alone.
  She met him in the orchard during the afternoon rest.
  “I might have recommended the same route,” she told him.
  He waited.
  “You let me tell you what that night felt like. You let me say I wished somebody had thought to warn us.”
  “I know.”
  “Don't say you know when what you mean is you're ashamed.”
  He looked away, then back. “I was ashamed. I wanted to remain the person you were relieved to come home to.”
  The honesty did not comfort her. It gave her something solid to answer.
  “You don't get to keep that person by editing what I know.”
  “No.”
  A bee struck Micah's sleeve and moved on. Sabet was arguing with a packing crew beyond the hedge. Nell could hear ordinary life continuing, indifferent to the difficulty of the bench.
  “What else?” she asked.

  Before leaving she asked him to put his account in writing for the transport review. The investigators were still correcting records from the harbor crisis. If his information mattered, it belonged there.
  He agreed.
  That evening he sent the statement, copying Nell with her permission. Then he returned to his dormitory without asking whether the task had earned him his room back.
  Nell read the notebook. Beneath his answer about making himself necessary, she wrote: You can be loved and still have to answer.
  The next morning she brought it to his workbench. He read the line with his hands flat on either side of the page.
  “Would you like to walk after supper?” she asked.
  “Yes.”
  It was an invitation to walk. They both let it be that much.
Chapter 16
A Week of Ordinary Miracles
For seven days nothing happened to separate them.
  Nell distrusted the first two. She waited for a summons during supper, a fracture alarm when Micah reached for her, an urgent departure that would let them postpone the difficult business of being happy. Work continued, but their shifts ended. Other crews took over. The world permitted them an evening and then another.
  They cooked in the shared kitchen. Micah burned onions while explaining that he usually did them differently. Nell cut her thumb on a bread knife she had just called blunt. They ate at the window with a clean bandage between the salt and the butter.
  On the third evening they helped Ilya rehearse an old song for the hospital departures. She stopped whenever Micah attempted the lower harmony.
  “You have other gifts,” she told him.
  Nell laughed until Ilya asked her to demonstrate. The verdict was the same.
  On the fourth, they disagreed about the room. Nell wanted to spread route charts over both beds. Micah had come off a double shift and wanted to sleep. He said so before becoming silently resentful. She found another table before deciding that needing rest was rejection.
  They were absurdly proud of this achievement and did not mention it to anyone.
  The fifth evening brought rain inside the orchard. The misting system had been reset for the remaining trees, and Sabet refused to move the schedule to accommodate romance. Nell and Micah sheltered beneath a maintenance awning, their knees pressed together, while drops collected on the white leaves.
  “I thought love would make me less frightened,” Nell said.
  “Of what?”
  “Losing things.”
  Micah watched water run down the gutter.
  “I didn't know there were so many things I wanted to keep.”
  She turned toward him. His face was familiar enough now that she noticed fatigue in the slight delay before his smile. She knew the scar beside his mouth and still wanted to touch it, as if familiarity could deepen without ever becoming complete.
  He asked before kissing her. She answered by pulling him closer.
  On the sixth evening she invited him back to the room. They moved one bed against the wall and joined the other two narrow mattresses on the floor because neither trusted the frames. Afterward they lay listening to a neighbor tell a child the same story twice. Micah's hand rested open against her shoulder. Nell did not sleep immediately. She remained awake in the quiet, learning that she could want the morning without needing the night to end.
  On the seventh day she woke before him. A blue square of sea-light moved across his cheek. She got up, made tea, and returned with two cups.
  He opened his eyes.
  “What?” he asked, because she was staring.
  “I was checking.”
  “That I'm still here?”
  “That this is allowed to be a day.”
  He took the cup carefully. “What's on the schedule?”
  “Laundry. A municipal argument. Possibly eggs.”
  He smiled into the tea.
  “I'd hate to miss the eggs.”
  She went to open the curtain. The ocean remained above them, imperfectly held. In their room a shirt hung over a chair, two cups stood on the floor, and Micah called her back because she had taken all the blanket with her.
Chapter 17
The Slow Road to Lantern
The first reply from Lantern was mostly noise.
  Nell ran it through the signal filter and recognized a docking identifier, three spoken words, and a burst that might have been laughter. She sent a narrow reply containing their position, a request for medical equipment, and a warning that any old transit schedule needed current confirmation.
  Then she waited six hours.
  Micah slept through the first four on a bench outside the communications room. When she came out, he woke and asked if she had eaten. She gave him half the sandwich she had been saving for him.
  The second reply arrived clear enough to hear.
  Lantern existed. Its population was smaller than the archive suggested. It maintained twenty-four transit cradles, eleven presently serviceable, and a medical team willing to discuss transfers. Its fast access had been destroyed years earlier. The slow service passage remained open, but only to vessels below a strict mass limit. The next usable interval would occur one hundred and twenty-six days after Pelagos's current day zero.
  Nell wrote the date beneath the fast passage's sixty-third day.
  Sixty-three days between the two openings. More than two months to shelter the patients after the main convoy left. A hospital barge might manage the distance if its shielding, fuel, crew, and receiving berths were secured. The word might filled half her page.
  Suri listened to the message three times and began asking questions no navigator could answer.
  “Which cradle models? Do their service logs include the motion tests? Can they support these oxygen connections? Who is on their receiving shift?”
  Nell sent the questions. Suri contacted the local settlements again. Three possible routes began to emerge instead of one impossible promise.
  Vey offered to provide the decommissioned ferry Lark as a stationary hospital. Adra inspected its hull. Micah surveyed its fittings. Its engines could manage local travel but not the longer service passage without major work.
  In the ferry's deserted dining room, Nell found a painted ceiling of swallows returning to a nest. She lay on a bench to look at it while Micah measured doorways.
  “Would you hate living on a boat?” she asked.
  “Depends who keeps repairing it.”
  “Answer properly.”
  He sat on the next bench. “I'd miss a garden.”
  “We could have tubs.”
  “A large tub.”
  “A ridiculous tub.”
  He considered the painted swallows.
  “Is this you asking about waiting for Lantern?”
  She nodded.
  “We don't have to decide tonight,” he said.
  “I know. But I'd like waiting to be something we plan, instead of a thing that happens to us.”
  They inspected the quarters before leaving. One room had a low shelf and space for a narrow workbench. Nell measured the shelf with her hand, then looked away before he could make a joke.
  He did not. He took out the notebook and wrote down its dimensions.
Chapter 18
The Trial Wedding
Ilya proposed a rehearsal because she said she was not wasting her last good singing days on an occasion arranged badly.
  “We haven't arranged an occasion,” Nell pointed out.
  “Exactly my concern.”
  The hospital patients had begun treating Micah and Nell's uncertain engagement as an amateur committee project. Suri intervened after someone put a flower budget on the clinical noticeboard. Ilya apologized for the noticeboard and moved it to her room.
  Micah asked Nell to meet him outside the empty theater after shift. She arrived expecting to help carry benches. He stood on the landing with no tools in his hands.
  “I have a question,” he said.
  “I've noticed.”

  “Yes,” she said when she could trust the word.
  He lowered his head. She touched his hair.
  “Look at me. Yes.”

  “All right.”

Chapter 19
The Lessons of the Roof
Asa spoke in the observatory while evacuation workers carried folded beds past the open door.
  He read the account of friends lowering a man through a roof to reach Jesus. Suri had suggested the passage after the freight-lift debate, then warned him that real patients disliked being treated as symbolic freight.
  Asa described the work in the story and the man at its center. Then he asked who in their own planning had been spoken about without being allowed to speak.
  Ilya raised her hand.
  “I have requested three times that my sister accompany me. You've found room for my ventilator but not the person who knows how to settle me when I panic.”

  “That too.”

Chapter 20
The Country They Could Build
By the thirtieth day, five thousand people had left Pelagos.
  The city grew louder in the places still occupied and strangely soft elsewhere. Footsteps echoed in the closed market. Shops displayed handwritten addresses where their owners could be found after departure. A teacher led her remaining pupils through the orchard, collecting descriptions of trees they might never see again.
  Nell joined a live communication window with Elian. For once the delay was only a few seconds.
  The fast passage convoy had a departure slot. Elian had appointed a second navigator, Jessa, who had already studied Nell's charts. Mercy would come for Nell on the fifty-seventh day. Micah could join the convoy if the hospital transfers were secure.
  “We have alternatives taking shape,” Nell said. “Lantern's slower. Lark needs work. We're not assuming everyone must make the same journey.”
  Elian nodded. “Good. Neither are we.”
  She described people choosing to stay at North Terrace, people choosing the new settlements, two brothers arguing because one remembered their grandmother's home differently. The green mark on the chart was becoming a place full of ordinary complications. Nell liked it more for that.

  “You've built our house at work,” she said.
  “Only the inconvenient bit.”
  He showed her the drainage channel and the removable panels. She inspected everything, then sat on the bench and imagined no patients, no emergency charts, only a rainy afternoon somewhere still undecided.
  Micah sat beside her.
  “We could build again,” he said.
  “Yes.”
  The answer came easily. She waited for fear to correct it and found only the normal anxiety of wanting something real.

Chapter 21
The Orchard beneath the Alarm
The crack reached its warning limit at two in the morning.

  “Those pipes were inspected?” Nell asked.
  “Two years ago. They're being checked now.”

It had to be done in stages, with pumps, ballast adjustment, and constant measurements. The ocean was not something one brave person could hold in place by refusing to leave.

  “I know.”

Chapter 22
The Bridge That Was Gone
The survey drone returned from Vesper on the thirty-sixth day with images of a broken railway.
  A rockfall had struck the ridge during a debris tremor. Two spans had folded toward the ravine. The eastern section containing the freight station was still pressurized, but its main docking lock had lost power. The medical cargo stored there could no longer reach the shuttle station by rail.

  Mercy would leave on the fifty-seventh.
  “We can split the crew,” she said. “Train someone else on the fittings.”
  “We can,” Micah replied. “Rusk is already training them. The first lock cycle still needs somebody who's worked the manual gear.”
  “Somebody.”
  He heard her.
  “Not necessarily me.”
  Vey cleared his throat. The room suddenly contained too many witnesses to a conversation they had not finished having privately.

  “Our date,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “The fifty-second. We're meant to marry.”
  “We still could.”

Chapter 23
The Words beneath the Moon
“I want to finish the lock,” Micah said. “I want to come with you. I don't know how to guarantee both.”
  Nell stared at the chart.
  “I can ask Mercy to delay.”
  “For how long?”
  “Two days.”
  “And if the lock needs three?”
  She hated him for asking the question she had been avoiding.
  Outside the room a child called for a missing shoe. Somebody laughed. Nell wanted the city to understand that something was ending and lower its voice.
  “You could come now,” she said.
  “Rusk needs another fitter trained. We're trying.”
  “You always find a reason only you can do it.”
  His face changed.

  “We shouldn't keep talking like this.”
  “No.”

Chapter 24
The Day They Did Not Marry
They apologized before Micah left.
  Neither tried to turn the apology into agreement about the journey. Nell named what she had said and why it was wrong to use his fear against him. Micah did the same. They stood in their room with the joined mattresses still on the floor and no sensible place to put their hands.
  “Are we still engaged?” he asked.
  She touched the brass star at her throat.
  “I want to be. I can't promise how this ends.”
  “Neither can I.”
  They kissed at the receiving platform. The kiss did not solve the timetable. When the shuttle door closed, Nell stayed until she could no longer see his face.
  The days that followed were full of tasks she performed well. She corrected ballast estimates, moved patients, taught Jessa the approach sequence over a delayed link. Pelagos's population fell below four thousand. Lark received its first ward.
  Micah sent reports from Vesper. The service tunnel was intact. The freight lock's mechanism had corroded around a bearing assembly. Rusk had injured his shoulder. The younger fitters were learning quickly. Nothing in the messages was a coded request that she forgive the distance.
  On the fifty-second day Nell finished her shift at the time they had once chosen for the wedding.
  She went to the hospital. Ilya had saved a packet of sugar from the rehearsal cake and insisted they share it in tea.
  “You can be sad here,” the singer said. “You don't have to improve my afternoon.”
  Nell cried so hard she felt embarrassed, then too tired to be embarrassed. Ilya sat with her and complained about the tea because it really was poor.
  At Vesper, Micah stopped work at the same hour. He washed, changed his shirt, and sat in the train compartment where he had first spoken about marriage. Outside lay the broken dark of the moon.
  He recorded a message.
  “I was going to say this in front of people today,” he began. “I still want the life we wrote down. I want to ask again when we're able to answer without a countdown in the room. You don't owe me a yes because I once heard one.”
  He stopped, listened, deleted a sentence explaining his good intentions, and sent it.
  Nell's message went out that evening.
  “I kept the drawing. I am angry about some things. I'm sorry about others. I loved you today. It was a difficult day to do it.”
  Their messages traveled on separate relay vessels. Neither arrived that night.
  Before sleeping, Nell moved the mattresses back onto their frames. She needed one ordinary task she could complete. In Vesper Station, Micah put the spare blanket over the seat beside him because he could not bear folding it into a cupboard yet.
Chapter 25
The Last Request of the Sea
Pelagos began dismantling its sky on the fifty-third day.
  The great field would never be safe for long-term occupation again. Adra had designed the final transfer sequence to preserve the remaining water in storage and lower reservoirs while emptying the damaged upper vault. Portions of the city would survive as workshops. People would not return to sleep beneath it.
  Residents came to watch from the designated gallery. Nobody called it a celebration.
  Sabet stood beside Nell with a wooden box of cuttings on her knees. Oren's rabbit slept in a repaired cage. Families had brought cameras, songs, a few arguments about whether photographing the departure made the grief worse.
  As the water level lowered, the luminous creatures were guided toward deep refuge tanks by specialists who had worked through three nights to prepare them. One old animal refused the channel until a familiar keeper entered the observation walkway. It followed the movement of her lamp, turning with an unhurried grace that made the crowd fall silent.
  Nell watched it disappear into the dark opening. She thought of Micah carrying a door beneath this impossible sky and was suddenly furious that memories could begin hurting while the place that made them still existed.
  Her communicator sounded.
  Vesper had suffered a pressure loss in the service tunnel. The crew had reached shelter. The first list named seven people. There should have been eight.
  Nell left the gallery before reading the names.
  At communications, the operator replayed the report. Rusk was safe. Adra's assistant was safe. Two trainees had minor injuries. Micah had gone back through a manual hatch to verify the far-side seal after an indicator failed. His return was not confirmed.
  “When was this sent?” Nell asked.
  “Four hours ago.”
  “Contact them.”
  “We're trying.”
  She knew the distance. She knew how relay gaps worked. Knowing gave her nothing useful to do with her hands.
  Suri found her an hour later. Nell had calculated shuttle travel twice. A launch now would arrive after any immediate rescue had succeeded or failed and would take a craft from the hospital transfers. Suri did not tell her to be reasonable. She sat beside her while Nell reached the answer herself.
  At dusk a second report confirmed the crew had secured both pressure sections. It contained no complete names. The operator refused to infer what that meant.
  Nell went back to her room. On the table lay the little house. She unfolded it and noticed for the first time that Micah had drawn one coat on each hook.
  She recorded a message he might never hear.
  “I don't want my last words to you to be about the person I was afraid you were,” she said. “I know who you have been with me. Please come back and let me say the rest badly in person.”
  She sent it, then lay down fully dressed beneath the curtain he had hung. Outside the window, the retreating sea uncovered a sky of pipes.
Chapter 26
The Ship She Had to Board
Mercy arrived on the fifty-seventh day.
  Damas found Nell waiting at the receiving platform with one bag, the route case, and the exhausted expression of somebody who had mistaken every footstep in a corridor for news.
  “Still no complete report?” he asked.
  She shook her head.
  “The rescue crew has independent air. The west shelter's intact. We know that much.”
  “I know.”
  He took the case. “You don't have to sound calm for me.”
  A transport arrived from the hospital with patients destined for the local berths. Suri supervised their transfer personally. Ilya rode in one of the recovered cradles, her sister walking beside it with a bag of clothes. She raised two fingers to Nell through the canopy.
  There were people leaving safely. Nell made herself see them.
  Asa accompanied the last group from the hospital. His next berth was West Orchard, where families had asked him to continue the study gatherings. He stood with Nell while the loading ramp folded.
  “What if I go and he comes back?” she asked.
  “Then he can learn where you went.”
  “What if he doesn't?”
  Asa looked toward the emptied platform. “I don't know how to make that bearable in advance.”
  She was grateful that he did not try.
  Mercy carried the navigation equipment the main convoy needed. Jessa had trained remotely but had never flown the approach under Nell's supervision. If Nell stayed, the convoy could still go with a revised plan. It would have less margin. Six hundred and forty people had arranged their lives around a journey she had helped promise them.
  She could leave a contact route and return later. She could not be everywhere for the moment she feared.
  Nell gave Suri copies of her forwarding instructions. She left another set at the receiving desk. On the door of their room she fixed a sealed note for Micah and asked the remaining building steward to hold it if the block closed.
  Then she boarded Mercy.
  Pelagos receded beyond the stern screen. Its upper sea had become a shallow blue crescent. Empty towers stood beneath it, exposed to work lights. The artificial moon remained stalled above the orchard, a single dim circle in a city dismantling itself.
  Damas set a meal beside Nell's station. She said she was not hungry.
  “Put it where you can see it,” he said. “That worked last time.”
  She ate half an hour later.
  During the first watch, Elian sent confirmation that the convoy was assembling. In the background of the message, someone was trying to persuade a goat into a transport harness. For ten seconds Nell could hear home: familiar irritation, a chair scraping, Anet telling somebody to mind the strap.
  She played those ten seconds twice.
  Then she opened the route chart for Jessa and began explaining the approach markers. Her voice shook at first. Jessa listened, asked useful questions, and did not pretend not to notice.
Chapter 27
The Man in the Unsent Message
Micah was alive on the fifty-seventh day and deeply tired of the shape of the shelter ceiling.
  A beam had pinned his boot when the manual hatch closed. His leg had been fractured below the knee, and his shoulder was badly bruised. A trainee named Sen had reached him by a narrow access chase and spent twenty minutes telling him a story about a disastrous holiday while Adra's assistant cut the obstruction free.
  He remembered very little of the story. He remembered Sen asking whether he had someone who should be told.
  “Nell,” he had said.
  Now he asked again whether the transmitter had been restored.
  Rusk sat beside him with his own shoulder immobilized.
  “Still not working. The spare's on the other side of the broken tunnel.”
  “Visual beacon?”
  “Running the emergency code. The orbital survey makes its pass tomorrow.”
  Micah turned his head against the pillow. The shelter had food, heat, water, and medical supplies. It had eight living people. It lacked the one small piece of machinery that could tell the rest of the worlds their names.
  Sen helped him record a message on a portable unit. Micah tried to begin with reassurance and found himself crying before the second sentence.
  They waited. Sen looked out of the little window until Micah could continue.
  “I'm alive,” he said. “My leg's broken. Everyone here is alive. I don't know when this reaches you. You should take Mercy if the plans are still the same. I want to come after you when I can travel. I'm sorry you're having to wait for this.”
  He stopped before saying he was fine. The fracture hurt, and being unable to stand made him angry at people trying to help.
  That afternoon the crew discussed the freight lock. With the damaged tunnel isolated, the manual gear could be reached from their side. Sen and another trainee had learned enough to finish the bearing replacement if Rusk talked them through it. Micah could inspect fitting drawings from bed.
  The work continued without his hands on it.
  He knew he should find that encouraging. First he found it humiliating. Then, slowly, he found room for both feelings.
  At evening Sen brought the repaired hinge bracket for him to inspect. It was good work. He told her exactly why, and she smiled with such unguarded pleasure that he felt ashamed of his earlier wish to be indispensable.
  Before sleeping he opened the message he'd recorded on the day they had not married. He could no longer remember whether Nell had received it.
  He added another, much shorter.
  “The coat hooks are still where you wanted them in the drawing. I've thought about them an unreasonable amount.”
  He saved it with his first message, set the portable unit beside his bed, and asked for his next dose of pain medicine before the pain became something he would have to pretend to master.
Chapter 28
Twenty Hours of Open Sky
The fast passage opened on the sixty-third day.
  Nell had spent the approach training Jessa in person. The younger navigator knew the machinery, admitted uncertainty promptly, and had a steadier hand than Nell did after too little sleep. They ran the sequence twice in simulation and once through the safe outer marker.
  Elian met them at the convoy assembly point with a signed command plan. The journey no longer depended on Nell remaining at the controls after entry. Jessa would lead under Damas's supervision. A courier called Venture could take Nell back toward the local settlements if she chose.
  That choice came before the good news.
  “I want to return,” Nell told Elian. “I can finish the approach and transfer before the final commitment point. I've checked Venture's fuel with its captain. Suri still needs help planning the slow route.”
  Elian read the revised assignments.
  “Are you expecting me to tell you whether it's love or fear?”
  “I was hoping you might.”
  “I can't. I can tell you the convoy is properly staffed. Your return won't put these families at an avoidable disadvantage. What happens afterward belongs to you.”
  Nell signed the handover.
  Two hours later the survey vessel's report arrived. Vesper's shelter had displayed eight live identification codes through its optical beacon. Micah's was among them. A rescue shuttle had made contact and was transmitting his recorded message.
  Nell listened standing up. At the sound of his voice her knees weakened so abruptly that Jessa brought a chair before she realized she needed one.
  She played his message twice. Then she recorded her reply, saying where she would go, what she knew, what she did not know. She did not promise they would meet on a date she could not verify.
  The passage itself filled the forward screen: huge transit frames aligning around a dark opening pricked with distant light. The agricultural settlements waited beyond, carrying possibilities Nell still wanted. She had not stopped wanting them because she was turning back.
  She watched the first ships pass the inner marker. Their signals reappeared on the far relay. Jessa repeated every necessary confirmation.
  At the transfer point Damas hugged Nell awkwardly around the route case.
  “You can still come later,” he said.
  “I intend to.”
  Elian gave her a paper packet. Inside were letters from North Terrace and a little envelope of calendula seeds.
  “Lio made me count them,” she said. “Apparently seed theft is becoming an administrative problem.”
  Nell laughed, wiped her eyes, and boarded Venture.
  From its window she watched Mercy enter the passage. The other vessels followed over the remaining hours. As the frames moved out of alignment, the opening narrowed until the lights on its far side vanished.
  For the first time there was a closed route between the life she had planned and the place she was going. Venture turned toward the long dark, carrying her back by a choice she had made while there was still time to choose otherwise.
Chapter 29
A Smaller Sky
West Orchard received Lark on the sixty-seventh day.
  The ferry had seventy-two patients aboard. Others had reached permanent local placements, chosen different destinations, or recovered enough to travel normally. The number was smaller than the original list, but every person on it now had a specific berth, a care plan, and somebody assigned to check whether the plan was working.
  Asa met Nell at the dock carrying a box of spoons.
  “They keep traveling to the wrong kitchen,” he explained.
  She hugged him so abruptly that three spoons fell into the box.
  For two days she worked with Suri, Adra, and the ferry crew. The slow route toward Lantern could carry Lark if its detachable cargo frame was removed and the medical loads were distributed correctly. It needed new tow fittings and an escort for the earliest stage. Vesper's crew was finishing both.
  Micah's rescue shuttle had taken him to the ring's functioning outer clinic. The clinic's medical officer would not clear a local return flight until swelling and other risks had been assessed. Micah hated the restriction. He obeyed it.
  Their first nearly live call lasted seven minutes before the link dropped.
  Nell saw his face against a white pillow. There was a bruise along his cheek. He saw her sitting in a borrowed office with a coat that had not dried properly.
  “You're angry,” he said.
  “I was frightened. I'm still trying to sort out which part has changed.”
  He nodded.
  “Does it hurt?”
  “Yes.”
  “Are they treating it?”
  “Yes.”
  She began crying anyway. He did too, which gave her something to laugh at without being cruel.
  “I sent the message,” he said. “About that day.”
  “I haven't got it.”
  “Then I'll ask them to send it again.”
  The link failed while she was telling him the color of the room on Lark. She looked at the frozen screen for a long time.
  West Orchard's reservoir curved above the docks, familiar in shape to the lost sea but smaller, bounded, inspected. People worked beneath it without remembering Pelagos every time they looked up. Nell envied them, then caught herself. Some of these families had their own memories of failing skies.
  That evening Mara brought supper aboard. Ben accompanied her with a paper boat designed, he explained, for waters that stayed where they were put.
  He asked whether Nell and Micah were still getting married.
  “If we both still want to when we can meet,” Nell said.
  Ben frowned. “Do you?”
  “Yes.”
  “Does he?”
  “I think so. We'll ask properly.”
  This satisfied him less than she had hoped. He placed the boat on her workbench and made her promise to keep it dry, a request she could manage without guessing the future.
Chapter 30
The Parts of Him She Could Hear
Micah's delayed proposal reached Lark with a replacement pump assembly.
  Nell listened in the little room beneath the painted swallows. He stood on Vesper's railway platform in the recording, uninjured, wearing the shirt he had chosen for their wedding day. He said she owed him no answer because he had once heard yes.
  She leaned against the workbench. The room's low shelf was exactly as they had measured it.
  The message she had sent on the night she feared he was dead had reached him three days earlier. His reply arrived in the same packet.
  “I wish I could have been there when you heard the next report,” he said. “I keep imagining the hours in between. I don't want to make them smaller by telling you it turned out all right.”
  She replayed that sentence. It was the first time his survival and her fear had been allowed to occupy the same room without either dismissing the other.
  They began sending a message every evening whether a relay was available or not. Each was dated. Each said where the speaker was and where they expected to be next. The practical beginning made space for other things.
  Nell described Mara's soup, Sabet's temporary nursery, the patient who objected to the ferry curtains because they resembled his mother's dressing gown. Micah described learning to move with crutches and the extraordinary hatred he had developed for a particular step outside the clinic.
  He sent a photograph from sixteen. The hair was, as promised, unfortunate.
  She laughed alone in the room until a nurse knocked to ask what had happened. Nell showed her the picture. By the next day three people were asking about the wedding again, and she learned to be more careful with evidence.
  Some messages were difficult. Micah said he had resented Sen for doing his work well. Nell admitted part of her return had been terror, though the work was real and she would choose it again. They did not require pure motives before allowing themselves a good decision.
  Their bodies remained absent from the messages. Nell missed the weight of his arm, the quiet before he fell asleep, the way he moved aside when she needed space without leaving the room altogether. Watching his face on a screen made some evenings lonelier.
  On the eighty-first day he recorded himself reading a chapter from a book Ilya had requested. Nell took the message to her bed. The singer listened with her eyes closed and interrupted twice to criticize his delivery.
  “Tell him he's improving,” she said afterward. “Don't let it reach his head.”
  Nell stayed until Ilya slept. Then she went back to her room and recorded no report at all.
  “I miss you,” she told the little camera. “Today there isn't a clever way to say it. I'd like your hand in mine. I'd like you to be too warm and steal the blanket. I'd like tomorrow morning with you.”
  She sent it without editing the silence at the end.
Chapter 31
The Song Ilya Kept
Ilya died on the eighty-ninth day, before the voyage she had spent so long helping to arrange.
  Her condition had worsened despite treatment. Suri had explained what she knew to Ilya and her sister. There had been choices about comfort, visitors, what she wanted recorded, what she wanted left alone. Nell knew only the parts Ilya invited her to know.
  On the final afternoon the singer asked to hear the ballroom music.
  Nell found the recording. The violin sounded thin through the bedside speaker. Halfway through, a scrape interrupted it: the chair being moved when the water first entered the room. Ilya smiled.
  “You were dancing after that.”
  “On the landing.”
  “I know. I could see you.”
  Nell had thought the moment belonged only to herself and Micah. Learning that someone else had held it quietly made her cry.
  Ilya's sister, Tessa, sat beside the bed with a damp cloth. When the recording ended, she asked whether to play it again. Ilya said no. She wanted the window open a little.
  The room looked toward West Orchard's lower gardens. Ordinary leaves moved in the air from the ventilation shafts. No suspended ocean, no artificial moon. Ilya watched them while Nell held the speaker, unsure where to put it.
  “Go and get your meal,” Tessa said gently. “We'll call if she wants you.”
  Nell went. Ilya died later with her sister there. Nell did not turn her absence into a failure of devotion, though some old part of her tried.
  For the remembrance gathering, Tessa selected a recording of Ilya singing before her injuries. She declined the file made in the hospital because she wanted people to remember her sister at work, commanding a room. Sabet brought cuttings and no speech.
  Asa read the passage Tessa requested, then listened while people told stories that contradicted one another. Apparently Ilya had been both generous and an appalling borrower of clothes. Tessa laughed loudly at that, then covered her face.
  Afterward Nell walked alone along the reservoir rail. She had once imagined that surviving the transport delays would deliver everyone into an ending where the work had been worth it because no one died. Ilya had spent her last months insisting on her sister's place, choosing curtains, teasing a man about his voice. Those months did not disappear because there would be no arrival photograph.
  Micah's next message reached her at dusk.
  He had recorded no explanation. He sat with the camera for a while, then said he remembered Ilya taking a pencil from him to move a switch on his drawing.
  “She knew where she wanted it,” he said. “I liked her.”
  Nell listened in the dark room. For once the distance did not require better words from him. She put the speaker beside her pillow and let the recording finish without restarting it.
Chapter 32
A Love That Could Not Insure Them
Asa's next gathering took place aboard Lark because two patients wanted to attend without another difficult transfer.
  He read Daniel's promise that many who sleep in the dust would awake. Nobody in the room mistook the painted swallows overhead for a sign that they could calculate the day.
  Tessa had asked him to say something about hope without explaining her sister's death.
  He honored the request.
  “I cannot tell you why Ilya died before this journey,” he said. “I can tell you what this passage asks us to hope for. Death does not have the final authority the kingdoms claim for themselves.”
  Nell listened from the doorway. A man near the windows asked whether believing ought to make him less afraid of losing his wife.
  “Has it?” Asa asked.
  “No.”
  The preacher waited instead of correcting him. The man's wife took his hand. After a while they talked about arrangements for her care if he became ill first. The conversation was neither grand nor evasive. It frightened Nell more honestly than a promise of safety would have.
  Afterward she asked Asa whether loving Micah had made her faith worse.
  “I was easier to reassure before,” she said. “Now every hopeful thing sounds as if somebody could take it away while I'm listening.”
  He leaned against the rail, tired from moving chairs.
  “You've begun naming what you hope for.”
  “And what if I don't get it?”
  “I won't tell you it was a lesser hope.”
  She looked down at the brass star resting against her shirt. She had wanted a preacher to tell her how to carry the future without feeling its weight. He kept handing back the future with fewer false assurances around it.
  “Will you marry us if we manage to arrive in the same place?”
  “If you both ask then, yes.”
  “Briefly.”
  “I've written that down.”
  She laughed.
  At the next communication window, Nell asked Micah whether he still wanted marriage. She sat where there was enough light for him to see her face. The delay between them was six seconds each way.
  “Yes,” he answered. “Do you?”
  “Yes. I don't want us to treat arriving as the whole marriage. We'll still be people who did those things and said those things.”
  “I know.” He stopped himself. “I mean I'm willing to keep answering. And I want the good things too.”
  “Which ones?”
  “Your cold feet. Apparently. I was surprised.”
  She laughed, then put her hand flat against the screen. He could not touch it. Six seconds later he laid his hand against his own camera view, at the wrong angle, and they spent half a minute trying to align fingers across a technology that had never been designed for this.
  When the window ended, Nell stayed with her palm against the blank glass until it grew cold.
Chapter 33
The Trial of Lark
Lark failed its first full-load test.
  The failure occurred in local space, with an escort nearby and no patients aboard. A vibration developed in the new tow frame when the engines reached transit power. Adra ordered the run stopped. The crew returned safely to the dock with four pages of useful measurements and a silence that lasted through unloading.
  Nell went to her room and opened a recording from Micah, then closed it before he spoke. She wanted him there so badly that his moving image felt like a deliberate insult.
  She called him during the next scheduled window instead.
  “I need to be unpleasant for five minutes,” she said.
  “About me?”
  “Possibly everyone.”
  “All right.”
  She described the test, the delayed fittings, her fear that she had chosen a road that would use up the years they had meant to share. He listened. When he began suggesting a bracket change, she held up a hand.
  “Not yet.”
  He stopped.
  After five minutes she asked about the bracket.
  He sent a drawing, crediting Sen, who had noticed a discrepancy between the old frame's standard dimensions and the reinforcement Nell's crew had added. Adra checked the idea against the measurements. It explained part of the problem. The rest came from a mounting point worn beyond the tolerance the old specifications allowed.
  They corrected both, repeated the test, and passed.
  Micah watched the recording from Lantern. He had arrived there on a medical transport, having finally been cleared to travel with support. His crutches stood beside his chair. Behind him, a thousand pieces of reflected light moved over a wall.
  “Where are you?” Nell asked when his report arrived.
  He turned the camera.
  Lantern was built inside a ring of derelict solar collectors. Its streets passed beneath layered sails of polished metal, some broad as fields, some patched with mismatched squares. The collectors no longer fed the old engines. Residents used them to guide light into gardens, workrooms, and a great central hall. When the ring turned, its reflections traveled over the city like weather.
  Micah showed her a narrow house near the receiving docks.
  “Temporary,” he said. “But it has two rooms. The shelf's too high. I'm trying not to take that personally.”
  Nell paused the image. The doorway was painted blue. Somebody had left a pot on the step, empty and waiting.
  She checked Lark's timetable. Departure on the one hundred and twelfth day. Service passage on the one hundred and twenty-sixth. Arrival three days afterward if all transfers proceeded normally.
  She wrote the dates in the notebook, then drew the empty pot beside them.
  For the first time in weeks the wait had a shape she could picture without first closing her eyes against fear.
Chapter 34
The Women Who Saw Her Off
Mara came aboard at departure carrying repaired clothing and a bag of bread.
  “People keep giving travelers things they can't wash,” she said. “So I checked.”
  Sabet brought the nursery cuttings that had survived from Pelagos. She was traveling with the later garden shipment, after the receiving beds at Lantern were ready. She gave Nell instructions for the first week and made her repeat them.
  Tavi arrived with Asa's message packet and a small carved swallow. She had made it from a piece of old orchard wood while learning a tool she disliked.
  “Father says the wedding address is still uncertain,” she said.
  “Father is correct.”
  “I'm not making him another itinerary.”
  Nell hugged her. Asa would remain at West Orchard until an agreed relief worker arrived. He had offered to travel for the wedding if possible and had already sent a simple form of service in case it was not. Nell had insisted that a marriage could proceed without arranging an emergency around its preacher.

Near the end she said she hoped Nell would have enough privacy to be happy without feeling obliged to report it to everyone who had worried about her.

Chapter 35
The House He Was Allowed to Need
At Lantern, Micah could walk the length of the blue house without crutches if he moved slowly. His clinician told him this was progress, not permission to climb a ladder. Sen made a sign for the ladder in the shared workshop to settle the matter.
  He hated the sign. He kept it.
  The house belonged to a receiving cooperative. Its rent was reasonable only because he had agreed to repair interior fittings when cleared to work. Nell had approved the arrangement over a recorded exchange containing nine practical questions and one indecently tender sentence that made him sit down halfway through answering.
  He lowered the shelf with Sen's help. He bought two cups that did not match. He placed the pot from the doorstep near the window and read Sabet's instructions until he understood why every third line forbade something.
  Then he discovered he was frightened of Nell arriving.
  Waiting had given him a version of their reunion in which he could say the right thing and she would recognize everything he had meant across the months. Living together again would give her his stiffness, his temper when tired, the bills, the fact that he had not learned to cook onions any better.
  He told her in a message.
  Her reply came two days later.
  “Good. I was worried you expected the woman in the recordings. She washes her face before most of those. You'll be receiving the morning edition.”

Children stepped between them, trying to catch the moving edges. A woman practiced a string instrument near the stairs, stopping often to tune.

He wanted that with a force that made his chest ache. They had spent so long loving across danger that the possibility of a shared errand seemed almost extravagant.
  He recorded the hall for Nell. Then, remembering that she might not receive it before arrival, he wrote directions on paper and left them in the house.
  At the window he placed a little sprig of white leaves from Pelagos. Sabet had sent the cutting in a sealed transport tube. It had rooted badly, then recovered. He did not know whether it would flower under Lantern's changing light.
  He watered it exactly as instructed.
  That evening the passage authority sent notice of unstable conditions along Lark's route. An inspection beacon had failed to respond. Micah forwarded the notice, checked that Nell's ship had received it, and called the transport office to ask what practical help he could provide from the ground.
  He left the ladder where it was.
Chapter 36
The Hall of Unfinished Journeys
Lark reached the service passage on the one hundred and twenty-fifth day and held outside its inspected limit.
  Through the forward windows Nell saw the old transit structure extending across the dark. Unlike the fast passage's paired frames, this one consisted of overlapping rings threaded through a long cylindrical chamber. Transit fields reduced the accelerations a properly shielded vessel would experience inside. Without the fields, the passage was simply a very large machine with too many ways to strike a ship.
  The failed beacon belonged to the third ring. An inspection drone showed its alignment sensor partly obscured by detached shielding. The authority had closed entry until a maintenance tender could reach it.
  Ossa accepted the closure. She called Suri to assess how long the patients could remain aboard. The answer allowed a limited wait, followed by a return to the last confirmed safe dock if no route opened.
  Nell checked the fuel herself. There was enough for that plan. There was no room for improvising a heroic extra day.
  The tender already escorting Lark had a trained exterior crew. They offered to inspect the shielding under remote direction from the authority. Nell supplied the transit drawings she had copied on Vesper. Adra, traveling on the tender, led the work.
  For six hours Lark remained in darkness beside a machine full of old reflected light.
  Nell went through the wards. A patient asked whether the delay meant going back to Pelagos. She showed him the actual alternate dock on the chart. He asked how far it was from his sister. She helped him record a message.
  In her room she listened to Micah's recording of Lantern's hall. Amber light moved over his face as he described where they could meet if the dock was crowded. At the end he looked directly into the camera.
  “I'll be there. If you're late, I'll find a chair.”
  She recorded a reply while the service rings glimmered through her little window.
  “When I reach you, I want us to choose a day,” she said. “Not when all the work is finished. A real day in the middle of the work. Will you marry me, Micah?”
  She paused, smiling through tears.
  “I know I'm late with my own proposal. You've been very patient with the administration.”
  The transmitter acknowledged the packet but could not confirm onward delivery. She saved a copy.
  At hour seven Adra's crew secured the shielding and restored the sensor. The authority ran two empty test cycles and released a revised safe route. Its timing was narrower. Its limits were explicit. Ossa reviewed them with every relevant crew member and accepted the passage.
  Nell stood beside her as the first ring opened.
  Inside lay a corridor of moving light. Old hulls rested in maintenance bays along the walls, the preserved remains of journeys finished long before their own. A sail spread from one abandoned craft like a moth's wing. Further on, gardens had grown under emergency lamps in a sealed service gallery, vines tracing bright green lines across dark steel.
  Lark entered slowly. Nell imagined Micah beneath Lantern's collectors, finding the chair he had promised. For the length of the first ring, she let herself believe he would not have to wait much longer.
Chapter 37
The Place beyond the Map's Edge
The fourth ring lost a section of its external shielding while Lark was inside the third.
  An alarm sounded across the bridge. On the screen the detached panel turned with terrible slowness, scattering bright fragments. Ossa stopped their advance at the designated holding station. The transit authority confirmed damage beyond it and ordered the safe exit route.
  The safe exit led to Sable Anchorage, a maintenance refuge nine days from Lantern by ordinary ferry. It was on Nell's chart in small print, the place she had studied because she hoped never to need it.
  She brought up its current confirmation. Power, air, docking, a medical room with limited capacity. They could reach it inside their fuel margin. The accompanying tender could take the heaviest stores and reduce Lark's load.
  The transfer had to be made before they cleared the last stable ring.
  Ossa called the crews to their assigned stations. There was no shouting. The absence of shouting frightened Nell more than noise would have. Everybody understood.
  She worked at the ballast controls while Adra's crew coupled the tender. A pipe burst in the cargo hold. Two workers isolated it. One hurt her wrist; another took over. Suri sent a nurse to attend her after checking the patients' immediate needs.
  Nell's screen flickered when the damaged ring threw a field disturbance across the corridor. The lights returned. She checked every value against the independent display before touching a control.
  Through the window the whole chamber seemed to be turning around them. The vines in the service gallery shook under artificial wind. Somewhere in the immense structure, parts built for travelers who had forgotten their makers were closing against one another.
  “Ready,” Adra said.
  Ossa moved Lark through the side exit.
  For a moment the ship passed beneath the abandoned sail. Light traveled through its thin material, staining the bridge red, gold, deep blue. Nell saw Micah's hand in memory against the train window. She wanted him with such sudden physical pain that she had to name the next control aloud to remain at her task.
  Then the sail was behind them. Stars returned.
  The tender released its cargo latch. Both vessels cleared the field boundary within the allowed interval. On the bridge nobody celebrated until Ossa confirmed the full passenger and crew count.
  All present. One minor injury. Systems stable. Route to Sable engaged.
  Nell sat back. Her legs trembled beneath the console.
  They sent a short message through the working emergency channel: ship, destination, persons aboard, medical status, estimated arrival. Personal messages would wait for a less constrained relay.
  At Lantern, Micah read the report with Sen beside him. He checked Sable's location, then went to the receiving office. He asked them to contact the refuge, arrange care, and quote the earliest safe ferry.
  A clerk suggested he stay near his communicator.
  “I will,” he said. “Can I help with the manifests while I'm waiting?”
  By midnight he had proofread every name twice. Nell's was there, spelled correctly, on a ship that had come through the broken light and was still traveling toward him.
Chapter 38
Tell Me Again
Sable Anchorage occupied the hollow center of an old engine bell.
  Its docks had been built along the inner curve. Small dwellings clung to terraces once designed for machinery, and flowering vines grew where heat exchangers had stood. Beyond the open end of the bell, a distant band of charged dust shone beneath work lights, green and violet against the stars.
  Lark arrived on the one hundred and thirty-eighth day. The refuge team had prepared food, beds, medical power, and enough hot water for people who had been traveling too long.
  Nell helped complete the handover before opening her personal messages.
  Micah was coming on the scheduled ferry. It would arrive the following morning. His clinician had cleared the ordinary flight and insisted on mobility support at the dock. He had accepted the chair without negotiation. In his message he sounded proud of this, which made Nell laugh and cry into the same breath.
  She slept badly. At dawn she washed, changed, and brushed her hair twice before deciding she looked like someone trying too hard not to look as if she were trying. She wore her ordinary coat. The brass star rested beneath it against her skin.
  The ferry approached under the bell's rim. Its hull caught the green light of the dust and turned briefly luminous. Nell stood behind the safety line while the dock crew secured it.
  She saw passengers appear in the door. A man with three bags. A woman supporting a sleepy child. Sen, carrying tools and waving so hard she nearly dropped them.
  Then Micah.
  He sat in a wheeled chair, one leg extended. His coat lay folded across his lap. He had lost weight. There was a new hesitation in the way he moved his right shoulder. He looked around the dock, saw her, and stopped trying to speak before the distance allowed it.
  Nell went to him when the crew opened the barrier.
  For a moment they could only look. The face she knew from screens had depth, warmth, a tiny nick where he had shaved badly. She touched his cheek. His hand covered hers.
  “I had a speech,” he said.
  “I don't want it yet.”
  “All right.”
  She bent until their foreheads touched. The dock carried on around them. Someone rolled a crate past. Sen gently redirected the people trying to walk through the space they occupied.
  “Why didn't you answer my last message?” Nell asked.
  “The one about the rings? I got the emergency report. Nothing after.”
  She shook her head, smiling now, unable to keep the tears from falling onto his collar.
  He lifted his hand to her face.
  “Tell me again.”
  She knelt so neither had to look up.
  “Will you marry me?”
  “Yes,” he said. Then, because she was crying too hard to hear properly, “Yes, Nell.”
  She kissed him. His arms came around her carefully at first, then held. She felt the solid resistance of his body, the warmth beneath his shirt, the unsteady breath he let out against her neck. For months she had wanted a message to make the distance end. Now there was no distance available inside the space between their hands.
  When they finally moved, Sen had acquired two cups of tea.
  “They're terrible,” she said. “I thought you'd want to begin realistically.”
  Nell laughed against Micah's shoulder. He kissed her hair. Beyond the engine bell, the green dust drifted past the stars, while the dock master asked them kindly to move the chair so the next passengers could come ashore.
Chapter 39
Under the Borrowed Constellations
They married seventeen days later at Lantern.
  The intervening days mattered. They traveled the ordinary ferry route together. Nell attended Micah's medical appointment with his permission and learned which help he wanted. He listened while she described the transit failure without interrupting to say he knew she would manage. They slept in the blue house, argued about a draft, bought food, and once sat in silence because being near again did not immediately teach them how to speak about every lonely night.
  On the fifth evening they chose the date. On the sixth they told people. Asa could come on a scheduled vessel, with his responsibilities covered. Tavi chose to accompany him and made clear she was coming for the wedding.
  Elian sent a recorded greeting from the agricultural settlements. The camera showed a porch still missing its roof and a table already overcrowded with seedlings. Anet sent a letter. Mara and Ben sent a paper boat with a sail made from a clean bit of the old food-distribution ledger. Sabet arrived with the garden shipment and said she was attending only to supervise the plants.
  The wedding took place in Lantern's central hall beneath the great collectors. Residents called their changing reflections constellations, though each moving point came from a known mirror above. Micah and Nell stood in a broad band of late gold while the light shifted across their hands.
  Micah used a cane. Nell wore a dark blue dress borrowed from Tessa and altered with her permission. In her hair she had one sprig from the cutting that had survived Pelagos. Its leaves were green beneath the protective white coating.
  Tessa played Ilya's chosen recording before the service. Nobody attempted to imagine the speech the singer might have given. They let her voice be there, then allowed the room to become quiet.
  Asa spoke briefly, as requested.
  He read that charity was kind, and said kindness would need a household in which to live. He made no claim that their survival proved they had been favored over those who died. Their promises concerned the life they were choosing now, and the responsibilities they would continue to carry when feeling alone did not make them easy.
  Then he stepped aside.
  Micah unfolded a sheet he had rewritten so often that one crease had split.
  “I will tell you what I know, including what I wish you didn't have to know about me,” he said. “I will ask before making a promise that spends your life. I will make room for your work and tell you when I need room for mine. I want your happiness without requiring it to excuse me. I want the ordinary days we kept describing. I choose you for them.”
  Nell had written her words in their notebook.
  “I will tell you when I want to stay,” she said, looking at him. “I will not make you guess love from the work I do around it. I will speak to the man in front of me when I'm frightened, and stop when fear makes me cruel. I want to know you beyond the reasons people need you. I want to be known beyond the things I can survive. I choose a home with you, and the work of making it a place we can both live.”
  They exchanged simple rings made by a cooperative metalworker. Micah's brass star remained at Nell's throat.
  As they kissed, the collectors turned. Gold moved away across the floor and a cooler silver light entered. People began clapping before Asa had finished the sentence that made the marriage official. He waited, smiling, and said it again.
  There was food afterward. Oren, newly arrived with a relative on the garden transport, helped Ben's recorded boat navigate a bowl while Sabet objected to the proximity of water to her cuttings. Sen danced with three people in succession. Micah danced once, slowly, with Nell, and stopped when his leg began to hurt.
  They sat together at the edge of the hall. Nell rested her head against his shoulder. Across the floor their friends carried dishes, corrected a child, argued about a tune. The celebration was already becoming a shared evening rather than an occasion everyone had to preserve perfectly.
  “Would you do it again?” Micah asked.
  “The whole journey?”
  “The wedding.”
  She kissed his knuckles. “Ask me after you've helped wash up.”
  He laughed. When the first dishes came back, he took the cloth she offered and made a place for her beside him.
Chapter 40
The Morning Edition
A month after the wedding, Nell accepted a survey assignment that would take twelve days.
  She told Micah at breakfast. He was cutting bread at the little workbench because the table was covered in parts for the porch window. Their porch was still a promise, but the window had achieved the more demanding status of an object in the way.
  “When?” he asked.
  “Next month. I haven't signed yet.”
  He put down the knife.
  For half a minute she saw the old paths available to them. She could explain too much, sounding defensive before he objected. He could say whatever you want and make the disappointment her private work to discover later.
  “I'll miss you,” he said. “I also wanted us to visit Suri that week.”
  “We can ask whether the week after works.”
  “I'd like that.”
  She reached for the schedule. They moved a date, checked transport, and finished breakfast. It did not become the argument she had prepared herself to survive.
  Outside the window, Lantern's collectors cast pale strips of morning along the street. The pot on their step held calendula seedlings from Lio's envelope. Only three had germinated. Sabet said three was enough if Nell stopped standing over them as though anxiety improved light.
  Micah's leg was healing. Some nights it ached. Nell had learned to ask whether he wanted company or quiet. He had learned to answer before pain made every offer sound like interference.
  The blue house contained two unmatched cups, more tools than either thought belonged indoors, and a drawer they still had not decided how to divide. Their notebook lay on the shelf Micah had lowered. Some pages held questions; others contained shopping lists. A pressed white blossom remained between the drawings of the house.
  They kept the messages. Neither wanted to play them often, but they had copied them to a second drive. Nell's proposal had finally arrived from the damaged transit relay two weeks after the wedding. They watched it together. She cringed at the joke about administration. Micah said it had been the part he liked best.
  News arrived from the other settlements. Elian's porch had a roof. Anet had visited Iven once and was deciding what, if anything, should follow. Tavi had finished her apprenticeship review and begun a paid position. Mara's son needed larger shoes. Pelagos's remaining workshop crews had stabilized the emptied vault and were documenting what could be used again. There would be no sea above the orchard. The preserved creatures were living in their refuge tanks under the care of people who knew their feeding times.
  Suri wrote that three former patients had asked to help design the next transport ward. Tessa had joined a local choir and wanted Nell to stop asking whether she was ready before sending an invitation.
  Life had made room for grief without leaving every chair turned toward it.
  That morning Micah discovered he had burned the onions again. The smell reached Nell while she was packing her instruments for a local inspection. She went to the kitchen and found him standing over the pan with an expression of personally betrayed dignity.
  “An experiment?” she asked.
  “A developing method.”
  She kissed him, laughed into his mouth, and opened the window.
  Cool air entered. Somewhere in the street a vendor called out the price of fruit. A child complained that the reflections on the pavement would not stay still long enough to jump over. Nell stood behind Micah and put her arms around him, resting her cheek between his shoulder blades.
  “I have to go in ten minutes,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “I'll be back for supper.”
  He turned in her arms.
  “What would you like?”
  She glanced at the pan. “Perhaps we could begin with bread.”
  They ate standing up, passing the butter between them. When she left, he walked with her to the blue door. She kissed him once more because she wanted to, then went down the street carrying the day's work.
  Micah watched until she turned the corner. Then he went inside, washed the pan, and took the window frame out into the shared workshop where there was room to finish it properly.
  On the step, three seedlings lifted their new leaves into the changing light. By evening one had opened a bud. Nell saw it when she came home and called Micah to the door.
  They crouched beside the pot together.
  The flower was small enough to cover with a thumb. Neither touched it. After a while they went in to make supper, leaving the door open to the mild air and the sound of their neighbors coming home.

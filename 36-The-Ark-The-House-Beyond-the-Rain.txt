Chapter 1
The Dry Room
The rain began inside the cupboard at six fourteen on a Wednesday morning.
  Lazara was looking for the blue mug. Elias had taken the good one to the workshop, forgetting it would be difficult to drink from while repairing a cupboard hinge two rooms away. She opened the door above the sink and heard water striking china.
  Nothing fell onto her hand.
  Drops descended between the stacked bowls, pattered across a saucer, and vanished before reaching the shelf. The wood was dry. A thin gray sky occupied the space behind the cups, where yesterday there had been the back of a kitchen cupboard.
  She closed the door.
  Opened it.
  Rain.
  "Elias."
  Something in her voice brought him without the screwdriver. He looked past her shoulder. For several seconds neither said anything.
  Outside the window, their pear trees stood in the cold morning light. A delivery truck turned at the corner. Ordinary tires hissed on ordinary damp pavement.
  Inside the cupboard a woman was running.
  She crossed between the teacups as a distant figure crosses a field seen through a train window. Dark coat. One sleeve torn at the elbow. Something rectangular held beneath her coat. She slipped, caught herself, and turned toward them.
  The rain passed through her face.
  Not through flesh. Through the image of flesh, which thinned as though projected on a sheet of falling water. Her mouth moved. Lazara leaned closer without crossing the plane of the cupboard door.
  "Nima," the woman said.
  Her voice came from behind the dishes. Quiet, perfectly clear.
  "I'm Nima Pell. Tell the Court I'm here."
  Elias took Lazara's wrist. Not pulling her away. Giving her something current to feel.
  "Where is here?" Lazara asked.
  The woman looked behind her. A white shape stood in the distance, so tall its upper edge disappeared into the gray. It might have been a wall. It might have been a waterfall moving upward.
  "North Desk. Twenty-four. Don't let him finish the page."
  A wooden door swung across the rain.
  The cupboard contained bowls.
  One pale blue cup was missing. In its place lay a folded strip of white paper, wet at the corners.
  Lazara left it where it was.
  Elias went to check Alex. The boy slept on his side, one hand underneath his cheek. The cat raised its head from the rug, considered the adults' behavior unworthy of assistance, and went back to sleep.
  They closed the kitchen door before returning to the cupboard.
  "Phone?" Elias asked.
  "Photograph from outside. No reaching through."
  The words were familiar enough to steady them, but she disliked how readily terror could borrow the shape of experience. Knowing what had happened once did not tell them what was happening now.
  He photographed the shelf. The paper appeared in the image. So did the gap where the cup had stood. They recorded the time and watched for ten minutes. No more rain. No movement. The kitchen clock agreed with both phones and with Lazara's watch.
  She found a wooden cooking spoon and moved the paper onto a dry plate without putting her hand inside the cupboard. It was ordinary paper now, if ordinary could still mean something brought through a vanished sky.
  On one side, in neat dark writing, were three sentences.
  At six fourteen, Lazara Moreau opens the cupboard.
  Elias Hart comes when she calls.
  At six twenty-seven, she reads what follows.
  Her phone displayed 6:27.
  "Don't turn it over," Elias said.
  She stopped. Her fingers had already moved toward the edge.
  "You're right."
  They put a clean bowl over the paper. Not a containment device, just something to prevent curiosity pretending to be an accident. He wrote DO NOT TURN on a piece of masking tape and stuck it beside the bowl.
  The kettle clicked off. Neither remembered putting it on.
  That was because Alex had.
  He stood in the doorway in his pajamas, holding the cat beneath its front legs. "You both forgot breakfast."
  Lazara turned toward him too quickly. He looked from her face to his father's.
  "Is it the boat?"
  Elias put down the phone. "Something happened in the cupboard. We don't know what it means yet. You're safe here with us. We're going to ask people to help us understand it."
  Alex let the cat down. It departed with offended dignity.
  "Do I have to draw it?"
  The question hurt Lazara more than the paper.
  "No," she said. "You don't have to do any work about this."
  Elias pulled out the chair beside him. The boy climbed onto it. They made toast. Lazara took the red mug instead of the blue one. Her hands shook enough that Elias moved the hot kettle away without making a remark.
  After Alex left for school with Mrs. Bell, who lived nearby now and sometimes helped on early workdays, Lazara called Miriam.
  "I'm coming," Miriam said after hearing the account. "Don't send me a photograph of the reverse. Don't send it to anybody."
  "We haven't looked."
  "Good."
  "Miriam. What's a North Desk?"
  Silence on the line.
  "There's a message from the Court," Miriam said. "It came last night. I didn't want to wake you until I had both copies. A courier is missing."
  Lazara sat down.
  "Nima Pell."
  "Yes. How did you know?"
  From underneath the bowl came the soft unmistakable sound of a pencil continuing across paper.
  

Chapter 2
A Voice Without a House
Miriam arrived with breakfast she had forgotten to eat and a cardboard document box. Tarin followed twenty minutes later, carrying two overnight bags and wearing an expression that suggested the drive had been too long for the things she had been thinking.
  Sam had stayed in Alaska. He had work enough helping the recovered households build a present that did not depend on anyone else's catastrophe. His hand remained stiff. He could write half a page before it cramped, which made the sound under the bowl particularly cruel when Tarin heard it.
  "There's no pencil in there?"
  Elias lifted the bowl. The strip lay exactly where they had left it. Its reverse remained down. A faint dark line had reached the edge, as though writing on the underside had begun to bleed through.
  Miriam photographed it with a scale placed on the table, not touching the sheet. She did not call it a prophecy. Lazara was grateful.
  "It's information arriving in a medium," Miriam said. "We haven't established which direction anything moved."
  "It knew the cupboard," Tarin said.
  "It described the cupboard. That isn't the same observation."
  They brought out the Court's messages. Since the shared storm reference had been isolated, the local relays operated on their own verified schedules. Sef at the channel house had signed both copies. Mara had confirmed them. No damaged route had reopened through the old Ark domain.
  Nima Pell, twenty-three, apprentice route recorder. Last verified arrival: Northern Exterior Service House. Purpose: delivery of copied maintenance records to the North Registry. Last message: ordinary acknowledgment, then a request to check a return plate whose lettering had changed.
  That was thirty-one local hours earlier.
  "Pell," Lazara said. "Iona's family?"
  Tarin shook her head. "Maybe an old branch. Common name in the Court. We shouldn't assume."
  Miriam spread an address diagram across the table. The North Registry connected to the pyramid installation but had not been part of the six storm-dependent domains. Its returns were dormant, independently sealed. At least, that was what the old atlas had recorded.
  "Dormant doesn't mean vacant," Elias said.
  He remembered a bench and six hundred eleven tabs. Remembered the relief of finishing a count, and how dangerous relief could become if it convinced a tired person every count was finished forever.
  On the table the paper moved.
  Not far. It curled at one end, exposing a triangle of the reverse.
  Miriam put the box lid over it before anyone could read.
  "We need a decision," Tarin said. "Not a ritual of almost looking."
  Lazara went to the window. Beyond the pear trees, frost silvered the fence. She had a contract to finish, a meeting that evening, and a coffee with Claire in six weeks that had become an anchor around which she arranged the future. She did not want every strange occurrence to take her life away from her and rename the theft a calling.
  "We can ask the Court to confirm Nima," she said. "Ask about the place and the message. We can go to the site with a defined search. That doesn't mean agreeing to whatever the paper says."
  Elias nodded. "I need Alex's care settled before I leave. He gets a say in the arrangements. He doesn't get responsibility for deciding whether we help."
  "And you two don't get to decide all the risk privately because you happen to open the garden," Tarin said.
  Lazara turned. "Agreed."
  It was harder than the first agreement had been. Then they had entered an unknown place. Now they had a home to leave and enough knowledge to imagine failing to return.
  They called the Court through the authenticated relay service Voss's replacement had reluctantly maintained. Asterion no longer controlled the inner routes. A small independent stewardship group, including residents and outside professionals, held access responsibilities jointly. The bureaucracy was tedious. Lazara had come to like its deliberate insistence that a person could not simply own every place a doorway reached.
  Sef answered after two exchanges of source and receipt marks.
  "Nima has a green coat," he said. "She tore the right elbow helping repair the west stair. Her mother's name is Dessa. She carries a ledger with a yellow cloth cover."
  The image's coat had been dark green. Its torn sleeve had been right. The rectangle beneath it could have been a ledger.
  They sent only what they had seen, not the unexamined paper. Sef repeated Nima's exact message.
  "Twenty-four?"
  "That's what she said."
  The line remained silent for so long Elias checked the connection indicator.
  "There are twenty-four floors in the North House," Sef said. "The last one has no stair."
  "How do you reach it?"
  "You don't. The old record calls it the author's floor."
  The paper beneath the lid made another little scratching sound.
  Miriam stood up.
  "We take the sheet in a closed opaque envelope. We don't burn it. We don't obey it. We find out who is writing."
  Tarin looked at the box. "And if there's nobody?"
  Lazara thought of the woman running through rain no more than a cupboard deep.
  "Then we find Nima first."
  

Chapter 3
Promises Kept
Elias told Alex about the journey that afternoon, before packing. Lazara stayed in the kitchen, close enough to be called, far enough not to make the boy feel outnumbered.
  His father described the missing courier, the return to Alaska, the people going with them. He did not say it would be easy. He gave the boy the actual care arrangements: school as usual, Mrs. Bell for the first nights, Ruth's sister Joanna coming on Friday, calls at agreed times if the outside connection allowed them.
  Alex asked whether the garden had opened again.
  "Not the one we closed. We won't reopen it just because something is calling."
  "But you are going."
  "Yes. Someone has been reported missing, and we may be able to help the search."
  The boy pressed his lips together. He had grown since summer. His sleeves did not quite reach his wrists. Elias saw those bare wrists and remembered his own too-short work coat in the holding room, the ridiculous recognition giving him something to hold while he waited.
  "How long?" Alex asked.
  "We review after three days at the site. If we need longer, I call and explain what we know. I don't leave you guessing."
  Alex nodded. Then said, "I don't want you to tell me I'm brave about it."
  Elias leaned back in his chair.
  "All right."
  "I'm not. I'm mad."
  "You're allowed."
  "I was allowed last time too."
  There was no defense that would not ask the boy to make his father's choice feel easier. Elias stayed with the anger. They talked for nearly an hour. At the end Alex asked whether Lazara was going because his father was, or whether she would have gone anyway.
  "You can ask her."
  Lazara came when Alex called. He asked directly. She sat in the chair he indicated.
  "I want to help the missing woman," she said. "I also want to go with your dad because we work well together. Those are both true."
  "What if one of you gets stuck again?"
  "We have more people checking the work this time. We have rules about when to stop. I can't promise nothing frightening will happen. I can promise that we won't treat being frightened as a reason to stop thinking."
  Alex looked at her for a long moment. "I don't want to draw maps for it."
  "You don't have to."
  He went upstairs. Later he left a note beside the packed bags: Call when you say.
  Elias put it inside the pocket of his outside coat, where it would stay aboveground.
  Lazara called Claire before dinner. It would have been easier to wait until the trip was over and present the whole thing as an ordinary survey that had worked out. She knew that ease. It had a familiar wrong taste.
  "I'm going north for work," she said. "A search connected to the site I told you about. I still intend to be at coffee. If anything changes, I tell you before you have to ask."
  Claire was quiet.
  "You don't need my permission, Mom."
  "I know. I wanted you to have the information."
  "Is this going to be one of those things where I have to wait by the phone?"
  "No. There will be an outside contact. I'm not making you that person."
  The relief on the other end was small but audible. They spoke briefly about Claire's course and the dog still getting into the recycling. Lazara let the ordinary subject finish the call.
  That night she and Elias packed separately in the same room. Cotton work clothes. Paper copies. Personal supplies. The old wooden peg, wrapped in a cloth, went into a box marked REFERENCE OBJECT, NOT A KEY.
  He watched her write the label.
  "Do you need to bring it?"
  "I don't know. Sam asked for it to be available. We won't use it without knowing why."
  He nodded. Then took it out and put it beside his bag rather than buried inside hers.
  "Shared decisions," he said.
  She almost objected. Not because he was wrong, but because being corrected by someone she loved could still wake an old instinct to prove she had not needed correcting.
  She let the instinct pass.
  "Thank you."
  They lay awake later, the house dark around them. The thought of rain inside a cupboard was worse in darkness. Outside, a loose gate clicked in the wind with the patience of a person trying one latch repeatedly.
  "I don't want to lose this," Elias said.
  She turned toward him. "The house?"
  "The part where a difficult day is just a difficult day. Where you're irritated because I forgot the milk."
  "I can still be irritated about milk in Alaska."
  He smiled, then grew serious. "You know what I mean."
  She did. She put her hand flat against his chest and felt his heart moving without any system agreeing to it. It was an ordinary miracle she had no interest in explaining away.
  "We go to help," she said. "We don't go because the unknown deserves more of us than the people here do."
  He covered her hand with his.
  Downstairs, in the closed document box, the paper stopped writing.
  Neither heard it.
  On its reverse a sentence remained unfinished after the word because.
  

Chapter 4
Four Pyramids
The installation looked smaller from the air because winter had hidden its boundaries.
  Snow softened the damaged pyramid into an almost ordinary rise. Spruce crowded the southern approach. The shaft shelters, fuel stores, and new access fencing might have belonged to a remote research station anywhere in Alaska. Only from a low turn over the eastern ridge did the other three shapes become visible.
  Four pyramids around a buried court. One broken, one almost entirely concealed, two connected by a line so straight that snow lay differently along it. Lazara followed the line with her eyes until the helicopter turned and the view disappeared.
  "You didn't see the others in June?" Miriam asked over the headset.
  "We saw possible foundations. Different light. Less snow in some places, more vegetation."
  She disliked how explanations accumulated when a place seemed to enjoy being discovered differently each time.
  Sam met them inside the access shelter. He hugged Tarin with his good arm and held her longer than she pretended to want. His beard had grown untidy. A red wool scarf made him look like someone dressed by a committee of worried neighbors.
  The shelter had become a small crowded village since summer. Two families from the carrier lived in prefabricated rooms at the edge of the site while their outside papers were resolved. A carpenter from the Court repaired benches with tools he was still learning to name in English. Children had taped drawings to a wall marked EMERGENCY NOTICES, and someone had moved the notices rather than take the drawings down.
  Tarin stopped at one of them. A little black cat beneath an enormous arch. She recognized the animal before she recognized the Court as drawn by someone small enough to have considered the cat its most important feature.
  Sam watched her. "They're doing all right. Different days, different kinds of all right."
  "And you?"
  He opened his damaged hand. "Tied a proper knot this morning. Took me ten minutes. Nobody applauded. Excellent people."
  She smiled and kissed his cheek.
  Beth Arlen came through the adjoining workroom carrying a tray of printed components, none related to the Ark at first glance. Replacement knobs. A housing for an outside water sensor. A child-sized clip for a cracked pair of glasses. Practical evidence of how a remote station actually used its clever equipment.
  "You must be the new arrivals," she said. "Your bags are by the heater. Don't put anything against the east wall. It sweats when the wind turns."
  She led them through a lab whose windows faced the four pyramid shapes. On the benches stood computers, surveying instruments, half-finished models, and a collection of rejected supports whose failures had been labeled in large handwriting. The modern room looked reassuringly cluttered. It had no reason to pretend every experiment had succeeded.
  Outside the window, the broken pyramid's north edge cast a shadow on the snow. The other three contributed dark triangles that overlapped in the central court. Elias noticed that their shared dark center remained nearly square despite the different shapes of the surviving structures.
  "A survey reference," Beth said, following his gaze. "Or an architectural coincidence. We've measured it. We haven't decided which story to make the measurements tell."
  Lazara liked her at once.
  They went outside while the access crew checked the lower air. Wind moved fine snow across the apron. From ground level the buried structures were less impressive and more troubling: corners that disappeared beneath ordinary trees, a surviving casing course high enough to block the light, a narrow vertical slit from which warm air breathed into winter.
  Sam showed Tarin the old survey truck's position. Not the actual vehicle; it had long since been removed. Just a place beside a stone where she had stood twenty-eight years earlier believing her father would come back in a minute.
  She remained there longer than anybody expected. Then took his good hand and walked him away from it.
  Lazara and Elias followed at a distance. A remembered place could be left without being denied. The thought stayed with her as they went below.
  "You're late," he said.
  "Plane was late. I was punctual inside it."
  He laughed and took her bag. She took it back because of his hand. The negotiation had become familiar enough to be tender.
  Niles still maintained the pumps. He showed Lazara a clean level log before she asked for it. The water inflow had stabilized. The main access stairs had a second escape path. She thanked him, and he looked pleased that she remembered why those things mattered.
  Down on the quay, the Ark waited beneath work lights.
  Dark timber. Curved sides. Roof high enough to make the ordinary people below seem briefly like drawings of themselves. The vessel's measured exterior remained unchanged. No snow reached it. No rain. Its side door was closed.
  Lazara put her palm on the sill.
  She had expected a return of dread. Instead she felt grief for the garden's lost afternoon and embarrassment at feeling it. She had a living man beside her, a daughter willing to meet for coffee, a life outside. Wanting an impossible place did not mean she had failed to appreciate those things.
  Elias knew her well enough now to wait before speaking.
  "Still four hundred fifty," he said eventually.
  "You checked?"
  "Niles did. Then I asked for the numbers rather than just the conclusion."
  She smiled at him. "You're a terrible person to reassure."
  "I learned from a specialist."
  They walked around the stern-side supports with Sam. An exterior service passage, previously blocked by unstable masonry, had been opened under the stewardship group's authority. It led away from the dock into a gallery that did not enter the Ark. Metal tools worked normally there. Their phones kept signal through a modern repeater.
  The distinction mattered. They were not reopening the old storm network. They were entering a separate part of the installation by an ordinary physical route.
  After three hundred yards the passage ended in a hall shaped like a long square well. Twenty-three visible levels climbed its sides. Timber balconies projected above one another. Stair towers stood at opposite corners. In the center, a hollow wooden column rose beyond the highest visible gallery.
  "North House," Sam said.
  Elias looked up until his neck hurt.
  Each level had doors, work bays, broad mechanical housings, and small sitting places beside the stair. The building was no empty vertical shell. Someone had fitted a town's worth of tasks into its perimeter: water treatment on the lowest levels, timing galleries above, growing courts, accommodation, survey rooms, storage, and an upper control floor facing four separate pyramid addresses.
  Twenty-four stories. The last was concealed within the crown above the visible balconies.
  On the lowest level stood a long wooden model of the Ark. Not a toy. Large enough for a person to walk beside its open interior and examine the three physical hull floors. A separate attached model showed the six-deck carrier. Cotton lines ran from the models into the central column.
  "Remote-control reference building," Elias said, reading the installed label as it clarified beneath his gaze. "A standing version of the long structure."
  Lazara pictured the carrier laid lengthwise beside this vertical house, each preserving a different arrangement of the same network addresses. Not secretly the same building. Two structures communicating through registered correspondences.
  On the far wall, beyond the model, hung a board of current occupants.
  Most tabs were blank.
  One read NIMA PELL, FLOOR 24, UNRECEIVED.
  Below it, in fresh dark lettering, another tab turned outward.
  LAZARA MOREAU, FLOOR 1, EXPECTED.
  There was no tab for Elias.
  

Chapter 5
The Twenty Fourth Floor
They did not have to climb twenty-three stories to find the first impossible part of the house.
  It stood beside the stair on the ground floor: a little room containing a coat peg, a chair, and a window showing the back of Elias's Spokane house.
  The view was wrong by only a few inches.
  The fence leaned more than it should. A branch from the neighbor's maple crossed the upper corner where no branch was visible from the real window. Someone had reconstructed a remembered place rather than opened a reliable view into it.
  Elias stood outside the room and felt the certainty of recognition begin doing dangerous work inside him.
  "That's not home," he said aloud.
  Lazara studied the fence. "No."
  She saw relief pass through his body. It was a strange intimacy, helping someone decline a place that knew how to look like what he loved.
  Miriam found the room's label. PERSONAL ORIENTATION. It had once been a way to help arriving visitors recover their sense of direction. The device borrowed familiar spatial relationships from their registered histories. It was not designed to deceive. Here, in a nearly empty building, a useful courtesy had become an unsettling imitation.
  They moved on.
  The first fourteen floors were accessible by ordinary stairs. Sam had surveyed the lower landings. He had not found Nima's route, but he had found a safe place to pause on every second level, water on six, and a maintained outside reference line that kept the clocks consistent.
  On seven, the phones stopped working. A resin boundary crossed the whole landing. They left their metal in a locked exterior cabinet and changed into the cotton work clothes brought from below. No one tested whether a familiar rule would make an exception for urgency.
  By twelve, the building no longer sounded empty. Footsteps crossed upper galleries. A cart rattled behind a closed door. Someone coughed with the irritated wet cough of a person working in cold air.
  Tarin called out. The footsteps stopped.
  On thirteen, they found a table laid for four. The cups were warm. Lazara thought of Anet, then noticed the important difference. No kitchen source. No receipt mark. Just a place arranged to look as though someone had expected them.
  "We eat what we carried," she said.
  They sat on the floor nearby, backs against the wall, and divided their own food. Elias took the sandwich with too much mustard, correctly identifying it as his mistake from packing. Lazara gave him half of hers. Their ordinary lunch occupied the space beside the false invitation like a modest refusal.
  At fourteen, the stair ended.
  The landing above could be seen through an open shaft, with additional stairs continuing beyond it. Between the two was an absence where a flight should have been. No broken ends. No collapse. The builders had deliberately left the connection unmade.
  A narrow human door stood in the side wall.
  Its plate read NORTH DESK, RECEIVING FLOOR 24. VISITOR ROUTE AVAILABLE WHEN PURPOSE IS DECLARED.
  "Purpose?" Tarin said. "Find Nima."
  Nothing happened.
  Miriam tried the courier's complete name, her departure point, the reported distress message. The door remained solid.
  Elias looked at the wording. "It says declared. Not justified."
  He stood beside Lazara.
  "We came to find Nima Pell and bring her to a return she accepts," he said. "We have not agreed to take her place. We have not agreed to complete any work left unfinished."
  The resin brightened.
  Miriam added her name and the same limited purpose. Tarin did likewise. Each time a narrow line appeared around the frame. Four visitors, independently declared.
  The door opened.
  Rain fell on the other side.
  Not gray rain this time. It fell in straight shining strands through a hall whose floor reflected the ceiling so perfectly that Lazara hesitated before stepping in. A dry path crossed the water. At its end, in a glass-clear booth with no visible glass, sat a woman holding a yellow ledger against her chest.
  She saw them and began to shake her head.
  Behind her, occupying the entire far wall, a giant page hung vertically from ceiling to floor. Words crossed it in a dark moving line.
  Four visitors enter the receiving hall.
  The engineer is not expected.
  Elias read it.
  Then, slowly, he smiled without amusement.
  "That's going to be inconvenient for whoever wrote the schedule."
  

Chapter 6
A Name in the Rain
Nima could hear them. She could not make her own voice cross the booth's boundary except through the falling water.
  She wrote on a page from her ledger and held it up.
  DON'T READ THE LARGE PAGE ALOUD.
  Miriam, who had begun to do exactly that, closed her mouth.
  The booth was not a prison cell in the ordinary sense. It had a chair, water, food, and an inner door leading to a small private room. Its exterior boundary looked like clear air interrupted at a perfect rectangle. Nima could press her hand against it and make ripples, but no solid object passed through.
  Elias knelt at the base. No visible load-bearing edge. No latch to repair. He hated it immediately.
  Lazara asked questions Nima could answer in writing. Had she been injured? No. Had anyone touched her? No. Did she know where the inner door returned? Same private room, independently checked. Had she seen whoever wrote the page?
  Nima's pencil stopped.
  YES. NOT ALL OF HIM.
  She turned the sheet.
  THEY CALL HIM THE AUTHOR. I THINK HE WAS A MAN BEFORE HE WAS A JOB.
  Behind her the wall page changed. The line naming four visitors faded. Another began below it.
  Lazara Moreau approaches the boundary.
  Lazara stayed still.
  The line remained for several seconds. Then a thin correction crossed it.
  Lazara Moreau declines the boundary.
  Tarin laughed sharply. Not because it was funny. Because something that seemed to know the future had just been forced to revise a sentence.
  "It's watching," she said. "At least some of it."
  Nima nodded so hard her hair fell into her face. She wrote: MAKING A VERSION. WANTS US TO FINISH IT.
  The rain thickened between them and the wall. Words blurred behind the falling strands. Lazara understood the temptation to look harder. The page seemed to promise that the right sentence would organize everything around it. She had once wanted a report to do that after a road collapse.
  She turned her back on it.
  "Nima. Tell us what happened. Start where you arrived, not where it got strange."
  The young woman sat, relief visible even through the boundary. She had delivered three copied ledgers from the Court's independent route stewards. The North House acknowledged receipt on fourteen, then offered her a refreshment room. She had seen her mother's kitchen in its window and realized the orientation system was working badly. She reported that instead of entering.
  A woman named Sera came to meet her. Gray hair, green apron, a burn scar along the left forearm. Sera said a recorder upstairs needed an independent courier to witness an address correction. Nima agreed to observe, not operate.
  "Good distinction," Miriam said.
  Nima's mouth twisted. It had not been sufficient.
  In the hall on twenty-four, the wall page showed Nima's mother receiving a message that her daughter was dead. Nima spoke without thinking: That isn't true. I'm here.
  The page revised. Nima Pell remains here.
  Then the booth formed around her.
  "Not consent," Elias said. "An interpretation."
  Nima pressed both hands against the boundary and nodded.
  Lazara asked whether the wall page had ever described something Nima did not already know. The courier thought, then wrote that it had predicted a meal she was about to request and a visit from Sera before Sera came. It had also described a brother arriving from the Court. Nima had no brother.
  "Did it correct that?" Miriam asked.
  The young woman nodded. It changed brother to familiar person, then person with shared origin. The description became less wrong by becoming less specific.
  Miriam copied the sequence. It was not a small detail. A thing that claimed to know every life could still be disguising an inference with confident language. The house had enough real records to make its guesses feel intimate. That did not make every conclusion true.
  Nima wrote another account while Lazara held the board steady on her side. She had grown up delivering messages with her aunt. The work began with errands to the nearest terrace, then household records, then supervised routes. Her aunt taught her to ask who needed the message and who would notice if it did not arrive.
  "I wanted to see places," Nima said through the rain. The water briefly carried her voice. "It sounded nobler when I called it service. I did want to help. I also wanted somewhere my mother hadn't already told everybody what I was like."
  Tarin laughed softly. "I know that place. Never found it either."
  Nima smiled, surprised into a younger face. Then the rain thinned and she had to return to writing.
  She had once delivered a wedding acknowledgment to a harbor court where people hung lights on the water. Another time she carried a death notice all afternoon because she could not bear to give it to the waiting woman. Her aunt made her finish the delivery herself, then sat with her afterward. Compassion did not permit her to decide someone else should be denied the news.
  "I'm trying to remember that," she wrote. "I don't want my mother protected from knowing I'm frightened."
  Lazara promised to send an accurate message through the Court's relay. Not a cheerful one. Not an alarming one made more dramatic by what they could not yet explain. Nima dictated what she wanted said and checked the copy before Tarin took it.
  It was the first choice anyone in the hall had asked the courier to make that was entirely hers.
  When Tarin returned to the threshold, Nima asked what the weather was like outside the pyramids. Snow. Low cloud. Wind from the ridge.
  She looked at the permanent falling strands beyond her booth. "I'd like to be cold for a real reason."

Chapter 20
A Place to Leave
The independent receiving frame required three things the North House could not supply by describing them.

  Lazara took a photograph and sent it to Claire without adding a request for a reply.
  By afternoon the kitchen smelled of soup and damp coats. The repaired cupboard contained cups. The blue one had a chip. They kept it because it still held tea, not because an object altered by an impossible journey deserved to rule the room.
  Elias washed bowls while Lazara dried. At some point he said, "I'd like you here more."
  She stopped with a bowl in her hands.
  "More often?"
  "Eventually. Not tonight because we planted trees. Not because Alex likes you. I mean I'd like us to talk about living together when you've had time to want it separately from me asking."
  She looked at him. The care in the awkward sentence moved her more than a perfectly delivered proposal would have.
  "I do want to talk about it."
  "Good."
  "And I want to keep some space that's mine."
  "I thought you might."
  He took the bowl before she dried the same place through the glaze. She laughed and leaned against him.
  The kiss was unhurried. No doorway waited for them to prove they trusted one another. Alex called from the other room that he could hear them being quiet, which was apparently worse than hearing them talk. They broke apart laughing.
  Messages from the North House came monthly.
  Nima had resumed courier work but declined two assignments until the route declarations were improved. Her mother approved of the improved declarations and disapproved of the courier work. Nima considered the balance familiar enough to be home.
  Pel and Leth reported ordinary disputes, crop questions, one broken water drum, and no missing days. The population had increased by one when a child was born. They registered her as a living person without asking a wall to predict what work she would do.
  Ilra wrote a history-room entry about Bren's bowl. Oren asked permission to contribute his records. She said not yet. He accepted the answer. His recovered hand could hold a pen for short intervals. He wrote past observations and left the next line empty until something actually happened.
  Sera divided her time between his recovery and the garden. They argued. They had meals that were not discussions of the house. Some days she stayed in the growing rooms overnight because the early work interested her, and he learned to ask how it had gone rather than whether she was returning soon.
  Miriam assembled the observations from the wilderness court without calling uncertainty a defeat. A living voice, an independently verified exchange, a tented sanctuary pattern, a covered chest carried on poles. Those were the records. Their relation to biblical history remained a question for later work, not a conclusion purchased with the urgency of a rescue.
  Tarin visited Sam every few weeks. He had planted potatoes in a borrowed bed and was offended by their refusal to follow his preferred schedule. She took photographs for Lazara. The old surveyor had finally found a practical use for expecting a future he could not control.
  At twenty-four, the large page remained mounted on the wall.
  The residents had voted not to burn it. Destruction would have been satisfying, but satisfaction was not the whole question. They covered its old author slot, isolated its predictive authority, and used the remaining surface for current observations copied from independently maintained logs.
  Rain at the west foundation: low.
  Garden frost interval: complete.
  North House present: count confirmed.
  On some evenings, beyond the closed sensory shutters, the ocean receivers still carried songs. No human message had yet been verified within them. The label ATLANTIS OUTBOUND remained in the archive with its original symbols beside it. It could wait without turning waiting into an invitation to surrender another home.
  One message did arrive from the independent wilderness court.
  Hadan's receipt marks matched. The words were brief.
  Your count is received. A dwelling may remain ready without requiring a traveler to come.
  Miriam sent the copy to Lazara, who read it at the kitchen table while Alex searched for a missing sock and Elias tried to persuade the cat to leave a clean basket of laundry.
  She showed him when he sat down.
  "Would you go?" he asked.
  "If we were invited. If we had a reason better than being unable to leave a mystery alone."
  He nodded. She knew the same desire moved in him: dry valleys, carried sanctuaries, gardens beyond doors, the chance to learn something enormous without making it a claim over the people who already lived there.
  Desire could remain desire. They did not have to turn it into today's departure.
  Outside, rain began on the two young trees. Ordinary rain, falling from an ordinary sky onto soil that would have to be tended again tomorrow.
  Lazara took Elias's hand across the table. He turned his palm upward. The house around them contained no sentence capable of finishing the gesture for them.
  Alex found the sock inside his other shoe and announced this as a discovery of considerable importance.
  They listened. Then they ate.

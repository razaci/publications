Chapter 1
The Leaf in the Jar
The leaf was still wet when Sela took it out of the jar, though the man who had carried it down from the mountain swore he had not opened the lid in sixteen days.
  She laid it on the bench beneath the crooked tree. It was the length of her hand, silver beneath and dark above, with a notch at the tip like the small bite a child might take before deciding a thing was not food. Water gathered on its ribs. One drop fell onto the wood.
  Tirzah watched the drop soak in.
  “Was there earth in the jar?” she asked.
  “Only that.”
  The traveler was called Oren. He had a split sandal, a patient donkey, and the worn look of a man who had learned that giving news did not entitle him to rest. Sela moved the bread closer. He ate at once, with an effort not to appear hungry that made her sorry she had waited.
  “Who sent it?”
  “A woman at the upper spring. She said to bring it to the house with the long table.”
  “Half the houses have long tables now,” Japheth said from the doorway.
  “She said yours had a leg that did not match.”
  “That narrows the world.”
  He came down the step slowly. His knee had been worse since the last wet week. Sela offered her arm without looking directly at him, and he took it without admitting he needed it. In the years since Babel, they had developed small treaties of that kind.
  The village beyond the garden was waking from its noon rest. A woman called for a boy in one tongue, and the boy answered in another. A cart wheel struck the loose stone at the corner. Somewhere Ruma was telling a customer that a jar could hold water or be cheap, and that it was discourteous to demand both at the price of neither.
  Sela turned the leaf over with a twig.
  “No teeth on the edge,” she said. “No holes. Whatever grows it has no worms.”
  “Or someone chose the best leaf,” Tirzah said.
  “Or there are worms that are very polite,” Japheth said.
  Oren smiled, then wiped his hands and brought out a second object.
  It was a strip of cloth folded around a thin flat plate. Sela thought it was polished stone until she touched it. It was too light. Along one side were seven circles, each cut by a short line. Beneath them someone had scratched a tree, a jar, and a bell.
  Tirzah stopped moving.
  Japheth looked at her, then at the plate.
  The garden did not fall silent. The boy still shouted. The cart still creaked. Those ordinary sounds made Sela feel that her parents had stepped a little way out of the afternoon and left her at its edge.
  “You know it,” she said.
  “I know the shape of the marks,” Tirzah answered.
  “From the ship?”
  Her mother looked at Oren.
  He put down his bread. “I was told to give you the things. I was not told to carry your answer anywhere.”
  “You have done nothing wrong,” Tirzah said.
  Sela heard how carefully she spoke. It was the voice she used when a frightened animal had come too close to a fire.
  Oren explained where he had found the woman: above the old vineyard road, near a stone house built partly into the slope. She wore a traveling cloak too large for her. Her hands were stained green. She had offered him salt for carrying the jar, but he had refused because she seemed to have little else.
  “Was she young?” Sela asked.
  “Younger than your mother. Older than you. It is hard to say when people are tired.”
  “Did she give a name?”
  “Nadi. She asked whether your mother still kept seeds in the little box.”
  Tirzah put a hand flat on the bench. Sela could see the tendons along her wrist.
  At last Japheth said, “The doors aboard the ark were closed. I took out the fittings myself.”
  “I did not ask about doors,” Oren said.
  “No,” Japheth said. “You did not.”
  They gave the traveler a place in the shade and sent a child for Eshar. Sela brought the plate into the house. The leaf followed in a bowl, for her mother would not put it back into the jar. It seemed suddenly important that the thing should be seen clearly, without curved glass between it and the eye.
  Their house smelled of flour, warm clay, and the wood Japheth had cut that morning. The shelves did not match. A rain stain marked the beam above the hearth. Sela loved the house most when she noticed such things, because none had been planned to impress a stranger.
  Before she entered the house, Sela noticed the jar had left a dry circle on the bench. Everything around it was damp from the leaf. She touched the circle, then the outside of the glass. The base was cold despite the warm afternoon.
  Oren watched her.
  “Do you think I have brought trouble?”
  “I think you have brought something you did not understand.”
  “That is not always different.”
  “No.”
  He twisted a loose thread at his cuff. The gesture made him seem younger than his road-worn face.
  “The woman asked whether you were good people. I said people at the crossing had spoken well of you.”
  “And if they had not?”
  “I would still have carried the jar. She looked as though she had been waiting too long.”
  Sela set the bowl down.
  “You should eat before anyone asks you to decide whether you were wise.”
  He laughed quietly.
  “My wife says that.”
  “Then she may be wise.”
  “She says that too.”
  Sela went to fetch more bread. When she came back, her mother was standing over the bowl, looking at the silver ribs of the leaf without touching them. She had the expression Sela remembered from the first days after Babel, when a familiar mouth opened and an unfamiliar word came out.
  “It is only a leaf,” Sela said, then regretted it.
  Tirzah looked at her.
  “Nothing is only itself when it has come from someone who needs an answer.”
  She took the bread and gave half to Oren before going inside. Sela carried the bowl after her. The leaf shifted with the movement, and another drop touched the rim. She counted it, not because the number helped, but because she needed something in the afternoon that could be counted.
  She laid the plate beside her mother's old scissors.
  “The bell is yours,” she said.
  “It resembles mine.”
  “And the jar?”
  “Many people carry jars.”
  “Mother.”
  Tirzah sat down.
  When Sela was small, the story of the woman in moonlight had frightened her less than the story of the city before the Flood. A stranger stepping out of a wall seemed easier to bear than neighbors who laughed while a house burned. Later she had thought the door story was a way her mother spoke of grief. Later still, Tirzah had shown her the pencil.
  It lay now in the seed box, worn short, wrapped in linen against damp.
  Sela fetched it.
  Tirzah did not stop her.
  “You told me those doors could not open again.”
  “Those doors could not.”
  “You think there were others.”
  “I think I was young and tired when I decided that a thing finished near me must be finished everywhere.”
  Japheth sat opposite her. For a while they looked at each other in the manner of two people reading a letter they had once written together and hoped never to receive.
  Sela waited. She had learned waiting at Babel. Waiting could be a gift or a way of keeping power. She tried to make hers the first kind.
  At last Tirzah reached for the plate.
  “The woman who came to us was called Elian,” she said. “She said her people had built roads into years that were not their own. They took seeds. Animals. People. We helped some of those people leave.”
  “And this woman?”
  “I do not know.”
  “Then we go to the mountain.”
  Japheth opened his mouth.
  Sela lifted a hand. “I did not say tonight. I did not say without food, or with one rope and a good opinion of ourselves.”
  Her father closed his mouth again.
  “You have been listening to your husband.”
  “I have been listening to you.”
  Eshar arrived carrying the hinge he had been repairing, as if he had feared the house might cease to exist if he let go of it. Sela told him about the leaf. He set the hinge on the table and examined the jar.
  “There is no seam in this glass,” he said.
  “Can that matter?”
  “It matters to the person who made it.”
  He held it to the light. The jar cast a pale oval on his palm. Near the base, too small to see at first, was another mark: a tree inside a broken ring.
  Tirzah rose so quickly that her chair scraped the floor.
  Outside, the bell above the garden gate rang once.
  Everyone looked toward it.
  A child stood on the path with a basket of onions, astonished by four adults staring at him.
  “Ruma says you forgot these,” he announced.
  Sela began to laugh. She could not help it. Her father laughed too, quietly at first, then until he had to press his knee.
  Tirzah took the basket.
  “Tell Ruma we remember.”
  After the child left, she shut the door, though the afternoon was warm.
  “We will take the upper road,” she said. “And before we go anywhere strange, we will tell someone where we mean to return.”
  Sela looked down at the leaf. A new drop had formed on its silver underside.
  This time she caught it on her finger.
  It was cold.
Chapter 2
What Could Be Left at Home
Ruma refused to call their journey a visit.
  “A visit is when you go to see a person who expects you and gives you something to eat,” she said. “You are going up a mountain to meet a woman who sends wet leaves in sealed jars. That needs another word.”
  Sela spread their grain on a cloth and measured it into bags.
  “We shall try to be fed.”
  “You shall try to come back.”
  Ruma was sorting cups she had brought without being asked. Every third cup she rejected for a flaw Sela could not see. Her hands were thick from years of clay, and she could judge the soundness of a pot by lifting it as another person judged a fruit by smell.
  Tovi waited beside the door with a carrying frame. He had built it for the trip before anyone invited him.
  “Six people need two frames,” he said.
  “Five,” Japheth answered.
  Tovi looked at him, then at his knee.
  “No,” Japheth said.
  “I have not spoken.”
  “You have been speaking with your eyebrows for some time.”
  The argument ended only when Tirzah set the medicine salve in the larger pack and asked which of the two men intended to carry it. Japheth went to find his better walking staff. Tovi remained.
  Their household could not simply disappear. Water had to be drawn, tools returned, a roof finished before the next rain. Eshar had promised to help a neighbor raise a wall. Sela had twelve jars in the kiln that would be ruined if nobody watched the firing.
  She went to the pottery yard and found her apprentice, Iva, sitting on the low wall eating a pear.
  Iva was fourteen and had the grave patience of someone constantly mistaken for a child.
  “I need you to finish the firing.”
  “I know how.”
  “I know you do.”
  “Then why do you look as though you are leaving me a baby?”
  Sela sat beside her. The girl moved her bare feet away from the broken tiles.
  “Because I am bad at leaving work in other hands.”
  “Yes,” Iva said.
  The answer was so prompt that Sela laughed.
  They went through the firing marks together. No new glaze. No more than two extra armfuls of wood after the middle vent showed red. If the wind changed sharply, close the small side opening first. Iva repeated each point, then made Sela repeat the location of the grain payment due from the family across the river.
  “You forget money when people tell you sad things,” the girl said.
  “Sometimes people have sad things.”
  “Sometimes they also have money.”
  Sela gave her the tally board.
  When she returned, Eshar was at the bench setting pegs into a narrow box. He had put a strap on it and lined the inside with wool.
  “For the leaf?”
  “For what we do not want crushed.”
  He was still not looking at her.
  She sat beside him.
  “You think I decided for both of us.”
  “You did.”
  “I said we would go.”
  “Yes.”
  The yard seemed to grow louder. She could hear Ruma and Tirzah sorting dried beans, the hollow rattle of them falling into a bowl.
  “I should have asked.”
  “You should have left room for an answer.”
  Sela touched the fresh wood. Eshar's work had always been more exact than his speech. At Babel that had made him useful to men who liked workers who did not argue. Since leaving the city he had learned to stop a load, refuse an order, and ask what would happen after a thing was built. Sela admired those changes until one of them obstructed her.
  “I want to go,” she said.
  “I know.”
  “I want you with me.”
  “That is a different thing.”
  She nodded.
  He put down the peg.
  “I will come. But if we find a door, we look before we walk. If one of us says stop, the other stops. Even if someone is calling from the far side.”
  “What if someone is hurt?”
  “Then we find a way to help without making two hurt people.”
  She did not like the answer. She liked that he had said it.
  They fitted the lid together. One corner caught, and he shaved it down until it sat true.
  Eshar found her later at the kiln, staring through the small opening where the heat showed red.
  “Are you asking the fire for advice?”
  “It gives clear answers. Too much wood, too much heat. Too little, and everything fails slowly.”
  “People do that too.”
  She shifted to make room beside her.
  “I am afraid you will say no and I will be angry with you for saying it.”
  “I am afraid of that too.”
  The honesty made her turn toward him.
  “What if I went alone?”
  “Then I would decide whether I could bear waiting. And you would decide whether being alone was worth what you wanted.”
  “That sounds cold.”
  “It is warmer than telling you I agree when I do not.”
  They watched a little spark lift inside the opening. The wood settled with a dry crack.
  Sela thought of their first roof after Babel. They had argued over the slope for half a day, both secretly afraid that needing to argue meant the house was already failing. The roof had held. Their argument had become a story they told badly because each remembered being more reasonable.
  “Will you tell me when you do not agree?”
  “Yes.”
  “Even if I am in front of other people?”
  “Especially if those people are about to walk on a bridge we have not tested.”
  She laughed and leaned into him.
  He stayed until the fire settled. When they rose, he took neither her arm nor the tools from her hands. They went back across the yard side by side, leaving the kiln to the apprentice who had already proved she could watch it.
  By evening the house had become a place of borrowed things. A neighbor brought a sound water skin. Another sent wool socks for Japheth and pretended she had made too many. Oren offered to guide them as far as the upper spring, though the donkey needed rest. They agreed to hire a second animal and leave his behind.
  Tirzah stood over the seed box for a long time.
  Sela watched from the hearth.
  “What will you take?”
  “Very little.”
  Her mother selected a small bag of beans, a handful of grain, and six seeds from the crooked tree. She wrapped them separately and marked the cloth with charcoal.
  “The woman asked about the box.”
  “That does not oblige me to bring everything in it.”
  She took out the pencil, considered it, and put it back.
  Sela reached for it.
  “May I?”
  Tirzah nodded.
  The pencil sat between them like a bone from an animal neither could name. Sela had used it only twice, on scraps her mother guarded because there were so few. Clay was easy. Wood could be scraped. This soft black mark on thin pale paper seemed fragile beyond reason, yet Elian had thought it useful enough to give away at a last farewell.
  “I could write what happens,” Sela said.
  “You could.”
  “Would you mind?”
  Tirzah closed the seed box.
  “There are things I did not tell you because you were small. Things I did not tell because they hurt. And things I cannot tell clearly because I did not understand them.”
  “I could leave spaces.”
  “Yes,” her mother said. “Leave spaces where you do not know.”
  Later Sela went out to the garden. The new beds were dark with water. Her father sat on the bench with his staff between his knees. He had tied a narrow strip of leather around the grip.
  “Better?” she asked.
  “For walking. It does nothing for sitting.”
  She sat beside him anyway.
  He took a little wooden bird from his pocket. Tirzah had received it from strangers on the road after Babel. The wings were uneven. One eye was only a dark knot.
  “Your mother put it with the grain,” he said. “I think she means to take it.”
  “Why?”
  “She may need reminding that strangers also give things.”
  Sela studied his face. Lines ran from his nose to his mouth. His hair was almost white now. As a child she had assumed that strength meant never needing to lean. Now she saw how much care it took for him to cross a yard on a bad day without letting pain decide the shape of his temper.
  “You do not have to come.”
  He smiled.
  “You have learned that sentence.”
  “I mean it.”
  “So do I when I say I want to.”
  They listened to the village settle. Somewhere a husband and wife argued over whose turn it was to bring in the goat. The goat gave its opinion. A baby cried, stopped, and started again with more conviction.
  Japheth turned the bird in his hand.
  “The first time I helped carry a stranger through a door, I thought the hard part was getting him across. Then he asked where he was going to sleep.”
  Sela waited.
  “If we open anything, think about the beds.”
  At dawn Ruma came to say goodbye and found them still tying packs.
  “You will be late for your wet leaf,” she said.
  She pressed a broad cup into Sela's hands.
  “For when you find someone too proud to ask for water.”
  It was the only cup from the day before that she had not rejected.
Chapter 3
The Upper Spring
On the second day Japheth stopped trying to hide his limp.
  The road had narrowed to a ledge above a dry stream. Oren led the pack animal around loose stones, while Eshar walked behind with a hand on the rear load. Tovi went ahead to check the turns. Tirzah kept pace with her husband, neither rushing him nor making a show of patience.
  Sela found herself counting the steps between shade.
  Her father noticed.
  “If you count aloud, I shall charge you for it.”
  “I was counting stones.”
  “They look much alike from where I am.”
  They rested beneath a leaning cedar. Oren knew the place. He showed them where an old flood had stripped bark from the uphill side of the trunk.
  “Not that Flood,” he said, glancing at Japheth. “A small one. Last spring.”
  “There are useful sizes,” Japheth answered.
  Sela filled his cup. Below them the land folded into brown hills and pale green strips along water. The river they had left was out of sight. She had thought she would feel larger on the mountain. Instead she felt the size of the land and the small reach of her own legs.
  At the cedar, Tirzah unwrapped a heel of bread and gave the larger piece to Japheth. He broke it again and returned half.
  Sela sat above them on a stone. Oren had gone ahead to water the animal. For a little while they could hear only wind moving through the needles.
  “Do you remember the first tree you planted together?” she asked.
  Japheth looked at Tirzah.
  “She planted. I dug the hole in the wrong place.”
  “He dug it where the ground was easiest.”
  “That seemed a sound principle.”
  “It was sound until the water ran away from it.”
  Sela smiled. She knew the tree, or what grew where it had stood. She had not known the disagreement beneath it.
  “Did you move it?”
  “The hole?” Japheth asked.
  “The tree.”
  “Twice,” Tirzah said. “And then I had to admit the seedling had been weak before either of us touched it.”
  Sela watched her mother brush crumbs from her skirt.
  “You tell it differently when people ask for advice.”
  “People asking for advice rarely want the whole history of a dead seedling.”
  “Perhaps they should have it.”
  “Perhaps.”
  Japheth held out the water skin. Sela took it, drank, and passed it back. The leather smelled of smoke from home.
  Below them, the land lay quiet under a haze. It was hard to believe that another sky could exist beyond a stone wall somewhere above it. Sela tried to imagine carrying this cedar's shadow into that other place, not the tree itself, only the exact coolness under the branches.
  She could not.
  She broke the last bread into three pieces and stayed where she was until Oren called them on.
  Near midday they passed the vineyard.
  Tirzah paused at the low wall. A man working between the rows straightened and looked at them. He raised a hand in greeting but did not come up. There was no reason he should know their business. Sela was glad. She did not want to turn their journey into a procession of people repeating the name of the ark.
  Beyond the vineyard, Oren showed them a path that climbed toward the spring.
  “The woman was here.”
  A strip of cloth hung from a thorn. Eshar knelt beneath it. In the dust lay the mark of a boot with little straight teeth along the sole.
  Sela had never seen such a shoe.
  Tirzah had.
  They followed the print to the stone house.
  Its roof had fallen at one corner. Nettles grew through the doorway. A clay gutter carried spring water into a trough, and the overflow vanished beneath a rock. Beside the trough stood a woman in a gray cloak, washing a cut across her palm.
  She looked up before anyone spoke.
  “Tirzah?”
  Her mouth formed the name with care. Her voice was rough and low.
  Tirzah stopped several steps away.
  “Nadi?”
  The woman nodded, then swayed.
  Tovi caught her before she struck the trough. She fought him for a moment, more from fear than strength. Tirzah said something soft, and the woman stopped. They got her inside and cleared a place among dry leaves.
  Her cloak hid strange clothing: seams so close Sela could scarcely see the thread, a band of dark cloth at one wrist, a small square attached to her belt. Her boots were gray and torn at the toes. There was dried blood along one sleeve.
  Oren brought water. Nadi drank too fast and coughed.
  “Slowly,” Tirzah said.
  “I thought you would not come.”
  “We have come. Who hurt you?”
  Nadi glanced at the doorway.
  “Metal. A shutter. I put my hand where it should not have been.”
  “Did someone close it?”
  “Yes.”
  The answer changed the air in the room.
  Sela took out Ruma's cup. It steadied the woman's hands better than the narrow traveling vessel. She noticed and gave Sela a look of gratitude too tired to become a smile.
  “My name is Sela.”
  Nadi looked at her for a long time.
  “Of course,” she said.
  “What does that mean?”
  “It means I should have asked your name before I came here.”
  Nadi closed her eyes. Tirzah cleaned the cut. The bleeding had stopped, but the edges were angry. They bound it with clean cloth and gave her bread softened in water. Nobody asked for her story until she had eaten.
  When she could sit, she brought the dark square from her belt.
  A light appeared in it.
  Oren stepped back so sharply that his shoulder hit the wall.
  “It is a lamp,” Nadi said.
  “No lamp I have carried,” he answered.
  She tilted it toward the floor. An image moved across the surface: rows of trees under a clear roof, people in pale clothes, a strip of red sky beyond the glass. Then the view shifted to a child lying on a cot beside a woman who held a wet cloth against his neck.
  The boy opened his eyes.
  “Is this now?” Sela asked.
  “No. Before I left.”
  “Is he alive?”
  “He was.”
  Tirzah's hands tightened on the bandage.
  Nadi touched the image and it went dark.
  “I grow food,” she said. “On a world called Haven. Our trees are dying. Our children are sick. The people who bring our supplies say we have done something wrong. They will not let us see their records.”
  “Why come to us?” Japheth asked.
  “Because our oldest planting story names a woman who kept a garden alive through the loss of her whole world. Because your people grew things without the feed they sell us. Because I needed someone outside their walls.”
  Sela looked at her mother. Tirzah was not flattered. She looked almost angry.
  “I lost most of what I planted,” she said. “Some years we had very little.”
  “Then you know what it costs.”
  “How did you find us?”
  “Through a service route. An old mountain station. The program used it before the ship. It reaches this slope now.”
  Japheth leaned forward.
  “The ship fittings were removed.”
  “I know only our records. This station has its own anchor. It does not touch the ship.”
  “Then why the marks?”
  “They use old marks on things they do not want local workers to understand. I copied them so you might believe I was telling the truth.”
  Sela looked at the bright square in Nadi's hand. She wanted to touch it and wanted it put away.
  “Who is the boy?”
  “Lio. My sister's son.”
  “Where is his mother?”
  “With him.”
  “And where are the people who closed the shutter on your hand?”
  Nadi's eyes went to the far wall.
  Under the fallen roof, beyond the nettles, a thin line of white light lay between two stones.
  Eshar stood.
  He did not approach it. He went to the doorway and looked outside, then returned and moved the packs against the sound wall. Tovi took the animal farther from the house.
  “We look first,” Eshar said to Sela.
  She nodded.
  The light widened.
  A voice came through it, speaking in a tongue Sela had never heard. Nadi answered quickly. The voice rose. She stopped replying.
  “What does he say?” Tirzah asked.
  “That I am needed back at work.”
  “Are you free to refuse?”
  Nadi looked at the bandage across her hand.
  The light widened again. Cold air spilled into the room, smelling of wet stone and something sharp, like a storm trapped inside a cup.
  A man appeared beyond the opening. He wore pale clothes and carried a flat black object against his chest.
  Nadi struggled to her feet.
  “Do not stand in front of it,” she said.
  Japheth got up with his staff.
  The man looked at the six travelers, the bread on the floor, the blood in the trough outside. Then he lowered the black object.
  “My name is Corin,” he said in slow, imperfect words. “Please do not come through yet. The floor on our side is missing.”
Chapter 4
The Floor on the Other Side
Corin showed them the hole before he tried to explain the road.
  He lowered a line through the bright opening. Its knotted end dropped out of sight. When he drew it up, the fibers were wet and gray with dust.
  “Stairs under us,” he said. “A tank below that. The near edge broke when they shut the door.”
  “Who shut it?” Eshar asked.
  “My supervisor.”
  “Did he know someone was crossing?”
  Corin looked toward Nadi.
  “Yes.”
  Eshar set his pack down. The answer made the work before him simpler in one way and worse in every other.
  The opening stood between them like a doorway cut from daylight. On their side was broken stone. On the other, a metal ledge ran above a dark space. A pipe along the far wall leaked at a joint, each drop vanishing before Sela could hear it land.
  Corin knelt and held out a narrow board of stiff gray material.
  “It will span.”
  “How much weight?” Eshar asked.
  “Enough.”
  “For which person?”
  Corin blinked, then looked at Japheth's staff and the packs. He withdrew the board.
  “I will find the rating.”
  “No,” Japheth said. “Find something that can hold two of you standing still. We can watch you test it.”
  It took time. Corin disappeared and returned with two braces and a wider panel. He worked while Eshar directed him in gestures. Their shared words were few, but Eshar could point to an unsupported edge in any language. Twice Corin began to hurry. Twice Eshar held up his palm.
  Nadi lay back against the wall. Tirzah asked whether Corin could close the opening from his side.
  “Leave this control here,” Nadi said. She unfastened the band at her wrist. Beneath it her skin was pale and bruised. “Press the smooth place for the light. Hold it to the ring for the latch. No other place.”
  She demonstrated without closing it.
  Sela watched her hands.
  “What if it is dropped?”
  “It will not open by itself.”
  “What if someone on your side closes it?”
  Nadi hesitated.
  “Then this will not force it.”
  They had found the edge of her promise.
  Tirzah took the band and put it in the wooden box. She did not put it on.
  Oren cleared his throat.
  “I can guide you home. I cannot guide you in there.”
  “You should not have to,” Tirzah said.
  He seemed relieved and ashamed of the relief. Sela knew the look. It was one she had worn when other people offered to do work she feared.
  They agreed that he would return to the village with the animal if they had not come out by the next noon. He would take a message naming the spring and the stone house. Tovi would stay with him for the first night, keep the packs dry, and watch the opening. They would leave most of their food on the mountain side.
  “Why?” Nadi asked.
  “So we still have a reason to come back,” Japheth said.
  By the time the span was ready, the sunlight outside had begun to lean west. Corin stood on the panel and shifted his weight. Eshar asked him to kneel, stand, and carry a heavy case across. When the braces held, he tested it himself with one foot, then both.
  Sela followed.
  The crossing was less grand than she expected. Her ears pressed shut. The floor seemed to rise under her while her stomach remained behind. She reached for the wall and found it cold enough to hurt.
  On the other side, Nadi was being helped onto a bench. Tirzah came across with the cup and medicine cloth. Japheth took longer. Nobody spoke while he crossed. He touched the wall at the end and exhaled through his nose.
  “I do not care for that road.”
  “You learn to,” Corin said.
  “Do you?”
  Corin did not answer.
  The chamber was built inside a mountain. Stone showed through cracks in a white coating. Metal shelves held sealed boxes, tools, empty bottles, and sacks marked with the broken ring. Above a worktable hung a drawing of seven doors joined by lines. Four had been crossed out in red.
  Sela pointed.
  “Those are closed?”
  “Those are gone,” Corin said.
  “What is the difference?”

The Name Below the Water

The Orchard Beneath the Red Sky

A Story With Missing Pages
Sela did not want to see the end of her own life.

On the road she had imagined asking how far her children might travel, whether her work would last, whether people in strange years still made bowls by pressing thumbs into clay.   “First,” she said, “we are taking this back to the child who made it.”
Chapter 9
The Price of a Safe Answer
Dr. Sen let them leave with the drawing.
  That frightened Sela more than if she had tried to seize it. The woman watched them walk away with the calm of someone who knew which doors they would need next.
  At the clinic, Irena put the samples in a cabinet with a lock she controlled. She asked Corin to write where they had come from, when he had taken them, and who had watched. Eshar made his mark beside Sela's. Nadi translated the note twice, once for the clinic and once for their own pack.

  “Because a thing should not become untrue merely because someone has the only copy,” Sela said.
  Nadi translated. Meret looked toward her son and nodded.

  Meret corrected the sound, laughing gently.

  “People who understand them.”
  “Then let them show us.”
  “That is what Dr. Sen has offered.”
  Irena took the tray and set it aside.
  “Not in front of the patients.”

  Daren placed the form on the desk.
  “There are reasons for procedures. We have limited stores. A rumor can stop people drinking safe water. Panic can harm more people than a fault in a pipe.”
  “Then fix the pipe,” Japheth said.
  “We need confirmation.”
  “You had a boy missing under a floor.”
  “The notice said he was transferred.”
  “Who checked?”
  Daren's face tightened.
  “I did not.”
  It was the first useful thing he had said.
  Irena moved the form away from him.
  “Until my tests are done, the clinic will use water from the north reserve. I want the south drinking line isolated.”
  “That affects three housing blocks.”
  “Yes.”
  “People will ask why.”
  “Tell them we are checking a fault. Give them water while we do it.”
  Daren looked from one face to another. He was searching for an easier person to answer.
  Tirzah spoke.
  “When I was young, men told me that a fire was work being done somewhere else. The house belonged to people who could not make them explain it.”
  He looked at her.
  She did not raise her voice.
  “You have a chance to ask before the water reaches another child.”
  Daren folded the unsigned form.
  “I can authorize one day.”
  “One day of what?” Irena asked.
  “North water. Testing. No public claim about cause until we have results.”

  He nodded reluctantly.

  Meret shook her head, then corrected herself.
  “Yes. I should.”

  Nadi came in quietly.
  “The tests will take time.”
  “How much?”
  “Enough that we should eat.”
  They shared bread from the mountain. It was harder than it had been that morning. Sela found a little grit in the crust and was absurdly glad of it.
  Nadi looked at the bread.
  “I thought your mother would know how to save the orchard at once.”
  “My mother would tell you to stop poisoning the roots before you asked them to grow.”
  Nadi closed her eyes.
  “I brought you because of a story.”
  “You brought people. There is a difference.”

  Sela heard only one name clearly.
  Vale.
  Tirzah appeared at the far end of the corridor.
  She knew the name too.
Chapter 10
The Voice That Remembered Her
Vale did not appear in the doorway. His face arrived on a wall.
  The image was larger than a man's head, clear enough that Sela could see the crease in his collar. Behind him stood a shelf of pale boxes and a window showing blackness. He looked younger than she expected. Her mother had spoken of him as one speaks of an old wound; Sela had given him the face of age.
  Corin set the sound low. It did not make the man less present.
  “Mrs. Tirzah,” Vale said.
  She did not answer the title.
  “You have met me before,” he continued.
  “Yes.”
  “Our local times make introductions difficult.”
  “They made your work difficult too, I hope.”
  A small pause. Vale moved something beyond the edge of the image.
  “I understand there has been a mechanical incident.”
  “A boy was left below a closed floor.”
  “That is being reviewed.”
  “He is called Brin.”
  “I have his record.”
  “Then use his name.”

  Irena asked what was in it.
  He referred her to the agricultural team.
  She asked which team.
  He said Dr. Sen would provide an approved account.
  Tirzah sat down opposite the wall.
  “You once came into my household speaking that way,” she said. “You offered help before you named the people you had hurt.”
  Vale studied her. For the first time his eyes seemed to move past her face toward the years behind it.
  “The event you describe does not belong to the interval under discussion.”
  “It belongs to me.”
  He looked toward Daren.
  “Director, the visitors must remain within the authorized area. The mountain route is not licensed for residence.”
  “They are not asking to reside,” Daren said.
  “Then arrange their return.”
  Sela stepped closer.
  “What happens to Nadi?”
  Vale's eyes moved to her.
  “And you are?”
  “Sela.”
  He knew the name. She saw it before he hid it.
  “My mother has asked about the boy. I am asking about the woman who came for help.”
  “Nadi has a contract.”
  “Does it say you may shut a door on her hand?”
  “I will not discuss an allegation through an unsecured channel.”
  Nadi laughed once, without pleasure.
  “It is your channel.”
  Vale ignored her.
  He offered clean transport, medical supplies, and an adviser. He would send a freight team within the next orchard day. Until then nobody should interfere with the tanks or circulate unverified claims.
  “What if the water tests show harm?” Irena asked.
  “Then we will act on the evidence.”
  “Will you accept a copy?”
  “Submit it through the proper review.”
  Sela understood then. The voice was not refusing proof. It was choosing where proof would have to go before it could matter.
  She took out the packing card with Brin's name.
  “We have begun another record,” she said.
  Vale looked at it as though she had shown him a child's toy.
  “Paper is not a secure scientific medium.”
  “No,” Sela said. “Anyone can read it if we teach them.”
  Corin made a sound that might have been a cough. Daren looked at the floor.
  Vale ended the call soon after.

  Tirzah remained seated. Japheth put a hand on her shoulder.
  “He knew you,” Sela said.
  “He knows records of me.”
  “He knew you had met.”
  “Then he knows he was asked to leave a house once before.”
  Daren asked to speak privately with Corin. Irena said nothing medical would leave the clinic without a copy kept there. Nadi returned to Lio. The room emptied in uneven steps until only the family remained.
  Eshar pulled a chair beside Sela.
  “You were brave.”
  “I was angry.”
  “Both can happen.”
  “I wanted him to feel ashamed.”
  “That may take longer than we have.”
  She leaned against him. The cloth of his sleeve smelled of the mountain and metal dust. It was the nearest thing to home in the room.
  Japheth spoke to Tirzah softly. Sela caught only a few words: ship, water, last door. Her mother shook her head.
  At last she turned to them.
  “We must be careful about what we think knowing means. I have seen him leave defeated. He has seen me before I was born in his reckoning. Neither of those things tells us what he will do here.”
  Sela looked at the blank wall.
  “Does anything we do matter if the records already exist?”
  Japheth picked up the cup from the desk.
  “Brin was thirsty. Now he has had water.”
  “That is not the whole question.”
  “No. It is the part I can answer.”
  Daren found Sela alone by the blank wall and asked whether her mother had really met Vale aboard the ark.
  “She told you.”
  “I know what she said.”
  “Then why ask me?”
  He rubbed his forehead.
  “Because I do not know what to do with the years.”
  “Neither do I.”
  He seemed relieved, then disappointed that relief did not solve anything.
  “If he already knows what happens, he has an advantage.”
  “Perhaps he knows a record. Perhaps the record leaves out the choice he needs to make now.”
  Sela was thinking aloud. She did not want him to mistake it for wisdom.
  “My mother asked not to know the name of the child she would have. They told her anyway. It did not teach her how to raise me. She still had to listen when I cried.”
  Daren looked at the empty wall.
  “I have a son at the northern settlement.”
  “Does he write?”
  “Not often.”
  “Do you?”
  He smiled without pleasure.
  “I send money.”
  Sela did not answer. She had no wish to become the sort of person who could diagnose a man's whole life from one sentence.
  After a while he said, “That was not a request for advice.”
  “I was hoping it was not.”
  He laughed then, briefly and honestly.
  She picked up the water cup from the desk. He moved aside to let her pass, then asked where the clinic wanted the reserve list delivered.

  Tirzah stood under the bell.
  “Did you look at the sky when you came here before?” Sela asked.
  “Yes.”
  “Were you frightened?”
  “I was carrying a child who had more reason to be frightened than I did.”
  “Anet.”
  Her mother nodded.
  “Is she here now?”
  “No one we have asked knows her. I do not want people searching the program's lists because of us. A name can bring danger as easily as help.”

  She had nearly asked Corin to search. The desire had seemed kind. Now she saw how little she knew about where a search might be seen.
  “Then we help the people in front of us.”
  “And we keep our eyes open for the others.”

  “Never use one clock for both sides,” he said.
  Sela put the mountain card beside the food pack.
  Outside the shed, machines moved through the beds all night, keeping the orchard alive in ways nobody on the mountain could have named.

Chapter 11
The Garden That Had Soil
Asha woke them before the orchard lamps brightened.
  “If you mean to see the roots, come before they run the mist.”
  Tirzah was up at once. Japheth took longer, rubbing his knee beside the heater. Sela offered him the salve. He took it without a joke, which told her more than a complaint would have.
  They walked through Asha's garden in the gray hour before the lamps rose. Here the ground was soft. Asha asked them to stay on the narrow boards between beds. When Sela stepped off one, she sank to the ankle and nearly lost a shoe.
  “Good soil,” Japheth said.
  “Bad path,” Sela answered, pulling free.
  Asha showed them bean roots washed clean in a basin. Small pale lumps clung to them. Beside them lay roots from the orchard, long and white where healthy, brown where they had begun to fail.
  “I am not asking you to name a new poison,” she told Tirzah. “I want you to tell me what you would ask if these were your trees.”
  Tirzah knelt slowly.
  “What changed first?”
  Asha brought a notebook. Unlike the program records, it was thick with loose sheets, pressed leaves, and stains. She had marked every planting for years. The orchard changed its feed in the last season. The south trees grew fast. The fruit swelled. Then the smallest roots darkened.
  “The director said fast growth meant health.”
  “Sometimes it means a thing is spending what it cannot replace,” Tirzah said.
  She dug a little soil from Asha's bed and held it near her nose. She broke it apart with a thumb. Tiny threads stretched between dark crumbs. A worm curled away from the light.

  “The bent tree had a worm.”
  “He used to help me,” Asha said. “He liked the ones with rings.”
  “Did he get sick here?”
  “No. He came less often after the south wall was closed.”
  Asha walked them to the water barrels. Each had a mark showing its source. She used roof condensation, stored water, and a little from the north line. None from the south return since the first leaf damage.
  “Why did you stop?” Eshar asked.
  “The smell changed.”
  “Did you tell anyone?”
  “I sent seven notices.”
  She showed him the replies. They thanked her for protecting the heritage collection and advised her not to disturb commercial procedures.

  “Can you grow enough here to feed the orchard?”
  “No.”
  “How many?”
  “A few households, if we use every bed and accept little variety. More if the empty nursery is released to us. But the whole settlement? Not in a season. Not without stores, workers, water, and years.”

  “Then we begin with the beds that can feed someone.”
  Asha looked at her carefully.
  “You do not mind that it is small?”
  “My first garden after the waters was smaller than this shed.”
  They spent the morning counting space.
  Eshar measured the empty nursery beyond the east wall. Its benches had been built for delicate young trees, but many stood bare. Asha thought tubs could fit beneath them if the drainage was repaired. Japheth checked the shade. Sela marked the plan in pictures because not everyone who might work there could read the same words.
  Nadi arrived with a group of orchard workers. Two were suspicious, one curious, and one openly angry.
  “We have been told to work harder for less fruit for years,” the angry man said. His name was Ben. “Now we are told to grow beans in a museum.”
  “No,” Asha said. “You are being asked whether you want to help choose what grows in the empty nursery.”
  “Who gets it?”
  “The people who need it.”
  “Who decides that?”
  The question stopped them.
  Sela looked at the plan. She had drawn beds, pipes, storage, and paths. She had not drawn a place where people could argue about the food.
  “Everyone who works and everyone who eats should know the count,” she said. “But those are not always the same people. Lio cannot work.”
  Ben folded his arms.
  “My mother cannot either.”
  “Then she belongs in the count.”
  “And if there is not enough?”
  “Then we say so before someone hides it.”
  He did not look satisfied. He stayed.

  “Before what?”
  He looked embarrassed.
  “The things in the story.”
  “Some of them. After others.”

  “Were people kinder then?”

Chapter 12
What the Workers Would Risk
The meeting began with a locked door.
  Ben had invited twelve workers. Twenty-three came. They filled Asha's shed, stood in the garden, and leaned against the wall outside. Somebody brought a pot of tea. Somebody else brought a child asleep on one shoulder.
  Daren sent a notice saying the gathering had no authority to alter production.
  Asha read it aloud.
  “Then we shall start by talking,” she said.
  That drew a few smiles. It did not remove the fear.
  Nadi spoke first. She described the samples, Irena's findings, and the difference between a concern and a proven cause. She did not say the new feed had caused every illness. She did say the south line would remain closed unless someone ordered the clinic to accept water Irena had rejected.
  A woman asked whether that meant no wages if the harvest failed.
  A man asked whether his housing belonged to the program.
  Another asked whether the people from the old stories would leave before the bills came due.
  Sela felt the question strike her. She wanted to answer at once. Tirzah touched her sleeve.
  “Let him finish.”
  The man was called Hal. He had three children, a wife whose lungs had never suited Haven's dust, and a debt for their passage from another settlement. He could not afford to become a lesson in courage.
  Sela listened until he stopped.
  “We will leave,” she said. “We have to. Our door has only days left. That means we should not promise work that depends on us staying.”
  Hal looked at her, surprised by the answer.
  “What can you promise?”
  “A record of what we saw. Hands while we are here. Nothing that belongs to you.”
  He lowered his eyes to his cup.
  It was not enough. It was at least true.
  Eshar laid the nursery plan on the table. He showed the repairs it needed and asked which could be done without shutting the commercial pumps. Three workers corrected him. One knew the drain slope. One knew the spare parts had been moved. The third knew the nursery door had been sealed after a dispute over storage space.
  “Who has the key?” Asha asked.
  “Sen.”
  The name moved through the room like a draft.
  Ben wanted to cut the lock. Another worker said that would give the program grounds to evict them. Nadi argued for a council petition. Hal said petitions took long enough for a child to grow up waiting.
  The argument rose. People began speaking over one another. Sela heard Babel in the way fear made each voice louder without making any more likely to be heard.
  She struck the side of the tea pot with a spoon.
  Everyone looked at her.
  She regretted the sound immediately. Then she used the silence.
  “I cannot follow three voices. Corin cannot translate three voices. If you want me to understand, one person at a time.”
  Asha took the spoon from her.
  “And nobody hits my pot again.”
  They found a slower order.
  The woman with the sleeping child spoke. Her name was Esa. She knew how to run the nursery's old water system because she had cleaned it before it was closed. She could make it work with parts already on site, if someone obtained access. She would not work after dark. She would not bring her child near unknown feed. She wanted those conditions written down.
  Sela wrote them.
  Then Hal spoke. If the council released the nursery, he would give two hours a day, but not from paid time unless his wages were protected. He would also help carry north water to the south houses. His wife could keep the count from home.
  Sela wrote that too.
  By the end of the meeting, nobody had sworn to save the settlement. Eleven people had offered specific work. Four had refused and stayed to listen. Two had asked for time to discuss it at home. Asha said all of that belonged in the record.
  Corin went to the council with Daren's reluctant signature on the water request. Nadi went with him. Sela wanted to follow but stayed to help clean the cups.
  Ben lingered in the doorway.
  “You did not tell us what the story says we do.”
  “I have not read that part.”
  “Why?”
  “Would you want someone choosing your words because he had seen them written somewhere?”
  He considered.
  “Depends whether they were good words.”
  “They might leave out the time you spent deciding.”
  He nodded slowly and picked up a stack of bowls.
  The worker who had said he would not help came back for his cup.
  Sela found it under a bench and handed it to him. He turned it between his palms instead of leaving.
  “My name is Parn,” he said.
  She repeated it.
  “I do not want you writing that everyone agreed.”
  “I wrote four people refused.”
  “Did you write why?”
  “Not yet.”
  He looked toward the garden gate.
  “My sister lost her place at another farm after she signed a petition. They called it a staffing change. She waited nine months for work. I had to send part of my pay every cycle.”
  Sela put down the cloth she had been using.
  “May I write that?”
  “Without her name.”
  “Yes.”
  He nodded. She fetched the page and added his reason while he watched. She did not ask him to change his answer.
  When she finished, he pointed to one line. Nadi translated it for him.
  “Do not say afraid,” he said.
  “What word do you want?”
  “Responsible.”
  Sela considered it. He had been responsible for helping his sister eat. He was also afraid. Both could be true, and one belonged more clearly to him.
  She changed the word.
  Parn took his cup and went to the gate. There he stopped.

Chapter 17
The Cost of Coming Back

  The mountain air touched Japheth's face. His eyes closed.
  “Better?” Tirzah asked.
  “It smells like rain.”
  There was no rain yet. Clouds moved above the ridge.

  Tirzah opened the seed box. She took out the little wooden bird and put it in Sela's hand.
  “I was carrying it for myself. You may need it more.”
  “You are coming back?”
  “I am taking your father home.”
  “That is not what I asked.”
  “No.”
  Sela looked toward the bright opening in the wall. Behind it, Haven's lamps were just beginning another work period. Here the day was ending. Time seemed to have split her family into two kinds of light.
  Tirzah touched her face.
  “I do not know whether the road will allow another crossing before it closes. We will not make a promise the clocks cannot keep.”
  Sela nodded, angry with the clocks, with the mountain, with her own wish to be reassured by something false.
  Japheth called her over.
  He took the pencil from his pocket. Tirzah had given it to him for safekeeping on the journey. He held it as if weighing a small tool.

  She laughed despite herself.
  “What else?”

  She kissed his forehead. His skin was warm and dry. For a moment she was a little girl again and wanted him to lift her onto his shoulders, though she had long since grown too large.

  Sela watched them leave the ruined house.
  At the first bend, her mother turned. She lifted one hand. Sela lifted hers.
  Then the cedar hid them.

  “Which?”
  “Should.”
  He waited.

  “I can stay another day.”

  “Going home.”
  Asha nodded.
  “And you?”
  Sela set the wooden bird on the table.
  “Not yet.”

Chapter 18
A Shipment With Conditions
The revised supply schedule arrived before the first seeds went into the nursery beds.

Chapter 19
The Road Made by Ships
The first answer from another settlement was an offer to lend food against future labor.
  Den read it twice. Then she put it beside the program schedule.
  “They have changed the seal on the same cup.”
  The second answer was kinder and nearly useless. A small station had spare medicine but no ship due for three weeks. The third could send grain if Haven provided transport. The fourth asked how much fuel Haven could pay in advance.

  “The orchard dock is under program contract,” Corin said.

  “Council land.”
  “Then we go there.”

  “It is meant to move.”
  “That far?”

  “Does anyone know it is still there?”

  “I will ask.”
  Asha nodded.

  “Who asked the right question?”
  “Asha.”
  “Put her on the crew list.”
  “She is not coming.”
  “She still did work.”

  “You are ready.”
  “For this buckle.”
  “An excellent place to begin.”

  “Once. When my sister came from the northern station.”

  “Yes.”

Chapter 20
The Storm at the Cut
The dust reached them before the wind did.

  “Can we wait?” Nadi asked.
  “Not here,” Esa said. “The low ground fills.”

  “I cannot see the marker.”

  “Your lengths or hers?” Eshar asked.
  Corin corrected himself with a number Esa understood. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

  “No,” Corin said.

  “Can we reach from here?”
  “Maybe.”
  “Then we measure before we move.”

  “We will not lose you between two machines.”

  “We help from something that holds.”

  Nadi handed her the list of medicines.
  “Check the temperature case. Keep it inside its limit.”
  “I can carry a rope.”
  “So can Venn. You can keep Irena's culture alive while we do.”

  The line snapped taut.
  Sela saw Eshar drop to one knee. She put down the case and reached for the hatch.
  Nadi blocked it.
  “Look.”
  Corin had the second line tight. Eshar rose. Venn held the child against his chest and began moving back.
  Sela's heart hammered so hard that the rest of her seemed to shake around it.
  The woman came last. She had cut her hand on the window edge. Eshar followed her, one hand on the line, the other shielding his face.
  When the hatch closed behind them, the inside of the carrier filled with dust and breath and the child's thin crying.

  “Does it still open?” Esa asked.
  “I used it last season.”
  “Then we look from here before we drive.”
  Corin sent a small survey light along the route. Sela watched its image appear on the display: rocks, a wall, a low door. The ground was rough but sound. They moved slowly, following the light.
  The shelter held six bunks, a water tank, and a table bolted to the floor. Its air smelled stale. Its door sealed. That was enough.

  He had seen the storm report before leaving the field. It had seemed far enough away. He knew the cut. He had taken the road many times. Each fact became another reason he should have made a different choice.
  Senn listened until the child began to listen too.
  Then she put a hand on his arm.
  “We are here. Tell me what we do next.”
  He looked at her.
  “The road may stay closed.”
  “Then we wait somewhere sound.”
  “We may lose the carrier.”
  “Then we will need help replacing it.”
  He began another apology. She shook her head, tired rather than angry.
  “Later, if you need. Now check the water with Esa.”
  He went.
  Sela sat beside Senn and helped loosen the child's dusty coat. The woman's cut hand had stiffened. She let Nadi clean it without watching.

  Sela looked at the child, who was picking a grain of dust from the blanket with great concentration.
  “Both happened.”

  Sela gave her the folded letter. Her mother read slowly. At the line about not reading the end of her life, she stopped and put a hand over her mouth.
  Neither spoke for a while.
  Then Tirzah said, “The stool was in the wrong place.”
  Sela laughed through tears.
  “Of course it was.”
  The first work Sela did at home was to mend a cracked water jar.
  Iva had set it aside for practice. The break was old and ordinary. Sela sat in the pottery yard, fitted the edges, and found herself listening for a chime that did not belong there.
  Ruma came to watch.
  “You are holding it too tightly.”
  Sela loosened her hands.
  “I know.”
  “Knowing does not always reach the fingers.”
  They worked in silence for a while. Beyond the wall a neighbor was singing in the tongue he had brought from Babel. Sela had never learned all the words. She found she understood the song better now, or perhaps she was willing to enjoy what she did not understand.
  Ruma examined the repair.
  “It may not hold water.”
  “It can hold seeds.”
  “It can hold tools. It can hold a person's foolish notion that every crack must be made invisible.”
  Sela looked at her.
  Ruma shrugged.
  “I am old. Occasionally I am allowed a sentence.”
  Sela laughed and set the jar in the shade to dry.
  She had thought coming home would fit like returning a lid to a box. Instead the village had stayed itself while she had acquired new edges. A cup reminded her of Lio. A gate reminded her of Soren. A damp cloth recalled the medicine case. None of those memories made the place less home. They made it crowded in a different way.
  When Iva came for the repaired jar, Sela showed her the seam rather than turning it toward the wall.
  “Use it where you can see whether it fails.”
  The girl nodded and placed it on the open shelf.
  Over the following weeks they wrote the account properly. Sela used the remaining modern paper for the parts most likely to be lost in copying: names, clock rules, the difference between a closed route and an expired one, and the facts they did not know. The rest went onto prepared boards and clay tablets, with duplicate passages carried by trusted travelers.
  She began one account of her mother's earlier life with the sentence Corin had read beneath the red sky.
  My mother kept a seed jar beside her bedding.
  She knew now why she had chosen it. The jar had always been there. The words belonged to an observation, not to a command sent backward through time.
  She wrote the story of Babel separately from the Haven visit. She marked where Tirzah's account of Elian belonged: during the Flood, before Sela's birth. The mountain passage came after Babel. It had been another installation, with another endpoint. Closing one had not reopened the other.
  Japheth checked the ship details. Eshar checked the repairs. Tirzah changed a line that made her sound certain when she had only been afraid and trying to act.
  Ruma read what Sela had written about the cup.
  “You may say it was sound,” she said. “Do not say it was beautiful unless you have seen the second firing.”
  Sela left beautiful in the draft and waited to see whether she would notice.
  The little packet from Asha grew in a separate bed. Three seeds failed. Two came up weakly. One put out a strong pair of leaves. Sela recorded all six.
  One evening Tirzah sat beside Japheth under the tree. He held the empty seed tray Brin had made, turning it over to admire the brace.
  “He has learned something,” he said.
  “So have we.”
  Tirzah leaned against him. He shifted so her shoulder rested comfortably, despite the stiffness in his own back.
  Across the garden, Sela watched them and did not write at once.
  Some parts of a life needed to be lived before they were carried anywhere.
Chapter 30
A Place for the Next Seed
In Haven's year 2189, Tomas found Sela's longer account in a store the program did not control.
  He had known the short version since childhood: the jar, the Flood, the first tree, the bell. Everyone at the orchard knew it. School groups stood under the name plate and repeated a sentence about careful stewardship that nobody remembered hearing from an actual gardener.
  The longer account had no such sentence.
  It had a broken floor, a boy's shoe, a storm shelter, a cup, and a list of fourteen names. Some of those people lived within walking distance of the nursery. Tomas had seen Oda at work with Asha and never asked where he came from.
  He sat down on a low wall and read until the lamps dimmed.
  By then another door had opened aboard the ark in the far ancient interval. Elian had found Tirzah, young and holding a bowl. The rescue through those doors had brought Anet and her family into Haven, and the ark routes had closed for good.
  The old mountain anchor remained dead. No one had reached back through Sela's morning to undo the farewell. The two visits belonged to different points in the family's lives and different intervals in Haven's records. People had spent months arguing about that before Jessa drew two plain lines on a public wall and wrote the dates beside them.
  Sela's visit had not made the settlement independent.
  The nursery fed a few households and the clinic kitchen. It taught workers how to grow crops without the proprietary feed. Its records helped expose the reclaim fault. Its success had been used in proposals, postponed in budgets, and defended at meetings whose minutes were almost as long as the first account.
  When the ark routes closed, Haven still faced shortages. The old independent farming plan, proposed years before Sela arrived, had finally been funded at a scale large enough to matter. It would take years. Some families moved to neighboring settlements. Medical stocks remained difficult. Nobody who had carried north water was allowed to describe the transition as painless.
  But there were beds already prepared.
  There were workers who knew the difference between a promise and a growing leaf. There were seed drawers outside the program archive. There were people who had practiced asking who would sleep where when a rescue ended.
  Tomas took the account to Anet's mother.
  She sat beneath the Founding Garden tree with Anet beside her. The child's hand rested in the soil near a small new seedling. Tirzah had given Tomas that seed during the ark crossing, before her own future daughter had learned to write.
  Anet looked up at the pages.
  “Is that about the door?”
  “Some of it.”
  “Does it say we came?”
  “Not this copy. It was written before you arrived here.”
  Anet considered that.
  “Then someone should add it.”
  Her mother looked at Tomas.
  “Only if she wants to tell it.”
  Anet nodded solemnly, as if the right to choose her own page were a tool she had been given and must learn to use.
  Tomas read a little aloud. The part about the cup. The part about Lio learning the word for rain. The part where Sela admitted she wanted to run out into the storm and had instead kept a medicine case safe.
  Anet listened without moving her hand from the soil.
  Later Nadi came with a basket of greens. She looked older than Tomas remembered, though only a year had passed since the mountain journey. She put the basket down and saw the account.
  “Does he read it too grandly?” she asked Anet.
  The child looked uncertain whether it was safe to laugh.
  Nadi grinned at Tomas.
  “He does. I can tell.”
  Anet laughed.
  Across the nursery, Brin was teaching two new workers to check the water marks before opening a valve. He walked with a slight unevenness. Some feeling had returned to his foot. Not all. He did not let anyone call that a small price for a useful lesson.
  Kesh and the children's aunt had chosen rooms near the east garden. The search for the missing father continued. Once, a message had come from a receiving town with a possible name. It had not yet been confirmed. The children knew what was known and what was not. Nobody promised a reunion merely to make bedtime easier.
  Daren worked under another director now, responsible for water maintenance. He attended the review hearings and answered questions that made him look worse than silence would have. Sen's case was still open. Pell had appealed his suspension. Vale's broader program had become the subject of investigations in places Haven could not see.
  Elian's evidence from 2026 joined records that had taken other paths through years. Not all copies survived. Not all witnesses agreed. The inquiry did not become simple because a family had crossed a door.
  Tomas had once thought history meant a chain of people who knew what they were doing.
  Now he thought of Sela sitting on a clinic floor, learning a child's word for water.
  Before the evening meal, Anet asked Nadi whether the woman from the jar story was the same woman who had brought her through the wooden ship.
  “Yes,” Nadi said. “At different times in her life.”
  “Was she older when you saw her?”
  “Much older.”
  “Did she still have the bowl?”
  Nadi smiled.
  “I do not remember a bowl. She had a cup someone had made for her.”
  Anet looked toward Lio, who was carrying that cup with both hands. Its handle had been repaired after a fall. The seam showed clearly beneath the glaze.
  “Can I ask her something?”
  Nadi's smile changed.
  “Not through a door. Those ways are closed.”
  “Could I write it?”
  “Yes.”
  “Would she get it?”
  “I do not know a way to send it to her. But you could write it for someone who reads her story.”
  Anet thought about that. She had been given numbers before she was given much practice choosing words. A question that did not have to be answered at once was a strange freedom.
  Tomas brought a sheet. He sat far enough away that she did not have to guard what she wrote.
  She made three marks, stopped, and asked for help spelling garden.
  He showed her on another sheet so she could decide whether to copy it.
  When she finished, she gave Nadi permission to read.
  Did you know I would like the tree?
  Nadi sat with the page on her knees. She could not answer for Tirzah. She could remember the older woman's hand in cold spring water, her delight in a bird no one had put beneath a roof.
  “I think she hoped you would have somewhere you could like things,” she said.
  Anet took the page back. Beneath the question she drew the tree. It leaned a little, as the real one did. She added a low branch where she might climb when her mother said she was ready.
  Tomas put no date on the page until she asked him to. Then he wrote it in the corner and told her what it meant.
  It was a day in her own life. Nobody else's record had chosen the question for her.
  That evening, workers gathered around the nursery table. It had been lengthened twice. One support was made from a carrier brace Eshar had measured. Nobody had polished it to match.
  Asha set down a bowl of beans.
  “Count the seed before you eat the rest.”
  Lio, taller now and impatient with being treated as fragile, took the counting tray. He still tired before other children did. Meret watched him, then deliberately turned back to the bread so he would have room to finish without being measured by her fear.
  Anet sat beside him.
  He showed her how to separate seed from supper. She corrected one of his counts. He checked, found she was right, and pretended he had meant to test her.
  Above the gate, the replica bell sounded as another family arrived.
  Far behind them in years, rain passed over Sela's village.
  She sat at the long table with her pages weighted against the draft. Tirzah was mending a cuff. Japheth slept in his chair, one hand open on the arm. Eshar stood at the doorway watching water fall from the eaves.
  Sela put down the pencil.
  She had reached the place where she could no longer tell what had happened to Haven. She could imagine it, but she would not write imagining as news.
  Instead she wrote the last thing she knew.
  We left them with living plants, clean water for the present, stores that would not last forever, and work they had chosen together. We did not leave them safe from every loss. We left them with more ways to help one another when loss came.

  Sela smiled and added it.
  Then she set the page aside and went to the doorway. Eshar moved his arm so she could stand beneath it. Rain darkened the garden beds. Beyond the wall, someone called for help with a cart wheel.
  He looked at her.
  She reached for the dry cloak.
  Together they went out.
